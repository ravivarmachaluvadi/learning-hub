Lab 55 · Work That Spans Services

Kafka Exactly-Once — nothing is ever undone, and the abort is a note in the margin

Tell a friend: Meera works at Rupaya, which settles card payments for small shops. Her service reads three payments, writes two ledger lines and two payout rows, and then writes down how far it got. That is five separate acts, and a crash between any two of them either pays a shop twice or never pays it at all. Kafka's answer is strange. It writes everything down immediately, at real places in the log, before anybody has decided anything. Only afterwards does it append a slip saying "that lot counts" or "that lot does not." Nothing is ever taken back. Priya, who moves the money, is simply told to stop reading at the first line whose slip has not arrived yet. So the price of the whole trick is paid by the reader, in waiting.

1. Why does one small job turn into three separate acts?

In one breath: A stream processor reads a fact, works something out, writes the answer, and then writes down how far it got. Those are separate acts. The machine can die between any two of them, and there is no order that saves you.

Meera runs the settlement service at Rupaya, a company that settles card payments for small shops. Her service does the same three things forever, and so does every stream processor you will ever write:

1. read a record from a topic
2. compute something and write the result somewhere
3. record that the input has been handled

Each of the three is durable on its own. Kafka can guarantee that a write lands and stays landed. You learned exactly how in the kafka lab. Wait until every in-sync copy holds the record (acks=all). Refuse the write unless at least two copies are in sync (min.insync.replicas=2). And keep three copies in the first place, which is replication factor 3.

But durable-on-its-own is not the property Meera needs. The three are three separate physical acts. A process can die in either gap between them. There are only two orders you can put them in, and both are wrong:

write output, then record progress     crash in the gap → the input is read again
                                        → the output is written twice
record progress, then write output     crash in the gap → the input is never re-read
                                        → the output is never written

This is not a Kafka problem. It is the same shape as the dual-write problem from transactional-outbox, one hop further downstream. It is exactly the reason that lab told you consumers must be idempotent.

What is new here is that Kafka can, for one specific and narrow case, genuinely make the three acts atomic. Not by fusing them into one act. By making all three invisible until they are all done.

That trick, its exact boundary, and the four years of bugs it took to get right, are the whole lab.

2. What actually breaks in Rupaya's settlement run?

In one breath: Meera's service turns three card payments into five records scattered over three machines. It needs all five to be true together. A crash in the middle does not produce an error. It produces a tidy set of books that agree with each other and are wrong.

Rupaya processes card payments for small merchants. Every captured payment lands on a topic called captures. A service called settlement, which is Meera's service, reads captures, takes Rupaya's 2% fee, and writes two things.

The first is a pair of double-entry lines to ledger-entries. Arjun runs reconciliation and balances the books against those. The second is a payout row to merchant-payouts. Priya runs the bank transfer job, which reads that topic and actually moves money into a shop's account.

Hold on to those three people. Meera writes, Arjun reconciles, Priya pays. Every failure in this lab is one of them getting something they should not have got.

Here is one batch. The service is consumer group settlement, reading captures partition 3, currently positioned at offset 5000.

captures-3 offset merchant amount
5000 TeaHouse ₹1,200
5001 TeaHouse ₹800
5002 BookBarn ₹2,500

The service polls those three, computes, and produces:

destination what
ledger-entries-1 three pairs of ledger lines, offsets 810, 811, 812
merchant-payouts-0 TeaHouse ₹1,960 (offset 44), BookBarn ₹2,450 (offset 45)

Then it commits offset 5003 for group settlement. Physically, that is a record appended to __consumer_offsets-12, Kafka's own internal topic for "how far has each group got". A committed offset is a record in a log, like everything else. Remember that. §5 turns it into the whole design.

So: five appends across three partitions, which live on three different brokers. Meera now needs all five to be true together, or none of them.

🔮 Predict. The service crashes after writing the two payout rows but before committing offset 5003. It restarts. What does Priya's bank transfer job pay TeaHouse, and what does Arjun's ledger say? Commit to an answer before reading on.

Answer

On restart the group's committed offset is still 5000. The service reprocesses captures 5000–5002 and writes everything again: ledger offsets 813–815, payout offsets 46–47.

Priya's job reads merchant-payouts and has no idea any of this happened. It now sees TeaHouse ₹1,960 twice. It pays ₹3,920 against ₹2,000 of captures.

Arjun's ledger is doubled too. So reconciliation does not even flag it. The books balance perfectly against a payout that is twice too large.

If you guessed "reconciliation catches it", that is exactly the guess this lab exists to break. Doubling both sides keeps them equal.

That last part is why this matters. The failure did not produce an error. It produced a consistent, self-agreeing, wrong set of records.

3. Can you fix it by choosing a different order?

In one breath: There are only two orders. One pays a shop twice. The other never pays it and never tells anyone. You do not get to avoid the choice. You only get to choose which failure you can see.

The obvious response is that Meera put the acts in the wrong order. So try the other one. Commit offset 5003 first, then write the outputs.

🔮 Predict. With the order flipped, the service crashes in the gap. What does TeaHouse receive, and what would you have to run to discover that anything went wrong?

Answer

TeaHouse receives nothing, ever. And nothing anywhere records that a payment was skipped.

The captures at 5000–5002 are now behind a committed offset. No consumer will ever be handed them again. No scan will find them, because nothing marks them as unhandled. The only marker was the offset, and the offset says "done".

If you guessed that a reconciliation job would catch it, look at what Arjun actually compares. He balances ledger lines against payouts. Here neither exists. The books balance at zero, and zero is a perfectly consistent answer.

This is the forced choice from the kafka lab, stated once more because it is the floor everything else is built on.

commit offsets you get you see
before processing at-most-once silent gaps
after processing at-least-once duplicates

There is no third ordering. Two things go in a sequence, and you choose which one is exposed.

So every real system chooses at-least-once. Duplicates are detectable and repairable; silent gaps are neither. Then the system spends its entire engineering budget on making the duplicates harmless.

4. Why not just make it idempotent?

In one breath: Usually you should. Give each unit of work a name, claim the name atomically with the effect, and a replay does nothing. This section is about the three situations where that answer is not available at all.

You already know the real answer to duplicates. It is the idempotency lab. Give each unit of work a key, make claiming the key atomic with the effect, and a replayed duplicate becomes a no-op.

Apply it to Priya's job and it works. She can key on the id of the capture plus the merchant. Engineers write that pair as (capture_id, merchant). She keeps a table of capture ids she has already paid, and inserts into it under a unique constraint. TeaHouse gets paid once, no matter how many times Meera's service writes the row.

That is a completely legitimate design, and a great many production pipelines are built exactly this way. The idempotent-consumer lab is that mechanism in full: identity, atomicity and memory, and the three ways each of them fails on its own. Nothing here replaces it.

If you can do it, do it. It is simpler than everything that follows, and it survives failures that transactions do not.

So why is there anything else? Three specific situations where it stops being buildable. Rupaya hits all three.

4(a) What if the duplicate lands where nothing has a name?

Priya's case is easy because a payout row is about one capture. There is a natural key sitting right there in the data.

Now add a stage that computes each merchant's rolling hourly total. Its output is one row: (TeaHouse, 14:00–15:00, ₹18,400).

If the upstream stage duplicated one capture, that number is wrong by ₹1,200. And there is no key on the aggregate that would let a downstream reader notice, because a sum has no identity. You cannot ask "have I already seen this ₹18,400?" The question is meaningless.

Deduplication has to happen before the aggregation, or not at all. That is the sentence to carry out of this section.

4(b) What if the dedupe store is itself a second system?

The idempotency lab's sharpest failure was keys in Redis and effect in Postgres. That is a dual write, reintroduced at the exact point that was supposed to solve it.

Now look at a stage in Meera's pipeline whose only output is another Kafka topic. The effect is an append to a Kafka log. There is no database to put the key table in.

You would have to add one, purely to defend a pipeline that otherwise needs no state at all. You would be buying a second system to protect yourself from the failure mode of having two systems.

4(c) What if you have to pay for it six times over?

Rupaya's pipeline is six stages long. Idempotency is a per-hop contract, which is the idempotency lab's phrase. So six stages means six key stores.

Six retention policies. Six sets of dashboards. Six things to get wrong.

And the keys have to survive every transformation in between, which §4(a) has just said they often cannot.

Say it back. Without using the words transaction, atomic or offset, tell a friend why Meera cannot solve her problem the way Priya solved hers.

A good answer sounds like "Priya pays one shop for one payment, so every job she does has a name, and she can keep a list of the names she has already handled. Meera's middle stages produce totals. A total has no name — you cannot look at ₹18,400 and ask whether you have seen it before. And her pipeline has six of those stages, so she would need six lists, each one a whole extra database to run and to get wrong."

Checkpoint. You can now say what problem exactly-once is for. It is not "duplicates are bad" — you already had an answer for that. It is "for a Kafka-to-Kafka stage, the answer you had costs more than the stage does, and for an aggregating stage it is not available at all." Next: what would actually have to be true to fix it at the source.

5. What exactly has to be true for Meera's batch?

In one breath: State the requirement precisely and the design falls out of it. Meera does not need the five records to exist together. She needs them to become visible together. Those are different demands, and one of them is much cheaper.

Go back to the settlement batch and write down what must hold.

Five records were appended across three partitions:

ledger-entries-1     offsets 810, 811, 812     the work
merchant-payouts-0   offsets 44, 45            the work
__consumer_offsets-12  one record: group settlement, captures-3 → 5003
                                               the progress marker

The requirement is this one sentence:

Either every consumer of these topics eventually sees all five, or no consumer ever sees any of them.

Two things in that sentence deserve attention, because most of the design falls out of them.

5.1 Why does "sees" matter more than "exists"?

It says every consumer sees. It does not say every record exists.

The requirement is about visibility, not about presence. Arjun does not care whether a byte is on a disk in a data centre. He cares whether his reconciliation job is handed it.

That distinction is the crack the entire design goes through, and §6 walks through the crack.

5.2 Why is the progress marker treated as one of the outputs?

Look at the third line of the list again. The bookkeeping record that says "input handled" is in the set, alongside the real work, subject to the same all-or-nothing rule.

That is the move that converts a three-action problem into a one-action problem.

And it works only because of something you learned two labs ago: in Kafka a committed offset is a record in a topic. So it can be swept into the same mechanism as the payout rows and the ledger lines.

In a broker that keeps per-consumer acknowledgement state in a database, you could not do this at all. The progress marker would live somewhere the mechanism cannot reach, and you would be back to a dual write.

Everything in §10 is machinery to deliver that one sentence.

6. If you cannot make the writes atomic, what can you make atomic?

In one breath: Kafka gives up on making the five writes happen together. Instead it writes them immediately, wherever they really go, and publishes a decision afterwards. Readers who care about the decision refuse to read past the point where one is still missing.

Meera's five records live on three partitions on three brokers. To make five appends atomic you would need all three brokers to agree, in one indivisible step, to accept or reject.

That needs a distributed commit protocol. The two-phase-commit lab spent its whole length on why that is expensive and how it blocks.

Kafka does something else. It is worth stating in one sentence before any detail:

The records are written immediately, unconditionally, to their real partitions at their real offsets. Afterwards, a decision is published. Readers who care about the decision refuse to read past the point where a decision is still missing.

Sit with the consequences, because they are the whole lab.

6.1 What does a "rollback" actually remove?

Nothing. That is not a simplification; it is literally nothing.

Records from an aborted transaction are in the log, at permanent offsets, replicated to every follower, consuming disk and retention, until retention finally deletes them. Nothing is removed. Nothing is rewritten.

6.2 Where does the decision live?

The abort is an append. The decision is itself a record, appended after the data, in each affected partition. Engineers call it a control record.

That is the second surprise. The thing that cancels the work sits after the work, in the same log, at the next offset.

6.3 Who does the filtering?

Not the broker. Filtering is the reader's job.

The broker hands the aborted records to the consumer over the network. The client library drops them before your application sees them.

Which means a client that does not ask for filtering gets everything. Hold that thought for ten lines.

6.4 What does "undecided" cost?

It is a real state with a real cost. A reader who wants committed-only data, and who reaches a record whose transaction is still open, has exactly one option: wait.

That wait is the price of the whole design. §11 is largely about who ends up paying it.

The simple version to carry around, and the sentence this lab is named after: Kafka does not have rollback. It has retraction, published after the fact, which the reader is responsible for honouring.

🔮 Predict. Given only that paragraph: a transaction writes three records to ledger-entries-1 at offsets 810–812 and then aborts. A consumer with default settings, one nobody has configured for transactions, polls that partition. What does its application code receive?

Answer

All three records, exactly as if they had been committed.

The consumer setting that decides this is called isolation.level, and it defaults to read_uncommitted. The 4.3 consumer documentation is explicit about what that means: "If set to read_uncommitted (the default), consumer.poll() will return all messages, even transactional messages which have been aborted."

If you guessed "nothing, because the transaction aborted", you have assumed the broker does the filtering. It does not. §6.3 said the client does, and a client that never asked never filters.

This is the single most common way an EOS deployment does nothing at all. A team turns on transactions in the producer, verifies the producer side, ships it — and every downstream consumer keeps reading aborted records, because nobody changed a consumer config. §11.6 covers the symptom.

Say it back. You have finished the beginner layer. Without using the word transaction, explain to a friend how Kafka manages to make five writes look like one.

A good answer sounds like "It does not make them happen together. It writes them all straight away, in the real places, and then afterwards it writes one more little note saying whether that lot counts. Anyone who wants to be careful has to stop reading at the first line whose note has not turned up yet. So the writing was never atomic — only the looking was."

7. Which six words do you now need, and what did each one cost?

In one breath: You have felt the need for all six of these. Now they get their names. Minor terms are defined where they first appear and collected in the glossary at §16.

Six terms carry the rest of the lab. Read each little story first; the comparison table at the end of the section is the map back.

7.1 What is the name of a job, rather than of a process?

Meera runs one settlement instance per input partition. The instance handling captures-3 is not special as a process — it gets restarted, redeployed, moved to another node, killed by the autoscaler. What must never happen twice is the work: settling captures-3.

So the thing Kafka needs a name for is the job, not the machine doing it.

That name is a string you configure on the producer, and it is called the transactional.id. Meera sets hers with transactional.id = "settlement-" + partition, so the instance for partition 3 carries transactional.id = "settlement-3". Pod restarts, redeploys and node failures all keep the same string.

The broker uses that string as the identity that gets fenced. When a producer with transactional.id = "settlement-3" starts up, any earlier producer using that same string is immediately invalidated, and any transaction it left open is aborted.

Why does the distinction matter so much? Because if two machines are both processing captures-3, Rupaya has a problem regardless of what the two processes are called. So the identity has to be attached to the work assignment, and it has to survive restarts.

A producer that generates a fresh id at startup fences nothing. A UUID, a hostname with a random suffix, a Kubernetes pod name — with any of those, the new instance's id does not match the old one's, so there is nobody to fence. §11.3 traces exactly what breaks.

This is distributed-locking's lease name and split-brain's identity-being-fenced, wearing new clothes. The transactional.id is who gets fenced. The next term is how.

7.2 How does the system know which holder of the name is current?

By a version number for "the current owner of this transactional.id."

In words: every time somebody new claims the name settlement-3, a counter goes up, and everything that instance writes carries the counter. Engineers call the counter the producer epoch.

Precisely: it is a monotonically increasing integer that the transaction coordinator attaches to a transactional.id. Every write carries it. A write arriving with an epoch lower than the coordinator's current one is rejected with ProducerFenced, or with InvalidProducerEpoch.

Why bother? Because "start a new instance" and "the old instance is definitely dead" are different statements, and only the first one is observable. You cannot detect death. You can only make death irrelevant, by ensuring the presumed-dead process can no longer act.

If you have read split-brain, this is the eighth costume of the same object: term, epoch, generation, fencing token, lease version, leader epoch, ProducerEpoch. Note carefully that this is a different epoch from Kafka's leader epoch, which fences stale replica leaders. Same idea, different subject.

7.3 Who keeps the minutes?

Somebody has to remember that a run is open, which partitions it touched, and how it ended. In the story that somebody is a duty supervisor. In Kafka it is a broker, and the role is called the transaction coordinator.

Precisely: each transactional.id hashes onto one partition of the internal topic __transaction_state, and the leader of that partition is that id's coordinator.

The coordinator owns the transaction's state machine. And here is the part that matters in §10.4: it records every state change as a record in that partition, which is an ordinary replicated Kafka log. Not a variable in memory. A log entry.

This is two-phase-commit's coordinator with one decisive difference, and §10.4 is entirely about that difference.

7.4 What does the decision physically look like?

It is a slip appended to a partition saying "everything from that producer, up to here, counts" — or "does not count".

Engineers call it a control record, or a transaction marker. Precisely: a record with a control bit set, containing a COMMIT or ABORT marker for a specific producer id. It is written by the broker, on the coordinator's instruction, into the ordinary log of each partition the transaction touched.

KIP-98 defines the encoding: "a control message type of 0 indicates a COMMIT marker, and a control message type of 1 indicates an ABORT marker."

Why does the decision live inside the partition at all? Because it has to be discoverable by a consumer that is reading one partition, on one broker, and talks to nobody else. Putting the decision in the partition means a reader needs no extra lookup, no coordinator round trip, and no distributed read.

The price is that markers occupy offsets. If Meera's transaction wrote 810, 811 and 812, the marker is at 813, and the next transaction's first record is at 814.

Her application never sees 813, because the client filters it out. So the offsets your code observes have gaps. Any logic that assumes consecutive offsets is broken by transactions, whether they commit or abort.

7.5 How far into a partition is it safe to read?

Up to the first record whose fate nobody has decided yet, and not one byte further. That boundary has a name: the last stable offset, usually written LSO.

Quoting the 4.3 consumer documentation: "in read_committed mode, consumer.poll() will only return messages up to the last stable offset (LSO), which is the one less than the offset of the first open transaction."

It is the pure expression of §6. Reading is allowed right up to the first record whose fate is unknown.

Make it concrete with Arjun's topic. ledger-entries-1 has a high watermark of 900, meaning 900 records are fully replicated. It also has an open transaction that started at 810. So its LSO is 810.

Reader Can read up to Records behind
a read_uncommitted consumer 900 — the high watermark 0
a read_committed consumer 810 — the LSO 90, none of which are in the open transaction

That table is the source of most EOS operational pain. One stuck transaction blocks the partition, not just the transaction's own records.

7.6 Does this consumer honour the retractions, or not?

That is the whole meaning of the sixth term. A consumer either agrees to stop at the LSO and drop aborted records, or it does not. The switch is called isolation.level, and it has two values.

read_uncommitted is the default, still, in 4.3. It returns everything, including aborted records. read_committed returns only committed transactional records and non-transactional records, and stops at the LSO.

Why does it default to the value that looks wrong? Because read_committed costs latency, and the vast majority of topics have no transactional producers at all. For those topics the two settings behave identically. Kafka declines to make everyone pay for a feature most topics do not use.

The consequence is the one Rupaya keeps tripping over. EOS is opt-in on both sides, and the two sides are configured by different teams. Meera owns the producer. Priya owns the consumer.

8. What picture should you carry, and where does it lie to you?

In one breath: A clerk pins five notices to three boards, then a supervisor countersigns each board. Casual readers read everything. Auditors only act on countersigned notices, so they stop at the first notice with no countersign below it.

8.1 The clerk and the countersign

Meera has to post five notices to three different noticeboards around Rupaya's building. There is no way to pin five sheets to three boards at one instant.

So the procedure is:

  1. Before posting anything, Meera tells the duty supervisor, Dev, which boards she is about to use. Dev writes it in the duty log.
  2. She walks around and pins all five notices. They are visible immediately. Anyone walking past can read them.
  3. She returns and tells Dev she is done.
  4. Dev writes the decision in the duty log first, as "settlement run 47: valid". Only then does he walk to each of the three boards. At each one he pins a small countersigned slip: "everything from clerk 4711 above this line is valid."
  5. Dev writes "run 47 complete" in the duty log.

Two kinds of people read the boards. Ben, who runs fraud scoring, is a casual reader: he reads every sheet as he passes, because a fast wrong signal is better for him than a slow right one. Priya is an auditor: she is only allowed to act on countersigned notices, because she moves real money.

So Priya, reading down a board, stops at the first notice that has no countersign below it yet, and waits there.

If Meera collapses in step 2, Dev eventually notices the run has been open too long. He goes and pins "everything from clerk 4711 above this line is void" to each board. The notices stay on the boards, because nobody takes them down. But Priya now skips them.

If Dev collapses, the duty log is the point. A replacement supervisor reads it, sees "run 47: valid" with no "complete" line, and finishes walking the boards. The decision was never in Dev's head.

8.3 Where does the analogy lie? Read this before it hardens

First lie: notices can be taken down. In a real building, someone eventually clears the void notices off the board. Kafka never does.

Aborted records occupy their offsets, occupy disk, and were replicated to every follower at full cost. They leave only when normal retention or compaction removes the segment they live in.

So you pay full price for work you threw away — bandwidth, disk, and replication. The abort costs more than the commit did, because it added the marker on top.

Second lie: the auditor can skip ahead. A human auditor who found an uncountersigned notice could look further down the board and come back.

Priya's read_committed consumer cannot. It is served nothing past the LSO. It is not filtering a stream it can see; the broker will not send it. That is what makes one stuck transaction a partition-wide stall rather than a per-record one.

Third lie: the supervisor decides. Dev records a decision that has already been reached, and the recording is what makes it true.

If Dev collapses after writing "valid" but before pinning any slips, the run is committed. The slips are a formality that a replacement will complete. There is no moment after PrepareCommit at which the outcome can be changed by anyone, including Meera.

This is exactly two-phase-commit's "the decision is the coordinator's durable write", and §10.4 shows why Kafka gets away with it where classic 2PC struggled.

Fourth lie: the boards are the only place notices go. The clerk model has no way to represent the thing that makes this useful. One of the three boards is the progress record itself.

Hold onto that. The transaction's atomicity includes "I have handled input 5000–5002", which is what turns duplicate-prevention into a property of the pipeline rather than a property of each sink.

9. What should you watch in the simulator?

In one breath: Open the page and step through one of Meera's settlement runs, one protocol message at a time. The one number that matters is the count under Priya's reader: what the outside world actually did.

Open visualization.html before reading §10. It shows one settlement run at Rupaya, stepped one protocol message at a time.

Five things are on screen. Look at them in this order:

  1. The app, on the left. Its producer state: transactional.id, PID, epoch, and which client call it is currently inside.
  2. The coordinator, centre top, with its __transaction_state partition below it. Watch the state name. This is the state machine of §10.3, and every state change is a record appended to that log, not a variable in memory.
  3. The three partition logs, centre. Records appear here early — during the produce steps, long before any decision. This is §6 made visible. Colour shows the record's transactional status; the marker is the wide slip at the end.
  4. The LSO line on each partition log. Watch when it moves. It does not move when records are written. It moves when a marker lands.
  5. The two readers, on the right. Identical except for isolation.level — Priya's and Ben's. The counter under each one, "payout rows the bank job has acted on", is the whole point of the lab. It is what the outside world actually did.

The single most useful habit: before each step, say out loud what Priya's read_committed reader is allowed to see, then step. If you can predict that number for the whole run, you understand the protocol.

Two controls are worth using immediately. Abort instead of commit — watch that nothing is deleted. And Crash: coordinator, after PrepareCommit — watch the decision survive.

10. How is the machine built, one failure at a time?

In one breath: Six versions. Each one is broken by a specific failure, and the next version is the fix. If you can name the failure that produced each piece, you understand the design. If you memorise the final protocol, you do not.

10.1 Version 1 — why not just flip a flag when you are done?

This is the simplest idea that could possibly work. Meera's producer writes all five records with a pending flag. When the batch is done, it goes back and flips each flag to committed.

It dies at once, twice over.

It requires mutating a written record. The log is append-only. That is not a policy; it is the reason btree-and-lsm said sequential writes win, and the reason segment files can be memory-mapped, replicated by byte range, and served with sendfile.

Going back to flip a bit in offset 810 means random writes into sealed segments, and re-replicating them. The entire performance argument for Kafka evaporates.

And it needs the flip to be atomic across three brokers anyway. Even if you could mutate records, "flip the flag on ledger-entries-1, merchant-payouts-0 and __consumer_offsets-12 at one instant" is precisely the distributed atomic update we were trying to avoid.

Version 1 does not solve the problem. It renames it.

The requirement this reveals: the decision must be expressible as an append, and the reader must be able to find it without leaving the partition.

10.2 Version 2 — what does the real protocol do, offset by offset?

This is the real protocol. Here is Meera's whole settlement run, with real offsets.

Starting state: ledger-entries-1 has a log-end offset of 810, merchant-payouts-0 is at 44, __consumer_offsets-12 is at 9001. The app's transactional.id is settlement-3.

This trace is the protocol as KIP-98 defined it, and as every broker before 4.0 ran it. Kafka 4.0 changed two steps for efficiency and one for correctness; §10.8 covers exactly what changed and why. Learn this version first. The newer one is an optimisation of it, and the old shape is still what runs whenever an older client connects.

Step 1 — initTransactions(). The producer hashes settlement-3 onto a partition of __transaction_state. There are 50 of them by default, which is the broker setting transaction.state.log.num.partitions = 50. It asks for that partition's leader with FindCoordinator, then sends InitProducerId.

Dev, the coordinator, assigns PID 4711, epoch 0 and appends to __transaction_state-17:

{ transactionalId: settlement-3, pid: 4711, epoch: 0, state: Empty }

That write is replicated like any other Kafka write. It obeys transaction.state.log.replication.factor = 3 and transaction.state.log.min.isr = 2 — the durable-configuration trio from the kafka lab, applied by default to Kafka's own bookkeeping.

If a previous producer had used settlement-3, this is where it dies. The epoch is bumped past whatever it holds, and any transaction it left open is aborted. That is the fencing of §10.6.

Step 2 — beginTransaction(). Nothing happens on the network. This call sets a flag inside the producer object.

The coordinator does not know a transaction has begun. That is not a detail. It is why step 3 exists.

Step 3 — the first send() to ledger-entries-1. Before the records go anywhere, the producer sends AddPartitionsToTxn(ledger-entries-1) to the coordinator, which appends:

{ pid: 4711, epoch: 0, state: Ongoing, partitions: [ledger-entries-1] }

This is when the transaction's clock starts. The 4.3 producer documentation is explicit: "The start of the transaction is set at the time that the first partition is added to it."

Not beginTransaction(). If Meera's code calls beginTransaction() and then spends four minutes fetching reference data before its first send(), none of those four minutes count against transaction.timeout.ms.

Step 4 — the produce. Three records are appended to ledger-entries-1 at offsets 810, 811, 812. Each carries PID 4711, epoch 0, a sequence number, and a bit marking it transactional.

Stop here and notice what is now true:

  • the records are durably in the log, replicated, past the high watermark;
  • Ben's read_uncommitted consumer can read them right now;
  • Priya's read_committed consumer is stopped dead at 810, the LSO;
  • and no decision exists anywhere.

Step 5 — merchant-payouts-0. A new partition, so another AddPartitionsToTxn goes first. The coordinator's partition set becomes [ledger-entries-1, merchant-payouts-0]. Then the produce: offsets 44, 45.

Step 6 — sendOffsetsToTransaction(offsets, groupMetadata). Two messages, and they go to two different brokers:

  • AddOffsetsToTxn(groupId = "settlement") goes to the transaction coordinator, which adds __consumer_offsets-12 to the partition set;
  • TxnOffsetCommit goes to the group coordinator. That is the broker that owns the settlement group — a different role, and usually a different machine. It appends the offset record to __consumer_offsets-12 at offset 9001, marked transactional exactly like the payload records.

This step is where "record that the input was handled" becomes just another output inside the set. The groupMetadata argument matters enormously, and §10.7 is entirely about it.

Step 7 — commitTransaction(). The producer sends EndTxn(commit). The coordinator appends:

{ pid: 4711, epoch: 0, state: PrepareCommit, partitions: [3 partitions] }

This is the commit point. Once that record is replicated to transaction.state.log.min.isr replicas, the run is committed. Not "will probably commit". Committed.

Every broker, the producer, and every network link involved can now fail, and the outcome is still commit.

Step 8 — the markers. The coordinator sends WriteTxnMarkers to the leader of each of the three partitions. Each broker appends a control record:

ledger-entries-1       offset 813    COMMIT(pid 4711)
merchant-payouts-0     offset 46     COMMIT(pid 4711)
__consumer_offsets-12  offset 9002   COMMIT(pid 4711)

The moment ledger-entries-1's marker lands, that partition's LSO jumps from 810 to 814. Every waiting read_committed consumer is released. Priya's job receives 810, 811 and 812, silently skips 813, and its next position is 814.

Step 9 — CompleteCommit. The coordinator appends the final state to __transaction_state-17 and forgets the transaction. Meera's commitTransaction() call returns.

Read the trace once more with one question in mind: at which step did the outside world's behaviour change?

Answer: step 8, and only step 8 — and only for readers who asked. Steps 1–7 are all invisible to read_committed consumers, and steps 4 and 5 are fully visible to read_uncommitted ones.

🔮 Predict. The producer completes step 7, so PrepareCommit is durably written. Then the producer process is killed instantly. It never comes back. What happens to the five records?

Answer

They commit. All five.

The producer is not a participant in the decision. It is the client that requested one. After EndTxn is accepted and PrepareCommit is durable, the coordinator drives steps 8 and 9 on its own. Whether the producer is alive to hear the answer changes nothing.

If you guessed "the transaction aborts because nobody is there to commit it", you are still picturing the producer as a participant that has to vote. It voted already, in step 7, and the vote was written down.

The producer's death costs exactly one thing: Meera's application does not learn that its commit succeeded. On restart it will reprocess from the last committed offset — which, because the offset commit was inside the transaction, is 5003. It resumes correctly at 5003 and the batch is not redone.

This is the payoff. Compare it to the §2 crash, which was at the same place in the sequence and produced a double payout to TeaHouse.

Three things to take from the diagram, rather than memorise.

The Prepare* states are the only interesting ones. Everything before them is reversible; nothing after them is. A coordinator that recovers and finds a Prepare* state has exactly one job: finish writing markers. It does not need to ask anybody anything.

There is no voting phase. In two-phase-commit, the coordinator asks participants "can you commit?" and they answer. Here the coordinator tells partition leaders to write a marker and they do.

A partition leader cannot refuse a marker. There is nothing for it to refuse: the data is already appended, and it holds no locks and no conflicting state. This is why the protocol is 2PC-shaped but missing half of 2PC's problems.

Dead is not a failure state. It is expiry. A transactional.id that has been silent for transactional.id.expiration.ms is forgotten, freeing coordinator memory. The default is 604800000 milliseconds, which is 7 days.

If Meera's app then restarts with that id, it simply gets a fresh PID. The relevance is capacity: an application that mints a new transactional.id per run accumulates state on the coordinator for a week per id, which is a real way to fall over.

10.4 Why does this two-phase commit not block?

In one breath: Classic 2PC blocks because a participant that has voted yes is stuck holding locks until the coordinator comes back. Kafka's participants never vote, its coordinator's memory is a replicated log, and the only waiting left is done by readers, who can afford it.

This is the section to remember when the topic comes up in an interview or a design review, because it generalises far beyond Kafka.

The two-phase-commit lab's central complaint went like this. After a participant votes YES it is in an uncertain state. It must hold its locks and can neither commit nor abort.

If the coordinator dies at that moment, the participant blocks for as long as the coordinator is gone, with no timeout that is safe to apply. It cannot guess, because guessing wrong breaks atomicity. That is 2PC's blocking problem, and it is not fixable within 2PC.

Kafka's protocol has the same two phases and does not have that problem, for three separate reasons that stack.

1. Participants never enter an uncertain state, because they never vote. A partition leader that has accepted transactional records holds no locks, blocks no readers of other data, and has made no promise it might have to keep.

Its log is append-only and already correct. The outstanding question is only how a reader should interpret a range of it. There is no state a broker is stuck in.

2. The coordinator's memory is a replicated log, so the coordinator is recoverable. The classic protocol's coordinator is a process holding a decision. If it dies, the decision is inaccessible until it returns.

Kafka's coordinator holds nothing. Every state transition is a record in an ordinary Kafka partition with replication factor 3. Coordinator failover is a partition leader election, which Kafka already does in milliseconds, followed by replaying the log to rebuild the in-memory map.

two-phase-commit told you the fix for a blocking coordinator was to replicate the coordinator's state. Kafka gets it for free, because replicating a log is the only thing it does.

3. Where waiting does happen, it is bounded and voluntary. Somebody does still wait. Priya's read_committed consumer is stuck at the LSO.

But that wait is bounded by transaction.timeout.ms. The default is 60 s, capped by the broker's transaction.max.timeout.ms, whose default is 900000 ms, which is 15 min. An abandoned transaction is unilaterally aborted by the coordinator on a timer.

And the wait is voluntary. Ben's read_uncommitted consumer, on the same partition, never waits at all.

The transferable idea. 2PC blocks when a participant must hold a decision it cannot resolve. Kafka moved the uncertainty out of the writers and into the readers. Readers can be given a timeout, a fallback and a choice. A reader that waits too long is merely stale. A writer that waits too long is holding locks that stop everyone else.

When you meet a distributed commit problem in any system, this is the question worth asking first: can the uncertainty be pushed to the reading side? If the answer is yes, you get a much cheaper protocol.

What this costs, stated honestly. The LSO stall is not free and it is not local. §11.1 traces a 60-second stall that hits every consumer of a shared topic, including applications with no connection to the transaction at all.

Kafka traded a bad blocking property for a milder one. It did not eliminate blocking.

10.5 Version 3 — what does the reader have to do, and why is abort not undo?

In one breath: The broker cannot afford to strip aborted records out of what it sends. So it sends them, plus a list of which ones to ignore, and the client library drops them. Nothing is ever deleted, and offsets stop being consecutive.

Version 2 is complete from Meera's point of view. Now take Priya's.

Her read_committed consumer fetches from ledger-entries-1. The partition contains, physically:

offset  808  ordinary record (no transaction)
offset  809  ordinary record
offset  810  txn record   pid 4711 epoch 0     ← aborted transaction
offset  811  txn record   pid 4711 epoch 0     ← aborted transaction
offset  812  txn record   pid 4711 epoch 0     ← aborted transaction
offset  813  ABORT marker pid 4711
offset  814  txn record   pid 4711 epoch 1     ← committed transaction
offset  815  COMMIT marker pid 4711
offset  816  txn record   pid 5002 epoch 0     ← still open

The consumer must deliver 808, 809 and 814, nothing else, and must stop at 816.

How the filtering actually works. The broker does not remove the aborted records from the response. It cannot do so cheaply.

The data is served from the page cache in whole batches, in log order, by byte range. That is precisely what makes Kafka fast: sendfile, zero copy, no per-record work on the broker. Rewriting the response per consumer would destroy it.

So the broker does two things instead. It truncates the response at the LSO, and it attaches a list.

KIP-98 defines the field. The fetch response carries AbortedTransactions => [PID FirstOffset] — every aborted transaction that overlaps the byte range being returned. Kafka maintains this list per segment in a small on-disk index alongside the log, a .txnindex file, so producing it is a lookup rather than a scan.

The client does the rest. The consumer library reads the aborted list, tracks which PIDs are in an aborted transaction at each point in the stream, and discards their records before your poll() returns. It also discards the control records themselves. Priya's application sees 808, 809, 814.

Three consequences are worth holding on to, and all three surprise people in production.

Consequence 1: aborted data crosses the network. Every read_committed consumer downloads every aborted record and throws it away.

A pipeline with a high abort rate is paying full fetch bandwidth for data no application will ever see, multiplied by the number of consumer groups on the topic. Abort rate is a bandwidth metric, not just a correctness metric.

Consequence 2: offsets are not consecutive, for anyone. Markers occupy offsets, 813 and 815 above. Aborted records occupy offsets too, 810 to 812.

An application that consumed 809 and then 814 has not lost anything. But code that computes expectedNext = lastSeen + 1, tests that assert exact offsets, and "gap detector" monitoring built on a non-transactional topic will all misfire the day a transactional producer starts writing to it.

Consequence 3: nothing is reclaimed by aborting. The three aborted records sit in the segment, replicated to every follower, occupying disk and retention, until normal deletion or compaction removes the whole segment.

An abort is strictly more expensive than a commit: same data, plus a marker. There is no version of "roll back to save the write."

🔮 Predict. The still-open transaction at offset 816 belongs to a completely different application. It is Ben's fraud-scoring service, which also writes to ledger-entries. It has hung: its process is frozen and it will never call commit or abort. Meanwhile Meera's settlement service keeps working perfectly and keeps writing new committed transactions to offsets 817, 818, 819…

What does Rupaya's downstream read_committed reader see?

Answer

Nothing after 815. Ever, until Ben's frozen transaction is resolved.

The LSO is "one less than the offset of the first open transaction", which here is 816. And the LSO is a property of the partition, not of a producer or an application.

Meera's own perfectly healthy committed records at 817 and beyond are on the far side of somebody else's undecided one, and are unreachable.

If you guessed that Meera's records get through because her transactions committed, you have attached the LSO to the writer. It is attached to the log.

Two things follow:

  • A transaction's blast radius is the partition, not the producer. Sharing a topic between a transactional application and anything else makes their latency a shared fate. This is a real argument for not mixing transactional and non-transactional producers on the same topic.
  • Ben's frozen producer will be timed out after transaction.timeout.ms, so in the ordinary case this resolves in 60 seconds. §11.5 covers the case where it does not resolve at all, which is the failure that motivated four years of protocol work.

Say it back. You have finished the intermediate layer. Explain to a friend, without the word filter, why a record that was cancelled still costs Rupaya disk, bandwidth and money.

A good answer sounds like "Cancelling does not rub anything out. The three lines are still on the page, still copied to three machines, still taking up space until the whole page is thrown away by age. And when Priya asks for that part of the log, the machine posts her the cancelled lines too, plus a note saying which ones to ignore. She pays the postage on rubbish."

10.6 Version 4 — what if the old instance is not dead, only asleep?

In one breath: A frozen process wakes up 90 seconds later and tries to commit a transaction from a job it no longer owns. You cannot detect that it was dead. You can only make sure its writes are refused.

Everything so far assumes there is only one producer per job. Remove that assumption.

Meera's settlement instance for captures-3, call it A, is mid-transaction. Records at 810 to 812 are written; nothing is decided. Then A's JVM enters a stop-the-world pause.

(jvm-garbage-collectors measured exactly this. A badly tuned heap can produce multi-second pauses, and a swapping or throttled container can produce much longer ones.)

While A is frozen:

t+0s    A pauses mid-transaction. Records 810-812 are in the log, undecided.
t+45s   A misses heartbeats. session.timeout.ms (45 s) expires.
t+45s   The group coordinator evicts A and rebalances captures-3 to instance B.
t+46s   B starts from the last committed offset, 5000, and reprocesses.
t+50s   B writes its own records: 813, 814, 815.
t+90s   A's pause ends. A has no idea any time has passed.
        A calls commitTransaction() on the transaction it began 90 seconds ago.

Without protection, A's commit succeeds. Records 810 to 812 become visible, and so do B's. TeaHouse is paid twice.

The offset commit races too. The batch may end up recorded as handled at 5003 by both, or A's stale offset commit may move the group backwards.

This is exactly the problem distributed-locking and split-brain are about, and the fix is the one those labs proved is the only one that works.

The fix: the transactional.id is fenced, not the process.

When B starts, its producer has the same transactional.id, settlement-3. Its initTransactions() sends InitProducerId to the coordinator, which:

  1. bumps the epoch for settlement-3 from 0 to 1;
  2. aborts A's open transaction — it writes PrepareAbort, then ABORT markers to ledger-entries-1 at 813, which pushes B's records to 814 to 816, then CompleteAbort.

Now A wakes up and sends EndTxn(commit) with epoch 0. The coordinator's current epoch is 1. The response is ProducerFenced.

The Java client treats this as fatal. The producer object is permanently unusable and the application must close it. Any produce A had in flight is rejected the same way.

A cannot do damage, and nobody had to determine that A was dead. That is the whole point, and it is worth saying in the general form one more time, because it is the single most reusable idea in distributed systems:

You cannot detect that a process has died. You can only make its continued existence harmless, by attaching a generation number to the right to act and refusing anything that carries a stale one.

The mistake this makes possible. All of that depends on A and B agreeing on the string settlement-3.

Suppose the application sets transactional.id = UUID.randomUUID().toString() at startup. Or it embeds a pod name, a start timestamp, or a container id. Then B's id is not A's id. No epoch is bumped. A is not fenced. A's transaction is not aborted.

You get every problem above, plus a transaction that stays open until it times out. §11.3 covers the symptoms; it is the most common EOS misconfiguration there is.

10.7 Version 5 — what if the zombie has lost the partition, not the producer?

In one breath: Fencing a producer and fencing the right to record progress for a partition are different jobs. The first design solved it by making one producer per partition, which was unbearable. The second sends the consumer's group generation along with the offsets.

Version 4 fences a producer. It does not fence the right to commit offsets for a partition, and those are different things.

Look again at the timeline. Suppose A and B do use different transactional.ids. Or, more subtly, suppose one process handles several input partitions with one producer.

A's transaction includes an offset commit for captures-3. Between A beginning that transaction and A committing it, the group rebalanced and captures-3 moved to B. A's offset commit is now a stale writer updating the progress of a partition it no longer owns.

The producer epoch cannot catch this, because A is not fenced. From the transaction coordinator's point of view, A is a perfectly valid producer doing perfectly valid work.

What A has lost is not its producer identity but its group membership, and the group coordinator is the only broker that knows.

How the original design avoided the problem: by making it impossible.

The first version of EOS ran from Kafka 0.11 through 2.4. It is retroactively called eos-alpha, or EOS v1. It required the transactional.id to be a static encoding of the input partition. Literally myapp-captures-3.

Then "the producer for captures-3" and "the owner of captures-3" were the same identity by construction, and producer fencing covered both.

The cost was brutal. A single application instance consuming 40 partitions needed 40 producers, each with its own transactional.id, its own coordinator registration, its own network connections, and its own transactions.

And every rebalance created new transactional.ids that had to be initialised from scratch, so rebalances became expensive as well as disruptive. The KIP-447 summary puts it plainly: "an application instance couldn't reuse a single thread-safe producer instance, but had to create one for each input partition."

The fix — KIP-447, Kafka 2.5, called eos-v2 or eos-beta. Stop trying to solve a group-membership problem with a producer identity. Instead, send the group membership with the offsets, and let the group coordinator do its own fencing.

That is what the second argument in this call is:

producer.sendOffsetsToTransaction(offsets, consumer.groupMetadata());

ConsumerGroupMetadata carries the consumer's member.id, its group.instance.id and its generation.id. That last one is the one that matters. It is the group's rebalance generation number.

The TxnOffsetCommit request carries them to the group coordinator, which compares the generation to its current one and rejects a commit from a stale generation.

So there are now two independent fencing mechanisms, catching two different zombies. Read the last column as "which sleeper wakes up".

fenced by held by catches
producer epoch transaction coordinator transactional.id an old producer instance still writing records
group generation group coordinator consumer group an old partition owner still committing offsets

With both in place, one producer can serve many input partitions, and one process needs one transactional.id for its lifetime rather than one per partition. That is the entire reason EOS became practical.

Where you meet this today. Kafka Streams' processing.guarantee accepts exactly two values in 4.3: at_least_once, which is the default, and exactly_once_v2. The valid-values list in the documentation is [at_least_once, exactly_once_v2].

The v1 and beta modes were deprecated by KIP-732 and removed in 4.0. There is nothing left to choose. Spring Kafka 3.x likewise supports only EOSMode.V2.

If you find exactly_once or exactly_once_beta in a config file, it is from before 4.0 and will now fail validation.

10.8 Version 6 — what happens when a message arrives from the past?

In one breath: A record can sit in the network for seconds, arrive after its own transaction was cancelled, and be adopted by the next transaction from the same producer. It took the Kafka project four years to close that hole properly.

Version 5 was the state of the art from 2020 to 2024, and it had a bug that took that long to close properly. It is the best failure in the lab, because you can derive it yourself from what you already know.

Two facts you already have:

  1. Records are appended to a partition before any decision exists (§6).
  2. A transaction's records are recognised by their PID and epoch — and in version 5, a normal commit does not bump the epoch.

🔮 Predict. Given only those two facts: a producer sends three records for transaction T1, but one produce request stalls in the network. T1 aborts. The producer then begins T2 and writes to the same partition. The stalled request finally arrives. What does the broker do with it, and what does a read_committed consumer eventually see? Commit to an answer — this one is genuinely derivable, and it took the Kafka project four years to close.

Answer

The broker appends it, and the consumer eventually sees it as committed data from an aborted transaction.

The record carries PID 4711, epoch 0. The partition currently has an open transaction for PID 4711, epoch 0 — because T2 has the same PID, and the commit did not bump the epoch.

Nothing in the record says which transaction it belongs to, so nothing can distinguish it. When T2 commits, its marker covers every undecided record from that producer in that partition, including this one.

If you guessed the broker rejects it as stale, you have assumed the record carries a transaction identifier. Before KIP-890 it did not. It carried a producer identifier, and both transactions had the same one.

Exactly-once is violated by the mechanism whose whole job is to enforce it. The trace below walks it with timings, and then the two-part fix.

Now the network does what networks do.

t+0     Txn T1. AddPartitionsToTxn(ledger-entries-1). Produce 3 records.
        Two land. The third request stalls somewhere in the network.
t+2s    The client gives up on the stalled request and aborts T1.
        ABORT markers are written. LSO advances. T1 is over.
t+3s    The same producer begins T2. Same PID 4711, same epoch 0.
        AddPartitionsToTxn(ledger-entries-1) again. Produces records.
t+4s    The stalled T1 request finally arrives at the broker.

The broker sees a transactional record with PID 4711, epoch 0, for a partition that currently has an open transaction for PID 4711, epoch 0. It appends it. When T2 commits, that record from the aborted T1 is committed by T2's marker.

A record that was explicitly aborted is now durably visible to every read_committed consumer. Exactly-once is violated by the mechanism whose entire job is to enforce it.

The second variant is worse, because it never resolves. Suppose T2 does not touch ledger-entries-1.

The delayed record is appended to a partition that has no open transaction for it and never will. No marker for PID 4711 is ever coming. The LSO for that partition stops at that record's offset and stays there forever.

This is a hanging transaction. KIP-890 describes it as "hanging transactions where the last stable offset (LSO) fails to update, blocking log cleanup and read_committed consumers."

There is no timeout that saves you. From the coordinator's point of view there is no transaction: nothing timed out, nothing is open, nothing is wrong. The partition is simply stuck.

The same thing happens if a buggy or non-Java client produces to a partition it never added to the transaction.

Fix, phase 1 — verification, server-side, and it works with old clients. Before appending a transactional record, the partition leader asks the transaction coordinator one question: is this partition genuinely part of an open transaction for this PID and epoch?

If not, the append is rejected with INVALID_TXN_STATE. This is implemented as an AddPartitionsToTxn request with a verifyOnly flag.

It closes the hanging-transaction variant completely, for every client version, and it is on by default: transaction.partition.verification.enable = true in 4.3.

It does not close the first variant. A delayed record from T1, arriving while T1's own partition is legitimately open again in T2, passes verification — the partition really is in an open transaction for that PID and epoch. Nothing distinguishes the two transactions, because nothing in the record identifies which one it belongs to.

Fix, phase 2 — give every transaction its own identity. This is transaction version 2. Bump the producer epoch on every commit and abort, not only on init. The EndTxn response returns the new epoch for the producer to use next.

Now T1 is epoch 0 and T2 is epoch 1. KIP-890's own sentence is the whole idea: "each transaction can be identified by (producer id, epoch)". And so "messages from previous transactions will be fenced because they will have an older epoch."

The delayed T1 record carries epoch 0 against a current epoch of 1, and is rejected.

Where this stands today. The 4.3 Transaction Protocol page states that "the protocol is automatically enabled on the server since Apache Kafka 4.0". It is controlled by the transaction.version feature flag, with =2 selecting the new protocol, settable at cluster creation with the storage tool or dynamically with the features tool.

Producers from 4.0 onward pick it up without a restart. The documentation says: "a producer will not upgrade mid-transaction, but on the start of the next transaction after it becomes aware of the server-side upgrade". Downgrades work the same way in reverse.

Two things changed that you will notice operationally.

First, AddPartitionsToTxn moved to the server. Instead of the client adding the partition and the broker separately verifying it, the broker adds it. One round trip instead of two.

Second, and this one shows up in dashboards: the old client-side 20 ms retry backoff for CONCURRENT_TRANSACTIONS is gone, and the server now retries internally.

The documentation says this plainly: "this can result in higher produce latencies reported on these requests. The transaction end to end latency … does not increase overall with this change. The time just shifts from client-side backoff to being calculated as part of the produce latency."

If you upgrade to 4.0+ and your produce latency percentiles step up while end-to-end latency is flat, this is why. The new knobs are add.partitions.to.txn.retry.backoff.ms and add.partitions.to.txn.retry.backoff.max.ms.

Checkpoint. The machine is complete. A coordinator whose memory is a replicated log. Records written before any decision. A decision published as an append into each partition. Readers who stop at the last undecided record. Two fencing mechanisms for two different zombies. And a per-transaction epoch, so that a message from the past cannot join the present. Every piece answers a failure you have now seen. The rest of the lab is what it looks like when it goes wrong, what it costs, and where its boundary is.

11.1 What happens when the producer dies mid-transaction?

Meera's service writes 810 to 812 into ledger-entries-1 and 44 to 45 into merchant-payouts-0. Then the pod is killed for running out of memory, before commitTransaction().

The thing for Asha to watch is not settlement's own dashboard. It is the LSO of both partitions, and the lag of every consumer group on those topics.

t+0     Records durable at 810-812 and 44-45. Coordinator state: Ongoing.
        ledger-entries-1 LSO frozen at 810. merchant-payouts-0 LSO frozen at 44.
t+0..60 Producers keep appending. HW advances. LSO does not.
        Every read_committed consumer of either topic is stalled.
        Every read_uncommitted consumer is completely unaffected.
t+60    transaction.timeout.ms expires (measured from the first
        AddPartitionsToTxn, not from beginTransaction()).
        Coordinator writes PrepareAbort, then ABORT markers into both
        partitions, then CompleteAbort.
t+60    LSO jumps to the current HW. Stalled consumers are released and
        receive a burst — everything written in the last 60 seconds at once.

Why does it work like that? §10.4. The uncertainty was moved to the readers, and the bound on their wait is the transaction timeout.

What is preserved is complete: no downstream ever saw a partial settlement. The ledger and payout rows for that batch were never visible, together or separately.

What is lost is up to 60 seconds of latency, for every read_committed consumer of every partition the dead transaction had touched — including applications with no relationship to settlement at all. That is the partition-scoped blast radius of §10.5.

Asha sees a latency cliff, not an error. Nothing logs a failure. A graph steps up by 60 seconds and steps back down.

If Rupaya's service level objective is a 99th-percentile latency of 5 seconds, one crashed transactional producer per hour ruins it, and nothing in the error dashboards will explain why.

The mitigation is two-sided. Set transaction.timeout.ms to the smallest value that safely exceeds your worst-case processing time. And keep transactional producers off topics shared with latency-sensitive consumers.

11.2 What happens when the coordinator dies, at five different moments?

The broker leading __transaction_state-17 fails, or is restarted during a rolling upgrade. Dev, in the story, collapses.

What Asha should watch is whether the transaction's outcome changes. It never does. Read the middle column as what the replacement finds in the minute book.

Coordinator dies… New coordinator replays __transaction_state-17 and finds It then
before PrepareCommit Ongoing leaves it; the timer eventually aborts it
after PrepareCommit, before any marker PrepareCommit re-sends WriteTxnMarkers to all partitions
after some markers, not all PrepareCommit re-sends to all; partitions that already have the marker ignore the duplicate
after all markers, before CompleteCommit PrepareCommit re-sends (all ignored), then writes CompleteCommit
after CompleteCommit CompleteCommit nothing — the transaction is finished

It works because the decision was never in the coordinator's memory. It was in a replicated log. Failover is a partition leader election plus a log replay, and there is no state that only the dead broker knew.

Atomicity is preserved in every row. The outcome is fixed at PrepareCommit, and no failure after that point can change it.

What is delayed is marker writing. It pauses for the duration of the leader election, plus the replay of that __transaction_state partition. That is why the coordinator's own load time is instrumented, as kafka.server:type=transaction-coordinator-metrics,name=partition-load-time-max.

Asha sees a brief LSO stall during a rolling broker restart, which is normal and self-healing. It becomes abnormal if the load times are large — which happens when __transaction_state has grown because of too many live transactional.ids. See §13.4.

11.3 What if the transactional.id is random? Silent, and it disables everything

The application sets transactional.id from something instance-scoped: a UUID, HOSTNAME, a pod name, a start timestamp.

This is extremely easy to do accidentally, because a per-instance id is the correct answer for almost every other identifier in a distributed system.

Asha's two tells are the number of distinct transactional.ids in kafka-transactions.sh --list, and ProducerIdCount on the brokers.

Instance A: transactional.id = settlement-a3f91e...   epoch 0
A pauses mid-transaction. Records 810-812 undecided.
Group evicts A; B takes captures-3.
Instance B: transactional.id = settlement-7c02bb...   epoch 0 (a NEW id)
  → the coordinator has no reason to touch A's id
  → A is not fenced
  → A's transaction is not aborted
B reprocesses and writes 813-815. Commits.
A wakes up and commits its own transaction successfully.

Records 810 to 812 and 813 to 815 are both committed. The batch is settled twice, and the offset commits from both transactions race. Every mechanism in §10.6 was present, and none of it fired.

Fencing is by name. Two different names are two different jobs, and the system is behaving exactly as configured. It believes two independent jobs are running, because that is what you told it.

Nothing that EOS was supposed to give Rupaya is preserved. Meera has at-least-once with extra latency and extra cost.

Asha sees duplicates in a pipeline where EOS is configured and enabled. Plus the diagnostic tell: a transactional.id count that grows with every deploy and never shrinks. Each abandoned id lingers for transactional.id.expiration.ms, 7 days by default.

If a team reports duplicates under EOS, list the transactional ids first. That single command usually ends the investigation.

The rule is short. The transactional.id must be a deterministic function of the work assignment. Something shaped like "<app>-<input-topic>-<partition>", or under EOS v2 "<app>-<task>". And it must be identical across restarts. Kafka Streams derives it for you, which is a genuine reason to prefer Streams over hand-assembled EOS.

11.4 What happens when a rebalance takes the partition mid-transaction?

This is ordinary group membership change: a deploy, a scale-up, or a max.poll.interval.ms breach, while a transaction is open.

The consumer's partition-revoked callback aborts the open transaction. The offsets were inside it, so they are not committed. The new owner starts from the last committed offset and reprocesses the batch. Its output goes into a fresh transaction; the abandoned one's records are aborted and filtered.

Under EOS v2 the stale owner is caught by the group generation check of §10.7 even if it somehow reaches the commit. A TxnOffsetCommit from an old generation is rejected by the group coordinator.

Exactly-once is preserved, fully. This is the designed path, not a degradation.

What is lost is work. The batch is done twice, once thrown away, and the aborted records still cost disk and replication. Under heavy rebalancing, EOS quietly amplifies the cost of every rebalance. The kafka lab's rebalance storm is the picture to hold.

11.5 What is a hanging transaction, and why does no timer save you?

This is §10.8's second variant. A transactional record lands in a partition that has no corresponding open transaction, so no marker will ever be written for it.

On brokers older than the verification fix, a delayed produce request or a buggy client is enough to cause it.

🔮 Predict. Ben's fraud service produced one record to ledger-entries-1 that the coordinator has no record of. Nobody notices for six hours. What is the first graph that moves, and what does the disk usage on those brokers do?

Answer

The first graph to move is the gap between the partition's high watermark and its LSO. The high watermark keeps climbing; the LSO does not move at all.

Disk usage on those brokers grows past what retention should allow, because log cleanup cannot reclaim a segment behind an undecided record.

If you guessed "consumer lag alerts fire first", they will fire — but only for read_committed groups, and only after they have fallen far enough to breach a threshold. The LSO gap moves immediately, from the first second.

Three signals together are the signature Asha should learn:

  1. one partition's LSO stops advancing while its high watermark keeps advancing — the gap grows without bound;
  2. read_committed consumer lag on that partition climbs linearly forever, while read_uncommitted consumers on the same partition are perfectly healthy;
  3. log cleanup stops reclaiming that partition — disk usage on those brokers grows past what retention should allow.

Why does it not time out? There is no transaction to time out. The coordinator has no record of this partition being in a transaction for that producer. Nothing is in Ongoing; nothing is late; every internal timer is idle.

It is resolved by hand, with the tool KIP-664 added:

kafka-transactions.sh --list                          all known transactions
kafka-transactions.sh --describe --transactional-id X state, PID, epoch,
                                                       timeout, partitions
kafka-transactions.sh --find-hanging --broker-id N     scan a broker
kafka-transactions.sh --abort ...                      force the marker

Note what the tool can and cannot do: abort only, never commit. There is no safe way to decide that an orphaned record should count, so the only recovery is to declare it void.

Correctness is preserved: no aborted data becomes visible. What is lost is availability of that partition for read_committed consumers, indefinitely, until someone runs the tool.

For Asha this is the classic 3am page. One consumer group's lag rises forever, the topic looks completely healthy from every other angle, and restarting the consumer changes nothing.

Reaching for --find-hanging early saves hours. On 4.0+ brokers with verification on, this failure should not originate any more. But it persists in clusters that have not upgraded, and a partition that is already hanging stays hanging after the upgrade, until it is aborted manually.

11.6 What if nobody ever changed isolation.level? Silent, and total

The producer side of EOS is turned on and tested. Meera ships it.

The downstream consumers belong to a different team, a different repository, or a third-party sink connector, and nobody changes their config.

Nothing changes at all. read_uncommitted is still the default in 4.3, and it returns aborted records to the application exactly as if they had been committed.

Every abort delivers its discarded work downstream as real data. Every timed-out transaction, every fenced zombie, every rebalance-triggered abort in §11.4.

This is the worst failure in the lab, for a reason worth stating carefully. The system reports success everywhere. Producer metrics show transactions committing. There is no error, no lag, no alarm.

And the failure is worse than not using transactions, because §11.4 shows that EOS deliberately creates aborted records on every rebalance — records that would never have existed in an at-least-once design.

So here is the check. When someone says "we have exactly-once", the only useful first question is: show me the consumer's isolation.level.

Then follow the topic downstream and ask the same question of every hop, including sink connectors and anything not written in Java.

11.7 What if the batch legitimately takes longer than the timeout?

A processing batch outruns transaction.timeout.ms. A slow enrichment call, a large batch, a GC pause, a throttled container — any of them will do it.

The coordinator aborts the transaction underneath the still-alive producer. The producer's eventual commitTransaction() fails. On 4.0+ with transaction version 2 the epoch has moved, so the producer is fenced and must be recreated. The batch is reprocessed.

If this is systematic rather than occasional, the application makes no forward progress at all. Every batch aborts, is retried, and aborts again.

The constraint on transaction.timeout.ms, whose default is 60000 ms, is two-sided. It must be:

  • larger than the worst-case wall-clock time from your first send() to your commitTransaction(), including GC pauses and slow external calls;
  • smaller than the broker's transaction.max.timeout.ms, whose default is 900000 ms, or 15 minutes. Otherwise InitProducerId fails outright with InvalidTxnTimeoutException;
  • and as small as you can stand, because it is also the worst-case stall you are imposing on every read_committed consumer of every partition you touch (§11.1).

Note the interaction with max.poll.interval.ms, which is 5 minutes by default. The two measure overlapping but different things.

By default the consumer is allowed to take five minutes over a batch that the transaction coordinator will abort after one. Long-processing applications get this pair wrong in both directions.

11.8 What if the consumer commits its own offsets?

enable.auto.commit is left at its default, which is true in 4.3, with a 5-second interval.

The consumer's background thread commits offsets outside the transaction. So the offset can advance while the transaction that produced the corresponding output is later aborted. Or the offset can advance for a batch whose transaction has not committed yet.

The atomicity of §5 is broken at exactly the point that made the whole design work. The progress marker is no longer one of the outputs.

It is easy to miss because the pipeline still mostly works. It fails only in the window between an auto-commit and an abort. So it produces occasional, unreproducible data loss under load and during rebalances, which is the hardest possible signature to debug.

The rule is not optional. Under EOS: enable.auto.commit=false, the application never calls consumer.commitSync() or commitAsync(), and offsets move only via producer.sendOffsetsToTransaction(...).

Kafka Streams and Spring Kafka's listener container both enforce this for you.

12. What exactly is promised, and what is refused?

In one breath: Kafka promises that a set of appends becomes visible all together or not at all, and that only one producer holds a job at a time. It promises nothing about your code, nothing outside Kafka, and nothing to a consumer that did not opt in.

12.1 What is guaranteed?

Five promises. The first is the one everything else exists to support.

Atomic visibility of a set of appends. For a set of records written across any number of partitions inside one transaction, every read_committed consumer eventually sees all of them or none of them. The offset commit for the input is one of those records, which is what makes a read-process-write loop atomic.

Durability of the decision at PrepareCommit. Once that record is replicated to transaction.state.log.min.isr replicas, which is 2 of 3 by default, the outcome is settled. No subsequent failure of any broker or the producer can change it.

At most one live producer per transactional.id. Enforced by epoch, checked on every write.

At most one live offset-committer per group generation. Enforced by the group coordinator via ConsumerGroupMetadata, under EOS v2.

Under transaction version 2, at most one live transaction per producer. The per-transaction epoch means a record from a previous transaction cannot be adopted by the current one.

12.2 What is not guaranteed?

Six refusals. Each one has bitten somebody at a company like Rupaya.

Anything outside Kafka. Not the database write, not the HTTP call, not the email, not the file. This is not a limitation to be engineered around. It is the definition of the feature. The transaction is a set of appends to Kafka logs.

That your processing function runs once. It does not. Under EOS your code may process the same input any number of times — every abort in §11.1, §11.4 and §11.7 is a reprocessing.

What is guaranteed is that the effects of the losing attempts never become visible. If your processing function has side effects, EOS does not touch them.

The precise term for what you get is exactly-once delivery of the outputs. Or, in Spring Kafka's careful phrasing, a read → process → write sequence where read and process are at-least-once and only the write is exactly-once.

Ordering across partitions. Unchanged by transactions. Ordering still exists inside a partition and nowhere else, exactly as the kafka lab said.

Freedom from duplicates for read_uncommitted consumers. They see everything, including work that was aborted. A single unconverted consumer downstream voids the guarantee for that path (§11.6).

Bounded latency for read_committed consumers. Their staleness is bounded only by transaction.timeout.ms, and only if the transaction is properly tracked. §11.5 is the case where it is not.

Deduplication of records the producer itself sent twice. If Meera's code calls send() twice for the same logical fact inside one transaction, both are committed. The transaction is atomic, not idempotent. That remains the idempotency lab's job.

12.3 Which assumptions is all of this standing on?

Six, and five of them are things a human has to get right.

  • transactional.id is stable across restarts and unique across concurrent workers. Violated in §11.3, and nothing detects the violation.
  • Every consumer that matters uses read_committed. Violated in §11.6.
  • Offsets move only through the transaction. Violated in §11.8.
  • Processing time fits inside transaction.timeout.ms. Violated in §11.7.
  • Durability is Kafka's ordinary durability. Transactions inherit every caveat from the kafka lab, including the big one: Kafka acknowledges when replicas hold the record in page cache, not after fsync. A committed transaction survives process crashes; a simultaneous power loss across the ISR is a different question, and transactions do not change that answer.
  • The clocks involved are only used for timeouts. No correctness property here depends on clock agreement — a welcome contrast with google-spanner.

12.4 What is the one sentence to remember?

Kafka gets multi-partition atomicity without a blocking commit protocol by writing first and deciding afterwards. The cost is paid entirely by readers who want committed-only data. They cannot see past the oldest undecided record in the partition. And the data they will eventually discard is written, replicated, stored, and transmitted to them anyway.

Every operational property in §11 is a restatement of that sentence.

A good answer sounds like "Inside Kafka, Meera's five writes appear together or not at all, and only one copy of her job can be writing at a time. That is all. The moment anything leaves Kafka, Kafka has no opinion. Priya's bank transfer, a database row, an email: for every one of those, Rupaya still needs a key and a list of what it has already done. And Meera's code itself may run five times on the same input; only one run's writing is allowed to show."

In one breath: Three ways to get exactly-once, and only one of them makes you assemble it by hand. Then the price, the metrics, and the six situations where the honest answer is "do not turn this on".

13.1 Which of the three routes should Rupaya take?

13.2 What does one Kafka Streams setting actually change?

One setting turns it on:

processing.guarantee=exactly_once_v2

Valid values in 4.3 are exactly [at_least_once, exactly_once_v2]. Two things it changes are worth knowing before you flip it.

It requires three brokers by default. The 4.3 documentation says so directly: "exactly-once processing requires a cluster of at least three brokers by default … for development you can change this, by adjusting broker setting transaction.state.log.replication.factor and transaction.state.log.min.isr."

A single-broker docker-compose will fail to start the application, and the error message points at the internal topic rather than at your setting.

🔮 Predict. Meera flips processing.guarantee to exactly_once_v2 on a Streams job that was running happily. Nothing else changes. One other setting silently takes a different default. Which one, and by how much?

Answer

commit.interval.ms. It defaults to 30000 under at_least_once and to 100 under exactly_once_v2. That is a factor of 300.

If you guessed "the batch size" or "the linger", those are unchanged. The changed one is the commit interval, and it is not a typo.

It is the single biggest performance consequence of the switch: a transaction every 100 ms per task, each one writing a marker into every output partition and every changelog partition it touched.

It is tuned that way because under EOS the commit interval is your end-to-end latency — nothing downstream is visible until the transaction commits. Raising it trades latency for throughput and marker overhead, and it is the first knob to reach for if EOS costs more than you expected.

Streams also runs its state-store changelogs inside the same transactions. That is what makes EOS meaningful for stateful operations — the aggregation problem of §4(a). That is the case where transactions genuinely have no substitute.

13.3 What can Kafka Connect do, and what can it never do?

Exactly-once for source connectors arrived in Kafka 3.3, via KIP-618. The framework atomically writes the source records and the connector's source offsets, and fences zombie tasks.

It is opt-in per worker, through exactly.once.source.support, and the connector must declare support. transaction.boundary controls whether a transaction spans a poll, a connector-defined interval, or is driven by the connector itself.

The sink side is not symmetric and never can be. A sink connector's output is by definition outside Kafka, so its guarantee depends entirely on the destination system's own idempotency. §13.6 is that discussion.

13.4 What does this look like in a Spring Boot service?

Here are the pieces, at the level you need in order to recognise them in a codebase:

spring.kafka.producer.transaction-id-prefix=settlement-
spring.kafka.consumer.isolation-level=read_committed
spring.kafka.consumer.enable-auto-commit=false

Setting transaction-id-prefix causes Spring Boot to create a KafkaTransactionManager. Wiring that into the listener container changes its behaviour to the EOS loop.

That loop is: begin a transaction before invoking your listener, let any KafkaTemplate sends inside the listener join it, call sendOffsetsToTransaction on success, and abort plus reposition the consumer on exception.

Spring Kafka 3.x supports only EOSMode.V2. The alpha and beta modes are gone, matching KIP-732.

The prefix matters for the reason §11.3 gave. Spring appends a suffix derived from the assigned partition, so that the resulting transactional.id is stable per unit of work rather than per process. Do not put anything instance-specific in the prefix.

The question every Spring developer asks next, and the honest answer:

"Can I put my JPA repository save inside the same transaction?"

No. You can nest the two transaction managers so that the database commits and then Kafka commits. Spring Kafka has historically offered a ChainedKafkaTransactionManager for exactly this, and the reference documentation now records that "the ChainedKafkaTransactionManager is now deprecated, since version 2.7."

It was deprecated because it never delivered what its name suggested. Nesting gives you an ordering, not atomicity. If the database commits and the Kafka commit then fails, the record is reprocessed and the database write happens twice.

There is still a window. You have only chosen which side of it to be on.

The real answer for database-plus-Kafka is the one you already own: transactional-outbox on the way out of the database, and idempotency's key on the way into anything that is not Kafka. §13.6 completes the map.

13.5 What does EOS actually cost Rupaya?

Five costs, in the order they usually bite.

  1. Marker amplification. Every transaction writes one control record into every partition it touched. A Streams application with a 100 ms commit interval, 24 output partitions and 12 changelog partitions writes ~360 markers per second per task. Markers are small, but they are appends, replications, and index entries.
  2. Round trips per transaction. AddPartitionsToTxn once per new partition, then AddOffsetsToTxn, TxnOffsetCommit and EndTxn — all amortised over the transaction's size. Transaction size is therefore the main throughput dial: the same number of records in ten times fewer transactions costs roughly a tenth of this.
  3. Reader staleness. Nothing is visible downstream until the transaction commits, so your commit interval becomes a latency floor for the entire downstream pipeline.
  4. Wasted writes on abort. Every aborted transaction paid the full price of producing, replicating and storing records nobody will ever read — and every read_committed consumer will download them anyway.
  5. Coordinator state. Every live transactional.id occupies coordinator memory and space in __transaction_state for transactional.id.expiration.ms, which is 7 days, after its last activity. An id-per-run design is a slow-motion capacity incident.

And there is a new entry, since Kafka 4.1: Kafka as a participant in somebody else's two-phase commit.

KIP-939 added transaction.two.phase.commit.enable — a broker config, default false, with a matching producer config. It also added Producer#prepareTransaction(), and an initTransactions(boolean keepPreparedTxn) overload that lets a restarting producer keep a prepared transaction instead of aborting it.

Together these let an external transaction coordinator hold Kafka in a prepared state and decide the outcome later. Flink's checkpoint coordinator is the flagship consumer, via FLIP-319.

The producer config names the hazard itself: "If set to true, then the broker is informed that the client is participating in two phase commit protocol and transactions that this client starts never expire."

Never expire. The timeout that bounds every read_committed consumer's wait in §11.1 is switched off.

A prepared transaction whose external coordinator dies blocks the partition until a human intervenes. That is two-phase-commit's original blocking problem, deliberately re-admitted, in exchange for genuine cross-system atomicity.

Which is why it is off by default, and why it is meant for frameworks with their own durable coordinator, not for application code.

So the honest 2026 answer to "can I have Kafka and my database commit atomically?" is this. The protocol now supports it, but only if something else is playing the coordinator role properly, and you should be very sure you want to re-introduce a blocking coordinator before you do. For an ordinary service, the outbox is still the answer.

13.9 When is EOS the wrong answer?

Six situations. In each of them the honest engineering decision is to leave it off.

  • The effect leaves Kafka. Then the guarantee does not apply and the complexity is pure cost. Use idempotency.
  • The sink is already idempotent. A keyed upsert into a database or a compacted topic makes duplicates harmless for free. Do not buy a guarantee you already have.
  • Latency matters more than duplicates. Metrics, logs, telemetry, clickstream: the LSO stall is a worse problem than the occasional duplicate record.
  • The topic is shared with latency-sensitive consumers you do not control. Your transaction duration becomes their 99th-percentile latency (§10.5, §11.1).
  • You cannot guarantee a stable transactional.id. Without it, EOS is off while looking on (§11.3). This is a real constraint in some orchestration setups.
  • You are on brokers older than 3.6 and cannot upgrade. You are exposed to §10.8's hanging transactions, with no verification and no per-transaction epoch.

14. Which beliefs about exactly-once are wrong, and what kills each one?

In one breath: Eight beliefs, each of which feels right for a reason. For each one there is a small concrete thing that destroys it. Read the table, then read the eight short stories.

The belief The smallest thing that kills it What is true instead
14.1 my code runs once a 60 s timeout, an abort, a reprocess your function is at-least-once; only the winning attempt's writes show
14.2 abort is a rollback read the partition with read_uncommitted after an abort abort is an append plus a client-side filter
14.3 the producer is enough §11.6 — the consumer default EOS is a property of a whole path
14.4 any unique string will do as an id §11.3 — a fresh id fences nobody it names a job, not a process
14.5 these are ACID transactions two writers interleave freely in one partition you get A and D; no C, no I
14.6 it is just slower a consumer that saw records in milliseconds now waits for a commit the cost is latency and coupling, not CPU

14.1 "Exactly-once means my code runs once"

This feels right because the name says exactly-once.

The smallest counterexample: a transaction times out at 60 s, aborts, and the batch is reprocessed. Meera's processing function has now run twice on the same input. If it increments a counter in an external cache, that counter is wrong.

What is true instead: the guarantee is about the visibility of outputs written into Kafka, not about the number of times your function executes. Your function runs at-least-once, always. Only the winning attempt's Kafka writes become visible.

14.2 "Abort is a rollback"

This feels right because every database you have used implements abort as undo, and the API is even called abortTransaction().

The smallest counterexample: produce three records, abort, then read the partition with isolation.level=read_uncommitted. All three records are returned, at their original offsets, from disk.

What is true instead: an abort is an append, plus a filter that the client applies. The append is a marker saying "the records above from this producer do not count". Nothing is removed, no space is reclaimed, no offsets are freed, and every byte was replicated at full cost.

It is not a rollback, and it is not a saga compensation either. A compensation is a new business action that reverses an effect that really happened. An abort ensures the effect never becomes visible in the first place.

Three different things. Mixing them up leads to designs that assume storage is reclaimed, or that downstream systems need compensating.

14.3 "Turning it on in the producer is enough"

This feels right because transactions are a producer API. That is where the code change is.

The smallest counterexample: §11.6. isolation.level is read_uncommitted by default in 4.3, so an untouched consumer reads aborted records as real data.

What is true instead: EOS is a property of a path, and every hop on the path has to opt in. One consumer, one sink connector, one non-Java client left on the default voids the guarantee for everything downstream of it.

14.4 "The transactional.id is just a name, any unique string will do"

This feels right because "unique identifier" is what you want for almost everything else in a distributed system, and a UUID is the reflexive answer.

The smallest counterexample: §11.3. A fresh id per process means the old process is never fenced, so two instances commit overlapping work and both succeed.

What is true instead: it is the name of a job, and its whole function is that a new holder can invalidate the previous one. Uniqueness across concurrent workers, stability across restarts. Getting this wrong disables the feature without any error anywhere.

14.5 "Kafka transactions are ACID transactions"

This feels right because the API is begin / commit / abort.

The smallest counterexample: two transactions producing to the same partition interleave freely. There is no isolation between concurrent writers, no conflict detection, no read-your-writes, and no way to read a value and act on it atomically.

What is true instead: compared with acid, you get A and D. A is atomic visibility of a set of appends. D is Kafka's ordinary durability, with the page-cache caveat included.

You do not get C in any application sense, and you do not get I as isolation-levels defines it. read_committed is a visibility rule for completed transactions, not a concurrency-control mechanism. There are no locks and no aborts due to conflict, which is exactly why §10.4's protocol can be non-blocking.

14.6 "Transactions make the pipeline slower but nothing else changes"

This feels right because the trade-off is usually stated as throughput versus correctness.

The smallest counterexample: a consumer that used to see records milliseconds after they were produced now sees them only when the transaction commits — and, if a producer hangs, not for 60 seconds. Meanwhile every downstream consumer downloads aborted records it will discard.

What is true instead: the cost is mostly latency and coupling, not CPU. Your commit interval becomes a downstream latency floor. Your transaction duration becomes the worst-case stall for every co-tenant on those partitions. And offsets stop being consecutive.

14.7 "A hanging transaction will time out eventually"

This feels right because everything else in Kafka has a timeout, and §11.1 really is resolved by one.

The smallest counterexample: §10.8's orphan. A transactional record sits in a partition for which the coordinator has no open transaction. No timer is running, because nothing is open.

What is true instead: timeouts resolve transactions the coordinator knows about. An orphaned record is invisible to every timer and requires kafka-transactions.sh --find-hanging and a manual abort. Alert on (high watermark − LSO) growing monotonically; nothing else will tell you.

14.8 "EOS is off by default because it is experimental"

This feels right because it defaults to off, and the history is full of KIPs fixing bugs in it.

The smallest counterexample: it has been in production at very large scale since 2017, and the 4.0 protocol work was a hardening, not a rewrite.

What is true instead: it is off by default because it is expensive for topics that do not need it and dangerous when only half-configured. The read_committed default alone would impose LSO tracking and staleness on every consumer in every cluster.

The right reading of the KIP history is the opposite of "unstable". A subtle protocol got four years of careful attention, and now identifies every transaction uniquely.

A good answer sounds like "I would ask to see the reader's setting, not the writer's. Turning it on where the records are written is the obvious half and it changes nothing on its own. If the people reading the topic never agreed to honour the cancellation slips, they are still being handed the cancelled work. 15.2 What is this constantly confused with?

  • The idempotent producer. That is per-partition deduplication of a network conversation between one producer and one broker. Transactions are atomicity across many partitions, plus offsets. Idempotence is a prerequisite for transactions and is on by default; transactions are not.
  • read_committed and a database's READ COMMITTED isolation level. Same words, different universes. Kafka's is a visibility filter on completed transactions; a database's is a concurrency-control level. isolation-levels has the real definitions.
  • The leader epoch and the producer epoch. Both are fencing tokens, both are called "epoch", and they fence completely different subjects — a stale replica leader versus a stale producer instance.
  • Compaction and abort. Compaction eventually removes superseded values by key. Abort removes nothing, ever.
  • A rebalance and a transaction abort. A rebalance triggers aborts (§11.4). They are cause and effect, not the same event.

16. Glossary — what does each word mean, precisely?

In one breath: Every term, in six themed groups rather than one long list. Each group names the section where the thing happened, so you can walk back to the story it came from.

16.1 The problem, and who is allowed to act (§1, §7.1, §7.2, §10.6)

Three-action problem — read, write output, record progress. Three separate physical acts with no atomic ordering that survives a crash in either gap.

transactional.id — a producer-side string naming a unit of work. The identity that gets fenced. Must be stable across restarts and unique across concurrent workers.

Producer id (PID) — an internal numeric id the coordinator assigns to a transactional.id. Records carry it so their transaction can be identified.

Producer epoch — a monotonically increasing generation number for the current holder of a transactional.id. Writes carrying a stale epoch are rejected with ProducerFenced / InvalidProducerEpoch. Under transaction version 2 it is bumped on every transaction, not just on init.

ProducerFenced / InvalidProducerEpoch — the errors a stale producer receives. Fatal for the Java producer; the object must be closed.

Zombie — a process that is still running and still acting after the system has concluded it is gone. Two kinds matter here: a stale producer instance and a stale partition owner.

16.2 The brokers that keep the minutes (§7.3, §10.2, §10.7)

Transaction coordinator — the broker leading the __transaction_state partition that a given transactional.id hashes to. Owns the transaction state machine and records every transition as a log record.

__transaction_state — the internal topic holding coordinator state. 50 partitions, replication factor 3, min ISR 2 by default. Being an ordinary replicated log is what makes coordinator failover cheap.

Group coordinator — a different broker role, owning a consumer group's membership and committed offsets. Under EOS v2 it performs the second fencing check, on group generation.

ConsumerGroupMetadata — member.id, group.instance.id and generation.id, passed to sendOffsetsToTransaction so the group coordinator can reject offset commits from a stale generation. The mechanism KIP-447 is built on.

16.3 The requests that make up one transaction (§10.2)

AddPartitionsToTxn — the request registering a partition with the coordinator before the first write to it. Starts the transaction timeout clock. Moved server-side under transaction version 2.

AddOffsetsToTxn / TxnOffsetCommit — the pair that pulls the consumer offset commit into the transaction: one to the transaction coordinator, one to the group coordinator.

EndTxn — the request carrying commit or abort. Triggers PrepareCommit / PrepareAbort.

PrepareCommit / PrepareAbort — the coordinator's durable record of the decision. The commit point: after this, no failure changes the outcome.

WriteTxnMarkers — the coordinator's instruction to each partition leader to append a control record.

16.4 What lands in the partition, and what the reader does (§7.4, §7.5, §10.5)

Control record / transaction marker — a COMMIT or ABORT record appended into each participating partition. Occupies an offset. Filtered out by the client, which is why observed offsets have gaps.

Last stable offset (LSO) — one less than the offset of the first open transaction in a partition. read_committed consumers are served nothing beyond it. A property of the partition, not of a producer.

isolation.level — consumer setting. read_uncommitted (the default) returns everything including aborted records; read_committed returns only committed and non-transactional records, up to the LSO.

Aborted transaction index — the per-segment on-disk index (.txnindex) that lets a broker cheaply return the AbortedTransactions list in a fetch response so the client can filter.

16.5 The eras of the feature (§10.7, §10.8, §13.6)

EOS v1 (eos-alpha) — the original design requiring one transactional.id per input partition, hence one producer per partition. Removed in 4.0.

EOS v2 (KIP-447, Kafka 2.5) — one producer for many input partitions, using consumer group metadata for the second fencing check. The only mode that still exists.

Transaction version 2 (KIP-890, on by default from 4.0) — the protocol variant that bumps the producer epoch on every transaction so that (pid, epoch) uniquely identifies a transaction, and moves AddPartitionsToTxn server-side.

Transaction verification — KIP-890 phase 1. The broker asks the coordinator whether a partition is genuinely in an open transaction before appending. transaction.partition.verification.enable, default true.

KIP-939 two-phase commit (Kafka 4.1) — prepareTransaction(), initTransactions(keepPreparedTxn) and transaction.two.phase.commit.enable, which let an external coordinator decide a Kafka transaction's outcome. Disables transaction expiry, and therefore re-admits two-phase-commit's blocking problem on purpose.

16.6 Timers, stuck things, and honest names (§11.5, §11.7, §13.2)

Hanging transaction — a transactional record in a partition for which no marker will ever be written. The LSO stops advancing permanently; no timeout applies. Resolved manually with kafka-transactions.sh --find-hanging and --abort.

transaction.timeout.ms — how long a transaction may stay open before the coordinator aborts it. Default 60 s, capped by the broker's transaction.max.timeout.ms (15 min). Also the worst-case stall it imposes on read_committed consumers.

transactional.id.expiration.ms — how long an idle transactional.id occupies coordinator state before moving to Dead. Default 7 days.

processing.guarantee — the Kafka Streams switch. at_least_once (default) or exactly_once_v2. Also silently changes commit.interval.ms from 30000 to 100.

Effectively-once — the honest name for what most systems deliver: at-least-once delivery plus idempotent processing. Kafka's EOS is the special case where the idempotent processing is provided by the broker, and only for effects that stay inside Kafka.

Kafka Exactly-Once — Solutions

Rungs 1–5 have right answers. Rungs 6–8 have worked reasoning; your answer can differ and still be good, provided it survives the same objections.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 A Kafka transaction makes one thing true: a set of appends — records written to any number of Kafka partitions, plus the consumer offset commit that says the input was handled — becomes visible to read_committed readers all at once, or never at all. The set is appends to Kafka logs and nothing else.

Used as a decision rule: if every output of the stage is an append to a Kafka topic, EOS applies. The moment one output is a database row, an HTTP call or a file, that output is outside the set and the transaction says nothing about it.

1.2 "It does not change how many times your function runs — it runs at-least-once, always. It changes whether the losing runs are ever visible."

What they bought: for a stage whose outputs are all Kafka topics, downstream consumers reading read_committed will never see the outputs of a run that did not complete, and the input offset advances at the same instant those outputs become visible.

1.3 A SQL rollback removes the effects: the rows go away and the space is reused. A Kafka abort removes nothing. The records are already appended to the log at permanent offsets, replicated to every follower. The abort is itself an append — a control record after the data saying "everything from this producer above this line does not count." The consumer client library does the filtering: the broker sends the aborted records over the network along with a list of aborted transactions, and the client drops them before your application sees them. A consumer that does not ask for filtering (isolation.level=read_uncommitted) receives them as ordinary data.

1.4 The three:

  • (a) No natural key where the duplicate lands. An aggregate — a rolling hourly total — has no identity to deduplicate on. This one is the hard impossibility: it is not that dedupe is expensive there, it is that there is nothing to key on, so the deduplication must happen upstream of the aggregation or not at all.
  • (b) The dedupe store is a second system, which rebuilds the dual-write problem at the point that was supposed to solve it. An expense plus a new failure mode, not an impossibility.
  • (c) The cost is per hop and hops multiply. An expense.

1.5 Kafka's reason: the two isolation levels behave identically on topics with no transactional producers, which is the overwhelming majority of topics. Making read_committed the default would impose LSO-bounded staleness and the head-of-line blocking of §11.1 on every consumer in every cluster, to protect a feature most of them do not use — and it would be a silent behaviour change on upgrade for everyone. The consequence you have to live with is that EOS is opt-in on both sides, and the two sides are usually configured by different teams.

1.6

  • Idempotent producer — deduplicates retries of a network conversation between one producer and one broker, per partition, using (PID, epoch, sequence). Protects the producer → broker hop. On by default since 3.0.
  • Transaction — atomic visibility of a set of appends across many partitions plus the offset commit. Protects the Kafka → Kafka hop, including the progress record.
  • Idempotency key — a client-minted identifier that lets a server recognise a replayed intention and return the original outcome. Protects any hop, including every hop that leaves Kafka, which is the only tool that does.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 At step 7, when the coordinator appends PrepareCommit to its partition of __transaction_state and that record is replicated to transaction.state.log.min.isr replicas (default 2). The record lives in an ordinary replicated Kafka partition, which is exactly why coordinator failure after this point cannot change the outcome (§10.4).

2.2

  • The transaction coordinator — leader of the __transaction_state partition that settlement-3 hashes to — receives AddOffsetsToTxn(groupId), which adds __consumer_offsets-12 to the transaction's partition set.
  • The group coordinator — the broker that owns the settlement consumer group — receives TxnOffsetCommit and appends the actual offset record.

The split exists because the two brokers own two different things: one owns the transaction's state machine, the other owns the group's offsets and its membership generation. Keeping them separate is what makes the second fencing check of §10.7 possible.

2.3 The broker does, and it is consulting the partition's last stable offset. This is not client-side filtering of a stream the consumer can see — the fetch response is truncated at the LSO, so the data beyond it is never sent. That is why one undecided transaction blocks the whole partition rather than just its own records.

2.4 LSO = 816 — one less than the offset of the first open transaction, which is producer 5002's record at 816.

  • read_committed: 808, 809, 814. (810–812 are aborted; 813 and 815 are control records; 816 is beyond the LSO and is never sent.)
  • read_uncommitted: 808, 809, 810, 811, 812, 814, 816. Control records are still filtered out by the client in both modes — they are never delivered to an application.

2.5

  • Leader epoch — fences a stale replica leader; introduced by KIP-101 to fix log divergence after failover (the kafka lab, §10.5 there).
  • Producer epoch — fences a stale producer instance holding a transactional.id, and under transaction version 2 also fences a stale transaction from the same producer.

The general object is the fencing token, unified in split-brain under its seven names — term, epoch, generation, fencing token, lease version, and now these two.

2.6 It starts at the first AddPartitionsToTxn — the producer documentation says so explicitly: "the start of the transaction is set at the time that the first partition is added to it." People wrongly assume beginTransaction(), which sends nothing over the network at all. The practical consequence: expensive setup work between beginTransaction() and the first send() is free, and expensive work after the first send() is on the clock.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 All five records commit. The coordinator drives the markers and CompleteCommit on its own; the producer is the client that requested a decision, not a participant in it.

The application observes only that it never learned the outcome. On restart it reads the group's committed offset — which is 5003, because the offset commit was inside the transaction that committed — and resumes there. The batch is not redone. Compare with §2's crash at the same point in the sequence, which double-paid TeaHouse.

3.2 Producer killed after sendOffsetsToTransaction, before EndTxn:

At t+30s — all five records are durably in their partitions, all undecided. Coordinator state Ongoing. ledger-entries-1 LSO 810, merchant-payouts-0 LSO 44, __consumer_offsets-12 LSO 9001. Every read_committed consumer of all three partitions is stalled at those offsets; every read_uncommitted consumer is unaffected and has already seen the payouts.

At t+120s — the 60-second timeout expired at t+60s. The coordinator wrote PrepareAbort, ABORT markers into all three partitions, then CompleteAbort. The five records are still physically present, marked aborted. The LSOs have jumped past the markers. The group's committed offset is still 5000, so the batch will be reprocessed by whichever instance owns the partition.

3.3 read_committed: only after the hung transaction is resolved, because the LSO is a property of the partition and sits at 44. A perfectly ordinary record at 46 is unreachable behind somebody else's undecided one at 44. read_uncommitted: immediately.

This is the single most surprising operational property of transactions and the strongest argument against mixing transactional producers onto shared topics.

3.4 TeaHouse is paid ₹3,920 — twice.

The mechanism that failed is producer fencing, and it failed because fencing is by name. A and B have different transactional.ids, so from the coordinator's point of view they are two unrelated jobs; no epoch is bumped, A is never fenced, and A's transaction is not aborted. Every EOS mechanism was enabled and behaving exactly as configured. (Simulator: scenario 6.)

3.5 Two things change immediately:

  1. The one they configured: output records and offset commits become transactional, and downstream read_committed consumers see them only at commit.
  2. The one they did not: commit.interval.ms changes from 30000 to 100 — the documented default under exactly_once_v2. Their pipeline now commits 300 times more often, writing a marker into every output and changelog partition each time.

A third, if the cluster is small: EOS requires at least three brokers by default, because transaction.state.log.replication.factor is 3. On a smaller cluster the application fails to start.

3.6 There is no data loss. Offset 813 holds a control record — a COMMIT or ABORT marker — which occupies a real offset and is filtered out by the consumer client before the application sees it. Aborted records occupy offsets the same way.

The monitoring rule is wrong: on any partition with a transactional producer, consecutive offsets in an application's view are not guaranteed and never were. Any logic of the form expectedNext = lastSeen + 1 breaks the day transactions are switched on upstream.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Raising transaction.timeout.ms 60 s → 600 s:

  • Less likely: §11.7, the transaction that legitimately outruns its own timeout and is aborted underneath a healthy producer. Long batches and long GC pauses stop causing spurious aborts.
  • Worse: §11.1's stall. The worst-case time a read_committed consumer can be held behind an abandoned transaction goes from 60 seconds to 10 minutes — and the people who experience it are not the team that changed the setting. They are every consumer of every partition that producer touches, including applications with no relationship to it.

4.2 With transaction.state.log.min.isr=1, PrepareCommit can be acknowledged by the leader alone.

Smallest sequence: the coordinator writes PrepareCommit (leader only, not yet replicated) → it sends WriteTxnMarkers and ledger-entries-1 writes its COMMIT marker → the coordinator's broker dies before the PrepareCommit record reaches any follower → a new leader is elected from a follower whose log ends before PrepareCommit → the new coordinator sees Ongoing, and eventually aborts → ABORT markers go to the remaining partitions.

Result: ledger-entries-1 has a COMMIT marker and merchant-payouts-0 has an ABORT marker for the same transaction. A read_committed consumer sees the ledger lines but never the payouts.

The broken guarantee is atomic visibility (§12.1). The general lesson: the durability configuration of __transaction_state is the durability of the decision, and it is exactly as important as the durability of your data topics.

4.3 Raising Streams commit.interval.ms 100 → 5000 under EOS:

  • Improves: throughput and broker load. 50× fewer transactions means 50× fewer markers written into every output and changelog partition, and 50× fewer coordinator round trips.
  • Degrades: end-to-end latency — and the unit is the commit interval itself. Under EOS nothing is visible downstream until the transaction commits, so the commit interval is a hard latency floor, not an average. Records processed at t+1 ms and t+4999 ms both become visible at t+5000 ms.

Also worse: an abort now discards up to 5 seconds of work instead of 100 ms.

4.4 Batching 3 → 3,000 captures per transaction:

  1. Throughput improves — the per-transaction round trips (AddPartitionsToTxn, AddOffsetsToTxn, EndTxn) and the marker writes are amortised over 1,000× more records.
  2. Latency worsens by the same factor — nothing is visible until the batch commits.
  3. The cost of an abort rises — a failure at record 2,999 discards all 3,000, and every one of them was written, replicated and stored.
  4. The LSO stall lengthens — the transaction is open far longer, so the worst-case block on every read_committed consumer of those partitions grows.

The binding constraint becomes transaction.timeout.ms (default 60 s): the whole batch must be produced and committed inside it. max.poll.interval.ms (5 min) constrains the same batch from the consumer side, and by default the two disagree — the consumer is allowed five minutes for work the coordinator will abort after one.

4.5 Running read_uncommitted and deduplicating downstream is sound only if every aborted record has a committed counterpart carrying the same deduplication key — that is, only if aborts are always retries of work that later succeeds identically.

It is strictly worse than never having used EOS when an abort represents work that never happens at all: a validation failure, a poison record aborted permanently, or a transaction aborted because the input was withdrawn. A deduplicator cannot tell "this is a second copy of something real" from "this was never real." Worse, §11.4 shows that EOS deliberately generates aborted records on every rebalance — records an at-least-once pipeline would never have produced. So this configuration takes on EOS's cost and its extra aborted records, and then disables the only thing that made those aborts safe.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 Redis dedupe set inside the transaction loop.

Smallest failing scenario: the service polls capture 5000, adds 5000 to the Redis set, writes its outputs, and the transaction then aborts (a rebalance, a timeout, anything in §11). The Kafka side is correctly voided. Redis is not. On reprocessing, the service sees 5000 in the set, skips it, and the capture is never settled by anyone.

Violated: no input is silently dropped — the at-least-once floor of §3. And the mechanism is the idempotency lab's sharpest failure, rebuilt exactly: the dedupe state lives in a different system from the effect, so the two can disagree. Kafka transactions cover appends to Kafka logs; a Redis SADD is not one.

6.2 read_committed with enable.auto.commit=true.

Smallest failing scenario: the consumer polls 5000–5002 and begins a transaction. Five seconds later — auto.commit.interval.ms — the background thread commits offset 5003 outside the transaction. At t+6s the transaction aborts. The outputs are void; the offset is not. Those three captures are never reprocessed and never settled.

Violated: atomic visibility (§12.1), by removing the offset commit from the set. The whole design of §5 depends on the progress record being one of the outputs; auto-commit quietly makes it a seventh, uncovered write. The signature is occasional, unreproducible loss under load and during rebalances.

6.3 Three replicas, all with transactional.id=settlement.

Smallest failing scenario: pod A is running and holding the id at epoch 3. Pod B starts and calls initTransactions() → epoch 4, and A's in-flight transaction is aborted. A's next write returns ProducerFenced, which is fatal, so A crashes. The orchestrator restarts A → epoch 5 → B is fenced and crashes. Repeat forever.

Violated: liveness. Correctness is intact throughout — that is what fencing is for — but the application makes no progress. The fix is the same rule as §11.3 read the other way: the id must be unique across concurrent workers as well as stable across restarts. One id per task assignment, not one per application.

6.4 transaction.timeout.ms=2000 with 800 ms batches.

Smallest failing scenario: a 1.5-second GC pause lands 1.2 seconds into a transaction. At 2.0 s the coordinator aborts it. The producer wakes at 2.7 s and tries to commit; under transaction version 2 its epoch has moved, so it is fenced and must be recreated. The batch is reprocessed.

Violated: liveness again, plus wasted writes. "Plenty of headroom" is the error — the budget must cover the worst case, including GC pauses, a slow broker, a leader election on an output partition, and a CONCURRENT_TRANSACTIONS retry storm. Note also which way the clock runs: it starts at the first AddPartitionsToTxn, so setup before the first send() is free, and everything after it counts.

6.5 @Transactional with a chained transaction manager.

Smallest failing scenario: the database transaction commits; the process is then partitioned from the Kafka cluster and the Kafka commit fails. The offset was never committed, so the record is reprocessed after the rebalance, and the database write happens a second time.

Violated: the claim of atomicity. Nesting gives you an ordering, not atomicity — you have chosen which side of the window to be on, not closed it. Spring's own reference records that ChainedKafkaTransactionManager has been deprecated since version 2.7, and this is why. The real answer is transactional-outbox on the way out of the database, and an idempotency key on the way into anything that is not Kafka.

6.6 "Aborted records are filtered by the broker."

They are not. The broker truncates the fetch at the LSO and attaches an AbortedTransactions list; the client does the filtering. So every aborted record is fetched over the network by every consumer group on that topic before being discarded.

Smallest failing scenario: a pipeline with a 30% abort rate and five consumer groups pays roughly 1.5× the aborted volume in extra broker egress, plus the disk and replication cost of writing the records in the first place. Nothing is incorrect — this is a capacity error, and abort rate belongs on the bandwidth dashboard, not only the correctness one. (The reason the broker cannot filter is the same reason Kafka is fast: fetches are served from the page cache in whole batches by byte range. Per-consumer rewriting would destroy sendfile.)

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 The six-hour LSO stall.

Diagnosis. The signature is unambiguous: high watermark rising, LSO flat, read_committed lag climbing linearly, read_uncommitted consumers healthy, and log cleanup no longer reclaiming that partition. That combination is a hanging transaction (§11.5) — a transactional record for which no marker will ever be written. Note what it is not: an abandoned transaction that will time out. No timer is running, because the coordinator has no open transaction for it.

Commands.

kafka-transactions.sh --list                            what the coordinators know
kafka-transactions.sh --find-hanging --broker-id <id>   scan the broker holding it
kafka-transactions.sh --describe --transactional-id <x> state, pid, epoch, partitions
kafka-transactions.sh --abort ...                       write the missing marker

Fix. Force the abort. The tool offers no commit, and that is correct: there is no safe way to decide that an orphaned record should count.

Prevention. Upgrade the brokers. transaction.partition.verification.enable (default true) makes the broker ask the coordinator whether a partition is genuinely in an open transaction before appending, which closes the origin of this failure for every client version; transaction version 2, on by default from 4.0, closes the related leak. Then add the alert nobody had: per-partition (high watermark − LSO), paging when it grows monotonically. Nothing else detects this, because nothing else is wrong.

7.2 Bank API call plus a Kafka write.

The pipeline splits at the boundary, and the split is the point:

captures ──▶ settlement service ──▶ bank API (external, not covered by EOS)
                    │
                    └──▶ merchant-payouts + offset commit (Kafka, covered by EOS)
  • What EOS covers: the payout row and the offset commit becoming visible together, so no downstream consumer sees a payout row for a batch whose progress was not recorded.
  • What it cannot cover: the bank call. abortTransaction() cannot unsend an HTTP request. This is the idempotency lab's crux, unchanged.
  • What carries the guarantee across the boundary: an idempotency key the bank honours, minted by the settlement service, deterministic in the capture id — e.g. settlement:{capture_id} — so that a replayed call after any abort or reprocessing returns the original outcome instead of moving money again.

The shape, then: call the bank first with the deterministic key, then write the payout row and commit offsets inside one Kafka transaction. A crash before the Kafka commit reprocesses the batch and re-calls the bank — safely, because the key is the same. A crash after it does not reprocess. If the bank does not support keys at all, you are in distributed-locking's box G: choose at-most-once or at-least-once deliberately, record the ambiguity durably, and reconcile.

7.3 A 5-second reader SLO against a 3-minute producer.

Both requirements are real, so something must give. Four ways out:

  1. Shrink the transactions. Split the 3-minute batch into many short ones. The 3 minutes was almost certainly batch size, not one indivisible operation. Cheap, and usually correct.
  2. Move the slow work outside the transaction. The clock starts at the first AddPartitionsToTxn, so enrichment, lookups and I/O done before the first send() cost nothing. Often turns 3 minutes into 3 seconds with no design change.
  3. Separate the topics. The stall is per partition, so give the slow producer its own topic and let the latency-sensitive consumer read a different one. Costs a topic and a fan-out step.
  4. Drop EOS for that stage and use an idempotency key at the sink instead.

Choice: (2) first, because it is free and usually sufficient; then (1); and (3) as the structural fix if the slow stage is genuinely slow. What you give up with (3) is the ability for the two consumers to read one ordered stream — they are now reading different topics and any cross-topic ordering assumption is gone.

7.5 The audit.

  1. isolation.level on every downstream consumer. Follow the topic to every hop, including sink connectors, non-Java clients and other teams' services. A wrong answer here means EOS has been doing nothing but adding cost — read_uncommitted is the default and nobody has to have done anything wrong for it to be set. This ends most investigations.
  2. transactional.id — how is it built? If it contains a UUID, a hostname, a pod name or a timestamp, nothing is ever fenced (§11.3). Cross-check with the id count over the last few deploys.
  3. enable.auto.commit, and any manual commitSync / commitAsync. If offsets move outside sendOffsetsToTransaction, atomicity is broken at the one place that made it work (§11.8).
  4. Is sendOffsetsToTransaction called with groupMetadata? Without it there is no group-generation fencing, and a rebalance mid-transaction can let a stale owner commit offsets for a partition it no longer holds (§10.7).
  5. Does anything in the pipeline touch the world outside Kafka? If it does, that hop is not covered no matter what the other four answers are, and it needs an idempotency key.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 The rolling hourly total.

idempotency's answer does not apply because an aggregate has no identity. The output is (TeaHouse, 14:00–15:00, ₹18,400). If an upstream duplicate added ₹1,200 twice, that number is wrong, and there is no key on the aggregate a downstream reader could use to notice — you cannot deduplicate a sum. The deduplication has to happen strictly upstream of the aggregation, at a point where the records still have identities, or not at all.

What does apply: transactions, and specifically Kafka Streams with processing.guarantee=exactly_once_v2, because the aggregation's state store changelog is written inside the same transaction as the output and the offset commit. That is the case where transactions have no substitute — a stateful operator's state, its output and its progress are one atomic set or the state drifts silently from the stream that produced it.

New operational properties you have taken on:

  • a commit interval of 100 ms by default, and it is now your downstream latency floor;
  • markers written into every output and every changelog partition on every commit;
  • a hard requirement for three brokers;
  • everything in §11 — including that a hung transaction now stalls consumers of your changelog topics too;
  • and a state store that must be restored from a compacted changelog on every reassignment, which is its own operational surface.

8.2 The shared topic.

The case against. Transactions are not a private setting. Three consequences land on the other five teams and none of them consented:

  1. Every read_committed consumer of that topic now stalls behind your transactions, up to transaction.timeout.ms. Your batch duration becomes their p99.
  2. Every consumer, at either isolation level, now sees non-consecutive offsets — markers and aborted records occupy them. Any offset arithmetic or gap detection anywhere on that topic breaks.
  3. Every consumer pays fetch bandwidth for your aborted records, and §11.4 guarantees you will generate them on every rebalance.

And one that lands on you: if any of those five teams' consumers are read_uncommitted — the default — they will act on your aborted records as real data, which is worse for correctness than not using transactions at all.

Conditions for yes: the topic gets its own partitions or, better, its own topic; every consumer is inventoried and moved to read_committed; transaction.timeout.ms is set to the smallest workable value and treated as a published SLO; transaction duration is monitored; and the offset-contiguity assumption is audited across all six teams' code. If you cannot do the inventory, the answer is no — because the failure you cannot rule out is the silent one.

8.3 KIP-939 for database-plus-Kafka atomicity in Spring Boot.

The mechanism is real. transaction.two.phase.commit.enable plus prepareTransaction() and initTransactions(keepPreparedTxn) genuinely let an external coordinator hold Kafka in a prepared state and decide later. This is not marketing.

The property it switches off. Read the producer config's own description: "the broker is informed that the client is participating in two phase commit protocol and transactions that this client starts never expire." The 60-second bound on how long a read_committed consumer can be blocked is gone. A prepared transaction whose external coordinator dies blocks that partition until a human intervenes.

What that costs. It is precisely two-phase-commit's blocking problem, deliberately re-admitted. §10.4's whole argument was that Kafka avoids blocking because the coordinator's state is a replicated log and the waiting is bounded. Using KIP-939 means you are now the coordinator, and you must supply the durable, replicated, recoverable decision log yourself. Flink can do this because its checkpoint coordinator already is one. A Spring Boot service is not.

Recommendation: no. In terms a non-specialist accepts: "This lets us hand Kafka a promise and decide later whether to keep it. If our service dies while holding that promise, Kafka waits for us — with no timeout — and every other reader of that topic waits too, until somebody notices and clears it by hand. We would be taking on the job of a transaction manager to solve a problem the outbox pattern already solves without it."

Do this instead: write the state change and the outgoing event in one database transaction (transactional-outbox), and relay the outbox to Kafka. You get at-least-once into Kafka, which is what consumers should assume anyway.

8.4 GDPR erasure on transactional topics.

What "delete" cannot mean. Kafka deletes by whole segments, never by record. There is no API that removes one record, and this is not an oversight — it is the append-only log that everything else depends on. Aborted records are in exactly the same position: they are on disk, replicated, and not going anywhere before their segment does. "Delete the merchant's data" is not an operation Kafka offers.

What it can mean, in increasing order of honesty:

  1. Wait for retention. Only defensible if retention is short and you can prove every copy — including tiered storage, mirrored clusters, and every downstream sink — expires on the same schedule. Usually you cannot.
  2. Compacted topic plus a tombstone. Works only if the topic is keyed by something merchant-scoped, and only eventually — compaction runs on closed segments past min.cleanable.dirty.ratio. Aborted records with the same key are cleaned the same way, but "eventually" is not a compliance answer on its own.
  3. Crypto-shredding. Encrypt each merchant's payload under a per-merchant key held outside Kafka; erasure is deleting the key. The bytes remain and become permanently unreadable. This is the standard answer, and it works uniformly for committed and aborted records because it never depended on deletion.
  4. Keep the personal data out of Kafka. Put a merchant id on the topic and the personal data in a database that supports DELETE. Erasure becomes a database operation and the log holds a dangling reference, which is fine.

Design: (4) where the schema allows it, (3) everywhere else, with (2) as housekeeping rather than as the compliance mechanism. And a rule going forward: personal data in an append-only log is a design decision, not a storage detail — decide it when the topic is created, because it cannot be undone afterwards.

8.5 Counterfactual — no last stable offset.

Smallest breaking situation. A consumer is served offset 810 the instant it is written. It acts on it — pays TeaHouse ₹1,960. Two seconds later the transaction aborts. The money is gone and no filter can help, because the consumer acted before the outcome existed.

What every consumer would have to implement. To be safe it would have to buffer every record from an open transaction until it learned the outcome, which means: maintaining a connection to coordinator state; holding unbounded memory for long-running transactions; persisting that buffer so a restart does not lose it or double-apply it; and reconciling with the coordinator after any disconnection. Every consumer, in every language, on every topic that has ever had a transactional producer. And the decision would be a distributed read on the consuming path, so the coordinator becomes a throughput dependency for every reader in the cluster.

What the LSO actually buys. It puts the decision inside the partition, so a reader needs no extra lookup, no coordinator connection, no memory, and no persistence — and it makes it structurally impossible to act early, because the data beyond the LSO is never sent. The cost is head-of-line blocking: one undecided record holds up everything behind it. That is the trade, and it is the same trade as §10.4 — uncertainty was moved to the reader, and then made cheap by putting the answer where the reader already is.

8.6 Counterfactual — no per-transaction epoch bump.

Smallest wrong result (this is the real pre-4.0 protocol):

T1, pid 4711 epoch 0: produce record R to partition P. The request stalls.
T1 aborts.               ← client-initiated abort, epoch NOT bumped
T2, pid 4711 epoch 0: produce record S to partition P.
R finally arrives: pid 4711, epoch 0, partition P has an open transaction
                   for pid 4711, epoch 0 → appended, joins T2.
T2 commits → one marker decides both S and R.

R was explicitly aborted and is now durably committed.

Why "reject requests older than N seconds" cannot be the fix. Three reasons, each sufficient:

  1. There is no trustworthy age on the request. A broker would have to believe a timestamp set by the client, on a different machine, with a different clock — and clock-synchronization is a lab about why that fails.
  2. Network delay has no bound you may assume. Any N is either too small (rejecting valid slow requests, turning a latency blip into data loss) or too large (leaving a window). This is the same reason the two-generals problem has no timing-based solution.
  3. It answers the wrong question. Age is a proxy. The actual question is which transaction does this record belong to, and until something in the record answers it, no amount of filtering on age can.

The correct fix is to make the record carry its own answer: bump the epoch on every commit and abort so that (pid, epoch) identifies a transaction rather than a producer. A late record then carries a stale epoch and is fenced by machinery that already existed and was already trusted — which is why KIP-890 phase 2 is a small change that closes the problem completely, rather than a heuristic that narrows it.
