Lab 32 · Why Distribution Is Hard

CRDTs — how two phones with no signal still hold the same list

Tell a friend: Amira is on the Tube and Ben is on a plane, and both add an item to the same shopping list while neither phone has signal. Most apps quietly destroy one of those two changes, because whichever phone syncs last replaces the whole list. A CRDT fixes that by changing what the phone stores and sends. Not "here is my list", but "here is every event I know about", combined by a rule that ignores order, grouping and repeats. Do that and the phones end up identical with no server, no leader and no vote. The price is that a list built this way can never refuse anything.

Play with it: visualization.html — Merge Without a Referee (https://claude.ai/code/artifact/fea9c00f-2004-4f4f-a867-7d6048751fdc).

1. Why can't the app just wait for a signal?

In one breath: Phones lose signal constantly. An app that freezes when the bars disappear gets deleted. So the app keeps working offline — and now two people can change the same thing without seeing each other.

Asha writes the shopping-list app. Her users are on phones. Phones go into tunnels, onto planes, and into buildings with thick walls.

People keep tapping anyway. An app that freezes the moment the bars vanish is an app they delete that evening.

So Asha does the obvious thing. The app accepts the change locally, keeps it on the phone, and sends it up when the signal returns.

That one decision creates this whole lab. Two people can now change the same shopping list, the same document, the same order, while neither can see what the other is doing. Somebody's change has to be dealt with.

Here is how almost every app deals with it. Whichever change reaches the server last replaces the other one.

Nobody wrote that rule down. It is simply what happens when nobody makes a decision. And it means real work that a real person did stops existing. There is no error message. There is no way for anyone to notice.

This lab is about the other road. Design the data so that both changes survive on their own, without asking anyone for permission.

2. What happens when Amira and Ben both edit the list?

In one breath: Two people add different things to one list while both are offline. They are not disagreeing about anything. There is an obviously right answer, and the app is about to get it wrong.

Amira is on the London Underground. Ben is on a plane to Berlin. Meera is at home on wifi, and her phone matters from section 7 onwards.

Amira and Ben share a shopping list in Asha's app. Right now both phones show the same thing:

bread

Amira, underground, adds milk. Ben, over the North Sea, adds eggs. Neither phone has signal, so both changes sit in a pocket for forty minutes.

Then they land. Both phones connect. Both send their list up.

Hold on to this: the two phones are not disagreeing about anything. Amira wants milk on the list. Ben wants eggs on the list. Those wishes are completely compatible.

There is a right answer, bread, milk, eggs, and everybody would accept it. The only reason the app might get it wrong is the way it stores and sends the list.

3. What naive solution does everyone write first?

In one breath: Each phone keeps the whole list, sends it up with the time, and the server keeps the newest one. It is simple, easy to test, and perfect for exactly one user.

Asha's first design is the one you would write too:

Each phone keeps the list. When you edit it, send the whole list up with the current time. The server keeps whichever list has the newest time.

There is nothing stupid about this. It is a naive solution in the precise sense of the word: correct for the case in front of you, and silent about the case that is coming.

One person, one phone, one list? Flawless. Two people, one of them in a tunnel? Read on.

4. What actually arrives, and who gets told?

In one breath: Two whole lists arrive. The server keeps the newer one. Milk stops existing, and because nothing failed, nothing is reported.

Amira's phone sends [bread, milk], stamped 14:32:07. Ben's phone sends [bread, eggs], stamped 14:32:09.

🔮 Predict: What does the list hold afterwards, and who is told about it?

Reveal

[bread, eggs]. Milk is gone. Nobody is told anything, because from the server's point of view nothing went wrong: it received two lists, it kept the newer one, exactly as designed.

If you guessed "the server notices that the two lists differ", look again at what it is holding. It has two complete lists and two clock readings. Nothing in either message says which items were decisions.

The deep problem is in the shape of the message. Ben's phone sent a complete list that happened not to contain milk. The server cannot tell the difference between "Ben deleted milk" and "Ben has never heard of milk" — because a list of items does not record which of them were decisions. The message threw away the information needed to combine the two.

Scene 1 of java src/Sim.java shows exactly this. Experiment 1 runs it at scale: over 1,000 rounds with 3 phones editing offline, users made 8,992 changes and 2,971 survived. 67% of all work was destroyed, and every single round "succeeded".

Two more things break the same way, and it is worth seeing that the break is the same one.

A retry duplicates work. Ben's phone never gets an acknowledgement, so it sends again. If the message had been add 1 to the counter, the counter is now 2. The transport is at-least-once — you met that in the transactional-outbox and communication-protocols labs. So this is not a rare case. It is Tuesday.

The clock decides. Ben won because his phone's clock said a bigger number. If Amira's phone were 60 seconds fast, Amira would win every argument for the next minute, regardless of who actually acted later. Experiment 8 runs this: a clock 60 seconds fast destroyed 1,000 of 1,000 genuinely-newer writes.

5. What does Asha really need, with no technology in it?

In one breath: Four plain sentences say what the app needs. Two of them look like a flat contradiction, and almost every system you have studied resolves it by quietly giving up the first one.

Write down the requirement in plain words, with no technology in it:

  1. Each machine can change its own copy immediately, with no permission from anyone. Otherwise the app freezes in the tunnel.
  2. Machines exchange information in any order, any number of times, with any amount of it lost along the way, and it must not matter.
  3. Once two machines have exchanged the same information, they must hold exactly the same thing — not "close enough", not "eventually if a repair job runs".
  4. No change that a person actually made may be silently thrown away.

🔮 Predict: Requirements 1 and 3 look contradictory. Which of the two do the systems you have already built give up, and what does that cost Amira in the tunnel?

Reveal

They give up requirement 1. A leader, a coordinator, a quorum or a lock is a referee who decides, and asking a referee means waiting for one.

You have built three of those: two-phase-commit, paxos and quorums-and-replication. You know what they cost: when the referee is unreachable, work stops. For Amira that is a frozen app forty metres under Oxford Circus.

If you guessed they give up requirement 3, that is the other well-known road — plain eventual consistency, with a repair job that picks a winner later. That road loses requirement 4 as well, which is exactly the 67% you just watched disappear.

This lab keeps requirement 1 and gets requirement 3 anyway.

6. What key idea makes both changes survive?

In one breath: Stop sending the answer. Send the evidence, and design the combining rule so that combining evidence is boring.

Two halves, both load-bearing.

Send evidence, not conclusions. Not "the list is [bread, eggs]", but "an event named ben#1 says eggs is on the list". Evidence from two phones is not in conflict. It is just more evidence.

Make the combining rule immune to the network. Design the rule so that combining in a different order gives the same result. So that combining in different groupings gives the same result. So that combining the same evidence twice changes nothing.

A network that reorders, regroups and duplicates then has nothing left to break.

Everything else in this lab is working out what that combining rule has to look like. For a counter, for a set, for a single field, and for a piece of text.

7. Which words do you now need, and what does each name?

In one breath: Five words carry the whole lab. Every one of them names something you have already watched happen to Amira and Ben, so nothing here is new. Only the labels are.

What is a replica, and why not just say "the data"?

Amira's phone holds a list. Ben's phone holds a list. The server holds a list. Three copies, all editable, all allowed to differ.

Engineers call each one a replica: an independently updatable copy. The word exists to stop you saying "the data". There is no "the data" here. There are only copies, and at any moment they may all be different.

At this exact moment Amira's replica holds [bread, milk], Ben's holds [bread, eggs], and the server's still holds [bread].

You used the same word in quorums-and-replication. The difference here is sharp. A replica in this lab may accept a write with no quorum, no coordinator and no permission at all.

What is a merge, and what is it forbidden to do?

The rule for combining two copies into one is called a merge. Written as code it is a pure function, merge(a, b): it takes two complete states, returns a state, and never changes either input.

The word exists to keep it apart from "apply", "write" and "resolve". A merge does not decide who was right.

It is not allowed to prefer the caller over the argument. It is not allowed to prefer the newer over the older. Unless that preference is written into the function itself, on purpose, and you can point at the line.

Everything interesting in this lab is a choice about what merge does. For the scoreboard you meet in section 8, merge({a:3}, {b:2}) = {a:3, b:2}.

It is the counterpart of the transform function in google-docs-system-design. Same job, reconciling two independent edits. Completely different strategy.

What three abuses must the merge survive?

Meera's phone has been offline all afternoon. When it reconnects, three things happen to it in the space of a second.

First it receives Amira's news and Ben's news, in whichever order the network felt like delivering them. Then a retry hands it Amira's news a second time. Then a relay delivers Ben's and Amira's news bundled together, rather than separately.

Three abuses. The network commits all three, every day. Take them one at a time.

The order did not matter. Meera combined Amira's news with Ben's, and she would have got the same list combining Ben's with Amira's. A rule with that property is commutative.

The grouping did not matter. Meera could combine Amira's and Ben's news first and then her own, or combine her own with Amira's first and then Ben's. Same list. A rule with that property is associative.

The repeat did not matter. The second copy of Amira's news changed nothing at all. A rule with that property is idempotent.

Now, and only now, the symbols. Write the combining rule as an operator, ⊔. Read a⊔b as "the copy you get by combining copy a with copy b".

Law In words In symbols The network fault it kills
Commutative order of combining doesn't matter a⊔b = b⊔a messages arriving out of order
Associative grouping doesn't matter (a⊔b)⊔c = a⊔(b⊔c) messages batched or relayed differently
Idempotent combining twice changes nothing a⊔a = a messages delivered more than once

Why exactly these three? Because they are exactly the abuses an unreliable network commits. Survive all three and the network has no move left.

That is not an analogy. Experiment 3 delivers seven pending messages in all 5,040 possible orders and gets 1 distinct final state.

🔮 Predict: Suppose the merge added two phones' counters together instead of taking the larger. Which of the three laws breaks, and what does the counter read after one duplicated message?

Reveal

Idempotence breaks, and only idempotence. Adding is still commutative and still associative, so a reordered or rebatched delivery is fine.

If you guessed commutativity, notice that 3 + 2 and 2 + 3 are equal. Addition was never the problem. Repetition was.

One duplicated message now invents a count that never happened. Experiment 2 delivers the same message ten times: the adding merge reads 11 where the true answer is 2.

What does "converge" promise, and why the word "strong"?

Everyone ends up holding the same thing. That is convergence, and the precise name for the promise in this lab is strong eventual consistency, or SEC.

Say it fully: any two replicas that have received the same set of updates are in the same state, immediately, with no further coordination.

Notice the word set, not sequence. The order the updates arrived in is not part of the promise, because it does not need to be.

Why "strong"? Plain eventual consistency, from cap-theorem, eventual-consistency and cqrs, only promises that things settle down somehow. Usually a background repair job resolves conflicts by picking a winner.

SEC is stronger and cheaper at the same time. Sameness is a property of the merge function itself. So there is no repair job, no conflict-resolution step, and no window in which two replicas that have seen the same updates disagree.

Be careful with one thing. Converging is not the same as being right. Section 10 shows you a design where all three phones converge perfectly onto an answer that is wrong.

What is a CRDT, and what does the name promise?

A data type built so that copies of it can be merged without a referee. Its full name is conflict-free replicated data type: a type whose merge is commutative, associative and idempotent, so replicas reach strong eventual consistency with no coordination.

The term exists to name a shift. Not resolving conflicts — not having them. In a CRDT there is no conflict to resolve, because two concurrent updates are, by construction, both representable in the merged state.

The name is a promise about where the work went. It went into the design of the type, so it does not have to happen at runtime.

There are two families, both called CRDTs. State-based ones, also called CvRDT for "convergent", ship whole states and merge them. Op-based ones, also called CmRDT for "commutative", ship individual operations.

This lab builds state-based ones, because they demand nothing at all from the network. Section 11 shows exactly what op-based ones demand instead.

8. Which mental models should you carry, and where do they lie?

In one breath: Two mental models of the same machine. A wall scoreboard that nobody may rub out, and a hill you can only climb. The first one gets you a long way, and then it lies to you in three specific places.

The simple model — what if it is a scoreboard nobody can rub out?

Asha pins a scoreboard on the wall, with one row for Amira, one for Ben and one for Meera. Three rules, and that is the whole thing:

  1. You may only ever raise your own row.
  2. To combine two scoreboards, take the higher number in each row.
  3. The score is the sum of all rows.

Amira and Ben raising their own rows in a tunnel cannot destroy each other's work, because they are writing in different rows. Overwriting somebody else's row is not an action the rules allow.

Combining is taking the larger number in each row. Taking the larger of a row you already hold changes nothing. So a scoreboard you have already copied can be copied again, ten times, in any order, harmlessly.

The engineering model — what is a lattice you can only climb?

Now the same picture in the language of the papers.

A replica's state is a point in a join-semilattice. That is a set of states with a partial order. The order is written ≤ and read here as "is at least as old as". In such a set, any two states have a least upper bound: the smallest state sitting at or above both of them.

The merge is that least upper bound. It is written ⊔ and pronounced "join".

Read the arrows below as "climbs to".

Amira and Ben go their own way, so they climb into different branches. Merging is finding the lowest point above both of them.

There is always exactly one such point. And a local update also only ever climbs, a property called monotonicity. Together those two facts mean replicas can never reach a state from which they cannot rejoin.

That single picture explains the three laws rather than assuming them. A least upper bound is symmetric in its arguments, which is commutativity. The least upper bound of three elements is the same however you bracket it, which is associativity. The least upper bound of a thing with itself is the thing, which is idempotence.

Where does the scoreboard lie to you?

The analogy breaks in three places, and each break is a whole section later on.

You cannot lower a row. Ben cannot subtract by writing a smaller number, because merge would immediately restore the bigger one. The scoreboard has no way to go down at all. Section 10 shows the actual fix.

Rows are for counting, not for identity. Amira's shopping list is not a number per person. Sets need a different construction, and deletion in a set is the hardest thing in this lab.

The board grows forever. Real CRDTs accumulate evidence. One row per replica that ever touched the data, one record per deletion, and nothing in the algorithm ever cleans any of it up. Experiment 7 counts it: a shopping cart with 10 visible items was storing 500 records, 50 per item.

10. How does the mechanism grow, one broken design at a time?

In one breath: Nine designs. Each one is what a careful engineer builds next, given what just broke. Every strange detail in the final design is the scar of one specific failure.

Nothing below is presented as finished. Asha builds v1, watches it fail, and builds v2. You are watching her think.

v1 — what if a counter is just a number?

Amira and Ben share a counter. Both phones show 5. Amira taps +1, Ben taps +1. Amira's copy says 6, Ben's says 6. Merge takes the larger: 6.

🔮 Predict: Two people each added one. What should the counter read, and what does it read?

Reveal

It should read 7. It reads 6. One increment is gone.

If you guessed the merge is at fault, look one level down. The bug is in the state. A single number cannot record who did what. So two different histories, 5+1 and 5+1+1, produce the same number and become indistinguishable.

The state has thrown away the information the merge needs. That sentence is the whole design method for the rest of this lab: when a merge cannot do its job, add to the state, don't add to the merge.

v2 — what if everybody gets their own row?

Keep a row per replica. You may only raise your own row. Merge takes the higher number in each row. The value is the sum of the rows.

Amira:  {amira: 1}                Ben: {ben: 1}
merge:  {amira: 1, ben: 1}   -> value 2   ✔

Amira and Ben are writing in different rows, so they cannot overwrite each other. Overwriting is not a thing you can do to somebody else's row. That is GCounter.java, and the merge is one line:

other.rows.forEach((replica, count) -> next.merge(replica, count, Math::max));

Duplicates become harmless for free, because max(1,1) = 1. Run java src/Experiments.java 2: the same message delivered ten times still reads 2, where an adding merge reads 11.

The name is G-Counter, G for grow-only. Which is also its problem.

🔮 Predict: Ben wants to subtract 3. His row says 4, so he writes 1 into it and syncs with Amira, whose copy still says {ben: 4}. What does the counter read afterwards?

Reveal

{ben: 4} — the decrement is gone. Merge takes the larger number, so a smaller number looks exactly like older news, and older news is what merge exists to discard.

If you guessed the sync would carry the 1 across, remember what merge is: a least upper bound. It climbs. It has no way to descend.

Run java src/Experiments.java 4 for the printed before and after. The general rule this teaches: in this family, the state may only ever climb. Any operation that moves it down is not implementable as written. It must be re-expressed as something that moves it up.

v3 — how do you subtract when nothing may go down?

Re-express the decrement. Keep two grow-only counters. P holds everything ever added. N holds everything ever taken away. The value is sum(P) − sum(N).

Both halves still only climb, so both halves still merge by taking the larger number in each row.

P{amira: 6, ben: 4}   N{ben: 3}   ->   10 − 3 = 7   ✔

That is PNCounter.java, eleven lines of real code. One pattern recurs everywhere in this family: if the operation goes the wrong way, add a second monotonic pile to hold it.

v4 — what if the thing is a list, not a number?

Amira's shopping list is not a number. So try the simplest possible design: a set, and merge is union.

Union is commutative, associative and idempotent for free. This converges with no machinery at all. That is GSet.java.

It cannot remove anything. Removing means the set gets smaller, and smaller is down the lattice.

v5 — can a second pile hold the removals?

Same trick as the PN-Counter. Add a second grow-only pile. One set of things added, one set of things removed, and the list you see is adds minus removes.

🔮 Predict: Amira removes milk, changes her mind in the dairy aisle, and adds milk again. Is milk on the list?

Reveal

No — and it never can be again. The removes pile contains the word "milk". The new add lands in the adds pile, where the removes pile cancels it, forever.

If you guessed the second add would win because it came later, notice that neither pile records when. They record whether.

Experiment 5 runs 100 add-remove-add cycles, with java src/Experiments.java 5. This design has the item in 0 of 100 carts. Try it yourself in the visualization under Adds plus a blacklist.

Now notice what kind of failure this is. All three phones agree completely. The merge obeys all three laws. It converges beautifully — onto the wrong answer. Convergence is a property of the merge; correctness is a property of the design. Nothing in the algebra will ever warn you about this.

The diagnosis is the same one as v1. The state threw away information. "Milk was removed" is not enough. Asha needs to know which milk.

The name is 2P-Set, for two-phase: a thing can be added, then removed, and that is the end of its life.

v6 — what if a remove tears up receipts instead of words?

This is the central idea of the lab, and it is one sentence:

An add is not a value. It is a unique event — and a remove destroys events, never values.

Every add mints a dot: a globally unique name for one event, made with no coordination at all. A dot is (which replica, how many things that replica has done). So amira#3 is the 3rd action ever taken on Amira's phone, and no machine anywhere can accidentally produce that name.

That is Dot.java. It is the same idea as the site-id tie-break in google-docs-system-design, and it is one entry of a version vector from quorums-and-replication.

The state becomes two piles, and the visualization's dashed box shows exactly these:

live:  milk -> {amira#3}      the adds still standing, with their receipts
torn:  {amira#1, ben#2}       tombstones: receipts that have been destroyed

A tombstone is a record that something was deleted. It is kept because "absent" and "deleted" have to be told apart. You met the word for deleted rows in btree-and-lsm and quorums-and-replication; here it names a dead receipt rather than a dead row.

Removing milk does not blacklist the word "milk". It tears up exactly the receipts for milk that this replica has actually seen. Which is why the type is called an observed-remove set, or OR-Set. In words: the merge keeps every receipt either side has torn up, and keeps every standing add that no torn receipt names.

torn = torn(a) ∪ torn(b)
live = (live(a) ∪ live(b)) minus every receipt in torn

Re-adding now works. Amira's second add mints amira#9, and no torn receipt names amira#9. In the same experiment that gave the 2P-Set 0 of 100, this gets 100 of 100 carts.

🔮 Predict: Both phones hold milk with receipt amira#1. The network is cut. Ben deletes milk; at the same moment Amira, in the dairy aisle, adds milk again. They sync. Is milk on the list — and does the answer depend on which message arrives first?

Reveal

Milk stays, and no, the order makes no difference.

Ben's delete names one receipt: amira#1. Amira's new add mints amira#4, which Ben has never seen and therefore cannot have meant to destroy. The merge tears up amira#1 and leaves amira#4 standing.

If you guessed "it depends who syncs last", that was true of every design before this one. It stopped being true the moment removes started naming receipts instead of words.

v7 — what about a field that must hold exactly one value?

A counter merges by adding evidence. A set merges by unioning it. But what about a field that is supposed to hold exactly one value — a delivery address, a display name?

LWW-Register (LwwRegister.java) keeps the value, plus a timestamp, plus the replica id. Merge keeps the bigger (timestamp, replicaId) pair.

It is a perfectly legal CRDT. All three laws hold. And the tie-break on replica id is load-bearing, not decoration.

🔮 Predict: Amira and Ben write different addresses in the same millisecond, and the tie-break on replica id has been deleted so ties keep whichever copy merge was called on. What goes wrong, and how would you ever notice?

Reveal

Commutativity breaks. Amira merging Ben's copy keeps her own value; Ben merging Amira's copy keeps his. The two phones now disagree permanently, and no further sync repairs it, because each one keeps winning its own merge.

If you guessed "one of them wins, which is unfair but consistent", that is the answer with the tie-break in place. Without it there is no winner at all, only two replicas that will never agree again.

You would notice almost never in testing, because same-millisecond writes are rare on a quiet machine and constant on a busy fleet. Run java src/Tests.java broken, which plants exactly this bug and catches it.

The LWW-Register converges by throwing one of the two writes in the bin, and your wall clock picks which one. Experiment 8 makes that concrete: a replica whose clock is 60 seconds fast destroyed 1,000 of 1,000 genuinely-newer writes, silently.

MV-Register (MvRegister.java) refuses to pick. It keeps every candidate that nothing else supersedes, and hands the set to whoever reads it.

Each candidate carries the version vector it was written against. That is the same structure you used in quorums-and-replication and, not coincidentally, the same lattice as a G-Counter.

A write joins every vector it can see and bumps its own entry. That is how it earns the right to replace what it saw.

Merge drops any candidate strictly dominated by another. A dominated candidate is not a rival opinion. It is an ancestor whose author has already been overruled by somebody who read it.

Two survivors are exactly what Riak and Dynamo call siblings. The trade is plain. LWW never bothers the application and sometimes loses data. MV never loses data and sometimes hands Asha's application a decision it must be written to handle.

v8 — how do you merge order itself?

Text is a set with a position, and position is exactly the thing that stops being true the moment somebody else edits.

"Insert at index 3" was correct when Amira wrote it and wrong when it ran. That is the failure the google-docs-system-design lab spent its whole length on.

RGA stands for replicated growable array, and it lives in Rga.java. It applies the OR-Set trick to order. Never name a position; name an identity.

Every character gets a dot. An insert says "this character goes immediately after that character". Two concurrent inserts after the same character are ordered by their dots, bigger first — a rule both phones can apply without talking. Deletes are tombstones, so a delete may safely arrive before the character it deletes.

Merge is union of characters plus union of tombstones, so the three laws hold for free.

One catch bit this lab during construction, and it is worth carrying. Union-merge makes RGA converge whatever dot numbers you use. But it only produces the order a human expected if the dot counters are Lamport counters — every new dot numbered higher than every dot its author has seen.

With plain per-replica 1, 2, 3… all replicas still agree. They just agree on HATSC instead of CHATS. Convergence and intention preservation are two different properties, and only one of them is free.

v9 — why not ship the operation instead of the whole state?

Everything so far ships a whole snapshot on every sync. For a 10,000-item set that is absurd.

The obvious economy is to ship the operation instead of the state: "add milk with receipt amira#3". That is the op-based family, and it is what Automerge, Yjs and most collaborative editors actually do.

🔮 Predict: An op-based set sends two operations: add milk (amira#1) and then remove milk (amira#1). Meera receives them in that order; Ben receives them backwards. Do the two replicas end up the same?

Reveal

No. Experiment 9 prints it: add-then-remove gives [], remove-then-add gives [milk]. Ben's remove arrived when there was nothing to remove, did nothing, and then the add landed with nothing left to cancel it.

If you guessed yes because "the operations commute", they do not. A remove is only meaningful once its add has landed.

Op-based CRDTs need causal delivery: an operation may not be applied before the operations it depends on. Somebody has to build that layer: sequence numbers, buffering, exactly-once. It is not free. This is the same bug the google-docs lab hit in its own CRDT: a delete arriving before its character was silently lost, in 428 of 500 runs.

The state-based version has no such requirement, because it never applies a bare "remove". It merges a whole snapshot with the tombstones included. Same experiment: both orders give [].

δ-state CRDTs are the modern middle. State-based semantics, but a sync ships only the part of the lattice that changed. That part is called a "delta", and it merges by exactly the same rule. You get op-based bandwidth with state-based network requirements.

Riak, Akka and most current research systems are delta-based. This lab ships full states because they make the algebra visible. Deltas are an optimisation on top, not a different idea.

1 · The same message is delivered five times. The transport retries, so Meera's phone receives Amira's news five times. Nothing happens. 6 · One machine's clock is wrong. Under an LWW-Register, Ben's fast phone wins every argument: 1,000 of 1,000 genuinely-newer writes destroyed. The wall clock has become the conflict-resolution algorithm. Everyone still agrees — on the wrong value. What is lost is the newer write, with no error, no log line and no metric for Priya to alert on. In production you have two honest choices. Accept it explicitly for data where losing a write is fine, such as a "last seen at" field. Or use an MV-Register and write the application code that handles siblings. NTP skew is not a hypothetical. It is the normal state of a fleet. Cause it with java src/Experiments.java 8, or the clock accurate / +60s fast toggle.

7 · The data grows forever. Amira's cart shows 10 items and stores 500 records — 50 per item, 250 of them tombstones. A 206-character document stores 403 cells. Every add is remembered as an event, every delete is remembered as a tombstone, and nothing in the algorithm ever frees either. Correctness is untouched. Nothing logical is lost. This is a cost, not a bug — and it is the single biggest operational concern with CRDTs. Freeing a tombstone requires knowing that every replica has seen the delete, a condition called causal stability, which is itself a distributed agreement problem. So real systems either run a stability tracker, or bound the number of replicas, or redesign the representation so version vectors replace most per-element tombstones. That last route is Riak's ORSWOT and Automerge's compaction. Cause it with java src/Experiments.java 7.

8 · Operations arrive before the operations they depend on. An op-based set diverges permanently: [] on Meera's phone, [milk] on Ben's. The remove had nothing to remove yet, so it did nothing, and the add later landed uncancelled. Nothing is intact here. What is lost is strong eventual consistency itself. In production, op-based systems ship a causal delivery layer, plus exactly-once delivery on top. That layer is vector clocks plus a buffer for operations whose dependencies have not arrived. If you adopt an op-based library, that layer is part of what you are adopting. Check it exists before you trust it. Cause it with java src/Experiments.java 9.

9 · A replica moves down the lattice. Somebody restores Ben's phone from an old backup, or a bad deploy resets a row. The invariant "state only ever climbs" is gone, and merges start producing different answers depending on direction. The join is only well defined over a lattice you climb. A state that goes backwards can be re-introduced by a peer and then removed again, forever. Nothing reliable is intact; convergence is lost. In production, restoring a CRDT replica from an old backup is not a neutral act. It is a non-monotonic move, and the safe procedure is to bootstrap the replica empty and let it re-merge, exactly as in failure 5. 12. What does correctness mean here, and where is the edge?

In one breath: A CRDT promises that everyone ends up agreeing. It promises nothing at all about whether what they agree on was ever allowed. Knowing exactly where that line falls is the whole professional skill.

What is actually guaranteed?

  • Strong eventual consistency. Any two replicas that have received the same set of updates hold identical state. Not "eventually", not "after a repair job" — immediately, as a property of the merge function.
  • No lost updates in the type's own terms. Every update that reaches a replica is represented in the merged state. A G-Counter loses no increments. An OR-Set loses no add that was not observed-and-removed.
  • Total availability for writes. Any replica accepts any update at any time, including alone in a tunnel. There is no quorum to gather and no leader to find.
  • Immunity to the network. Loss, duplication, reordering, regrouping and partition cost latency, never correctness — for state-based types.
  • Monotonic progress. Replica state only climbs the lattice, so replicas can never reach a state from which they cannot rejoin.

What is explicitly not guaranteed?

🔮 Predict: Arjun has 10 bags of flour and Dev has the same 10 bags in his copy. Could a cleverer CRDT, or any CRDT at all, stop the two of them selling 12 bags between them while the link is down?

Reveal

No. Not a cleverer one, not any one. Two replicas that cannot see each other cannot both check a rule about the total. Accepting a write with no coordination is giving up the ability to refuse it.

If you guessed "an OR-Set of reservations would fix it", it would not. It would record all 12 reservations perfectly and converge on all 12. Recording is not refusing.

The measured result: −2 bags of flour.

  • No global invariant. stock ≥ 0, balance ≥ 0, "this username is unique", "at most 5 members" — none of these can be enforced. This is not a weakness of a particular type. It follows directly from accepting writes without coordination, and it is the reason CRDTs and consensus coexist rather than compete.
  • No linearizability, no total order. There is no "the order in which things happened". Concurrent updates are genuinely unordered, and read-your-own-writes holds only on your own replica.
  • No atomicity across objects. Two CRDTs merge independently. There is no transaction spanning them, so Meera can see one merged and the other not.
  • No semantic conflict resolution. The type decides mechanically, by its own rule — add-wins, last-writer-wins, keep-both. Whether that rule matches what a human would have wanted is your problem, not the algorithm's. A CRDT guarantees that everyone agrees. It does not guarantee that what they agree on is sensible.
  • No free garbage collection. Metadata grows with the number of operations, not the size of the data.

What was traded away to get it?

Coordination — and with it, the ability to say no.

A referee-based system can reject a write. "Insufficient stock." "Name already taken." That rejection is precisely what a CRDT gives up in exchange for working in Amira's tunnel.

15. What does a real production system add?

In one breath: Four things, in order of how much they matter: ship less on each sync, store less per element, put it on disk, and work out when it is safe to throw metadata away.

What is the chain from this lab to a running system?

This lab is a single JVM, in memory, full-state syncs, a hand-driven network, one CRDT at a time. It exists to make the algebra visible.

A real implementation adds four things, in roughly this order of importance.

First, deltas. Nobody ships a 10,000-element set on every sync. δ-state CRDTs ship only the part of the lattice that changed and merge it with the same join. Riak, Akka Distributed Data and most current systems are delta-based.

Second, a smaller representation. The OR-Set in this lab keeps one tombstone per deleted element forever. Riak's ORSWOT stands for observed-remove set without tombstones. It replaces most of them with a version vector per replica, which is O(replicas) instead of O(deletes-ever). Automerge and Yjs do the equivalent for sequences, plus run-length encoding of adjacent characters.

Third, persistence and compaction. State on disk, snapshot plus subsequent deltas, periodic rewrite. That is structurally the same problem as btree-and-lsm compaction and event-sourcing snapshots.

Fourth, garbage collection with a stability rule. A tombstone may only be dropped once every replica has certainly seen the delete. Tracking that is called causal stability, and it needs membership and liveness. That is what gossip-protocols provides.

A production architecture looks like this. A set of replicas discovered and health-checked by gossip. Anti-entropy sync between them on a timer, gossip again. Client requests routed to any replica, often the nearest, sometimes sticky so Amira can read her own writes. And a completely separate path for the small number of operations that need to be able to say no: a quorum write, a leader, a database transaction.

Commonly confused with — one sentence each.

CRDT versus operational transformation. Both make concurrent edits converge. OT rewrites an operation against the ones it did not know about, and needs a server to fix the official order. A CRDT changes the data so no rewriting is needed and no server is required. OT is cheaper on the wire and in memory, which is why a product that already has a central server picks it — the google-docs-system-design lab built both.

CRDT versus eventual consistency. Eventual consistency says replicas settle down somehow, usually via a conflict-resolution step. A CRDT says replicas that have seen the same updates are already identical, because sameness is a property of the merge. Start at eventual-consistency if that distinction is still fuzzy.

CRDT versus a version vector. A version vector detects concurrency and hands you siblings. A CRDT resolves it by construction. A version vector is a component inside several CRDTs, not an alternative to them.

CRDT versus consensus. Consensus picks one value and can therefore refuse a write. A CRDT keeps all values and can never refuse. Systems need both, for different data.

CRDT versus "last write wins". LWW is a CRDT, the simplest one, not the opposite of one. The interesting question is never "CRDT or not". It is "which merge rule, and what does it throw away".

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Your phone doesn't send "I added milk"; it sends the whole list, and the list it sends happens not to contain the item your colleague added while you were both offline. 1.2 Store the evidence rather than the conclusion, and make the rule for combining evidence one that doesn't care about order, grouping or repetition. Then two machines that have seen the same events are automatically holding the same thing, without anyone having to decide who was right.

1.3 Wrong verb: CRDTs don't resolve conflicts, they make the state unable to conflict. Two concurrent updates are both representable in the merged state by construction, so there is no resolution step at runtime — the work was done at design time, in the shape of the data.

1.4 Idempotence (a⊔a = a) is what makes a re-delivered message a no-op, and at-least-once transports re-deliver by definition. @Override public YourMerge merge(YourMerge other) {
    TreeSet<Dot> nextTorn = new TreeSet<>(torn);
    nextTorn.addAll(other.torn);                       // 1. learn ALL tombstones first

    TreeMap<String, SortedSet<Dot>> nextLive = copy(live);
    other.live.forEach((item, dots) ->                 // 2. union the live receipts
        nextLive.computeIfAbsent(item, k -> new TreeSet<>()).addAll(dots));

    nextLive.values().forEach(d -> d.removeAll(nextTorn));   // 3. THEN subtract
    nextLive.values().removeIf(Set::isEmpty);
    return new YourMerge(nextLive, nextTorn);
}

Rung 7 — Worked design discussions

7.1 Tombstone garbage collection

The fact that must be true: every replica that will ever merge this state has already seen the tombstone. That is causal stability. If it holds, the tombstone can never again be needed — there is no surviving copy of the add for it to cancel.

The mechanism: each replica publishes a version vector of what it has seen; a replica takes the pointwise minimum across all members ("the stable frontier") and may free any tombstone whose dot is below it. Membership and liveness come from gossip (gossip-protocols); the frontier is a lattice too, so it converges by the same rules.

Dropping one too early: the add it was cancelling gets re-delivered by a replica that never heard about the delete, and the deleted item comes back, permanently. This is the single worst CRDT failure in production because it looks like data corruption and cannot be undone by retrying.

A permanently unreachable replica: the minimum stops advancing and GC stalls forever. Every real system therefore has an eviction policy: after a timeout, a silent replica is removed from the membership and must re-bootstrap from scratch if it returns. That eviction is a coordination decision — note that even a coordination-free data type needs a coordinated membership service around it.

The check:

boolean safeToDrop(Dot d, List<VersionVector> whatEveryoneHasSeen) {
    return whatEveryoneHasSeen.stream()
        .allMatch(vv -> vv.entries().getOrDefault(d.replica(), 0L) >= d.counter());
}

The subtlety worth naming: this is safe only if the list really is everyone. An incomplete membership list turns a correct-looking check into the too-early-drop failure above.

7.2 Restore from an old backup

A wiped replica is safe (experiment 10: recovers to 60 in one sync) because empty is the bottom of the lattice — merging can only climb out of it.

An older non-empty state is only safe for a type whose merge cannot be confused by it. GCounter survives: max(old, new) = new. OrSet survives: unioning old receipts back in is harmless, because any that were tombstoned are subtracted again by tombstones the peers still hold.

Where it breaks is any type whose state is not purely accumulative — including, critically, a garbage-collected OR-Set. Restore a replica from before a GC run, and it re-introduces adds whose tombstones have since been freed. The item returns from the dead, everywhere, permanently. Same mechanism as 7.1's too-early drop, arriving by a different door.

The operational rule: never restore a CRDT replica from a backup into a live cluster. Bootstrap it empty and let it re-merge. A backup is for total-cluster-loss recovery only.

7.3 Sibling explosion

The number of siblings grows with concurrent writes that did not read each other. The client-side fix is read-before-write: every write carries the causal context it read, which lets it dominate and collapse those candidates. That is not an optimisation, it is how a write earns its place in the order — the quorums-and-replication lab hit the exact opposite bug, where writing with an empty context made every write's vector identical and the store ignored them all.

The read-side behaviour that makes it worse: a client that reads, sees siblings, picks one to display, and then writes back without merging the others. Each such write looks concurrent to something, so the sibling set never shrinks. Cap the sibling count, alert on the 99th percentile, and treat "value with 500 siblings" as a code bug in the client, not a storage problem.

Rung 8 — Worked design discussions

8.1 Offline-first cart for a payments product

CRDTs:

  • The cart — an OR-Set of line items, keyed by product id, add-wins. Justification: an item that reappears is a support email; an item that cannot be deleted is a chargeback.
  • Quantity per line — a PN-Counter per item, not an LWW integer, so "+1 on the phone" and "+2 on the tablet" both survive.
  • Saved-for-later, wish list, recently viewed — OR-Sets. Nothing here has a cross-replica rule.
  • UI preferences, address, delivery note — LWW-Registers. Losing a concurrent edit to a delivery note is acceptable; say so out loud in the design doc rather than letting it be an accident.

Not CRDTs, and what they use instead:

  • Stock / inventory — a single-writer or consensus-backed store. Experiment in Sim.java scene 5: two partitioned warehouses each sell 6 of 10 bags and the merged stock is −2. A PN-Counter cannot enforce stock ≥ 0, ever.
  • Payment capture — one authoritative ledger, idempotency keys, exactly-once semantics. An append-only ledger has no conflicts to resolve, which is why event-sourcing's expected-version check is the right tool: it refuses the conflicting write rather than preserving it.
  • Price — server-authoritative, read-only on the client. A merged price is a fraud vector.
  • Coupon "one use per customer" — a uniqueness invariant. Consensus, or a conditional write on a single-partition store.

The boundary: the cart is a proposal; checkout is a decision. Everything before the tap of "Pay" is a CRDT and syncs freely; the tap crosses into the coordinated world, where the server re-prices, re-checks stock, and can say no. What crosses the boundary is a cart snapshot plus an idempotency key.

What the user sees on disagreement: items merge silently (both survive, and that is the desirable outcome). Quantities merge silently. If checkout fails because stock ran out while they were offline, they see an explicit "1 of these is no longer available" screen at checkout — never a silently altered cart, because a cart that changes itself destroys trust in the number next to the payment button.

Three launch metrics: (1) cart metadata size, p99 per user — the tombstone tax, which is what will page you first; (2) sync lag between regions, p99 — the disagreement window, and the number that explains every "my phone and my laptop disagree" ticket; (3) checkout stock-rejection rate — the rate at which the offline promise is meeting the coordinated world, and the leading indicator that your CRDT/consensus boundary is drawn in the wrong place.

What would get you fired: making stock, price, or payment a CRDT. Those need the ability to say no, and a CRDT structurally cannot.

8.2 CRDT or OT for a document editor

Offline for a week decides it. OT requires each edit to be transformed against the operations it was written without knowing about, and the cost is roughly (edits missed × edits made) — the google-docs-system-design lab measured 8,200 transforms for a client that missed 200 edits while making 20. A week offline makes that unbounded, which is why real OT products tell the client "your revision is too old" and force a reload — unacceptable when the whole promise was working on a plane. OT also needs one server per document deciding the official order, so the laptop cannot merge with a colleague's laptop directly. A CRDT needs neither, at the cost of metadata: this lab measured 2.0 cells per visible character after light editing, and the google-docs lab measured 1,000 cells for 100 visible characters under heavy churn. Accept the memory and the compaction work; take Automerge or Yjs rather than writing your own, because the parts you would get wrong (causal delivery, GC, encoding) are the parts they have already got right.

8.3 The CRDT you should not build

Why it cannot work. A seat reservation is a uniqueness invariant: seat 14C may be held by at most one person. Enforcing that requires being able to refuse a booking, and refusal requires knowing what everyone else has done — which is exactly the coordination an offline-capable data type gives up. Any CRDT you build will faithfully record that two people booked 14C and converge on a state where both did, the same way Sim.java converges on −2 bags of flour. The merge is not wrong; there was never a rule for it to check.

What to build instead. Keep the offline experience for everything that is not the invariant: browsing, seat maps, party size, preferences, and a held intent ("I want 14C") that syncs as an OR-Set entry and is explicitly labelled as not yet confirmed. Confirmation is a single coordinated write against the authoritative seat map — a conditional write or a short lock — with an idempotency key so a retry over a bad connection cannot double-book. The user sees "requested" and then "confirmed", which is honest, and on the rare loss they see "14C was taken; here are two seats next to each other". The general rule: let the CRDT carry the intent, and let consensus carry the commitment.
