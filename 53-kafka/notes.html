Lab 53 · Work That Spans Services

Apache Kafka — stop deleting the messages, and everything else follows

Tell a friend: In the town of Deodar, Arjun runs a parcel courier. Every scan of a parcel is one small fact, and four teams want that fact for four different reasons. The usual answer is a post office that hands each message to one person and then destroys it, so the second team never sees it. Kafka's answer is to stop destroying anything: write each fact once in a ledger that only grows, and let every reader keep its own bookmark. Once nobody deletes, replaying last year is free and a hundred audiences cost almost nothing, and the only price is that order now lives inside one ledger rather than across the whole town.

Keep visualization.html open beside this page; §9.5 maps its four tabs onto the sections. §17 says what this lab is made of.

1. Why does one fact end up wanted by people you have not met?

In one breath: Something true happens once. Several teams care about it, and some of those teams do not exist yet. Every older way of telling them made the teller keep the list of who cares, and that list changes far more often than the facts do.

Arjun's courier company scans a parcel. That scan is one fact. It is true from the moment it happens, whether or not anybody is told.

Now ask who wants it. Billing wants it. Notifications wants it. Analytics wants it. Fraud wants it. And in eight months, a team that does not exist today will want it too.

Every pre-Kafka answer assumed the opposite. It assumed the sender knows the receivers. An HTTP call knows its callee. A queue knows its consumer. A database write knows its table. All three make the producer of the fact responsible for the list of people who care about it.

That assumption fails in a specific, expensive way. The list changes far more often than the fact does. And every change to the list is a change to the code that emits the fact.

Six months in, the function that records "parcel scanned" is 400 lines long and calls nine services. It is the most dangerous file in the company. Not because scanning parcels got harder. Because being interesting got harder.

Kafka's answer is one sentence, and everything else in this lab is a consequence of it:

Write the fact down once, in order, keep it, and let each audience read at its own speed and its own position.

That sounds trivially obvious. It is not. It costs you the one thing every previous messaging system considered its core service: the broker knowing who has handled what. In exchange it buys three things those systems structurally cannot offer. Replay. A brand-new consumer reading history. A hundred consumers at zero extra write cost.

2. Who wants a parcel scan in Deodar, and can one rule serve them all?

In one breath: Four teams want the same scan for four incompatible reasons. They disagree about how fast, about how far back, and about what a mistake costs. No single delivery policy is right for all four.

You already know the town of Deodar. You partitioned its library two labs ago, and its noticeboards taught you eventual consistency. Arjun runs Deodar Dispatch, a parcel courier. A parcel is scanned at each step of its journey:

parcel P-1001   09:02  PICKED_UP        (courier Nila, Cedar Street)
parcel P-1001   09:41  AT_HUB           (hub 3)
parcel P-1001   11:15  OUT_FOR_DELIVERY (courier Vikram)
parcel P-1001   12:30  DELIVERED        (signed: R. Sharma)

At peak, Deodar's scanners produce roughly 4,000 scans per second. Four teams want them, and each team is one person you will meet again in every later section of this lab.

Priya runs billing. She only cares about DELIVERED, because that is when Deodar may invoice. Sameer runs notifications; he texts the customer on every scan. Devi runs analytics; she models hub throughput and wants every scan ever recorded. Kabir runs fraud; he watches for routes that are physically impossible.

And there is a fifth team that does not exist yet. In eight months Asha will be asked to build a delivery-time prediction model, and her first question will be: "can I have last year's scans?"

3. What if the dispatch office simply phones everybody?

In one breath: The scanner calls all four teams itself. It is the first thing anyone writes, it is genuinely reasonable, and it makes the scanner the least reliable thing in the building.

This is the obvious solution number one. When a scan arrives, Arjun's scan service calls each team's HTTP endpoint:

scan arrives → POST /billing/scan
             → POST /notifications/scan
             → POST /analytics/scan
             → POST /fraud/scan
             → return 200 to the scanner

Do not sneer at it. It is simple. It is synchronous, so you know it worked. It needs no new infrastructure. Plenty of companies run it happily for years.

It then dies of three well-understood diseases, all of which you met in the microservices lab, so we will move fast. The arithmetic is the interesting part, so read the middle column.

What breaks The arithmetic What Arjun sees
Availability multiplies downwards four teams at 99.9% each give the scanner 0.999⁴ ≈ 99.6% the scan endpoint is up only when all four teams are up; the scanner became the least reliable thing in the building by depending on everyone
Latency adds the courier waits for the slowest of four calls, and queueing-theory says the busiest team contributes its tail, not its average the courier's phone hangs on the worst call, not the typical one
Every new audience edits the producer team five costs one code change, one review, one deploy the deploy is of the most critical service in the company

There is a subtler fourth problem, and it is the one that has no good answer.

Suppose billing succeeds and notifications times out. What now? Retry only notifications, and you must now track per-callee state. Fail the whole scan, and you are pretending a parcel was not scanned when it physically was. Return 200 and drop it, and you have silent data loss.

None of the three is right, because you are trying to make a fact about the physical world transactional with four network calls. That is the dual-write problem from transactional-outbox, wearing a different hat.

🔮 Predict: Arjun buys a second copy of every team's service, so no team is ever fully down. Does that fix the fourth problem?

Reveal No. Redundancy raises each team's availability, which helps the first disease and nothing else. The fourth problem is not about anyone being down. It is about a *partial* success: one call landed, another did not, and there is no way to undo the one that landed. If you said "add retries", ask what happens when the retry succeeds after the scan service has already returned 200 — you now have a duplicate, which is Priya's double-charge. Availability is not the disease here. Atomicity is.

4. What if a proper message broker sits in the middle?

In one breath: Arjun puts a pigeonhole board between the scanner and the teams. The scanner drops a note in; the board hands it to a team; the team says "done"; the board destroys the note. That last step is the whole story.

So Arjun does the correct next thing. He puts a message broker in the middle, the classic kind that had existed for twenty years before Kafka. Picture it as a pigeonhole board in the dispatch office.

The model is the one almost everyone means by the word "queue":

  • the scanner drops a message into the board and is done, so the scanner is decoupled from the teams, which fixes every disease in §3;
  • the board holds the message and hands it to a consumer;
  • the consumer acknowledges it, and the board deletes it;
  • if the consumer dies before acknowledging, the board hands the message to somebody else.

This is a good design. It is what RabbitMQ, ActiveMQ, SQS and JMS do. For "here is a job, someone please do it", it beats Kafka to this day, and §13.7 says exactly when to reach for it.

Now look again at the third bullet, because it is load-bearing: acknowledged means deleted. The board's job is to track, for every single message, whether it has been handled.

That one commitment produces four failures. Every one of them turns into a design constraint of Kafka, so it is worth watching each one happen to Deodar.

5. How does the pigeonhole board break Deodar Dispatch?

In one breath: Four ways. Two audiences fight over one note. A team that arrives late finds nothing. A retry scrambles a parcel's story. And the bookkeeping itself becomes the bottleneck.

5.1 Two audiences want one message — who gets it?

Sameer's notification service and Kabir's fraud service both subscribe to the scan board. The scan P-1001/PICKED_UP arrives once. Sameer's consumer picks it up and acknowledges. The board deletes it. Kabir never sees it.

The classic fix is fan-out at the broker: declare one queue per team, and have the board copy each message into all four. This is exactly what an AMQP exchange does. Count what that costs Deodar:

What the board now does At Deodar's peak Why it hurts
stores 4 copies of every scan 4 × 4,000 = 16,000 stored messages/sec four times the disk and four times the write path
tracks 4 acknowledgements per scan 16,000 tracked deliveries per second each one is a small random-access state mutation

And team five is now an operations change rather than a code change: declare a queue, bind it. Better than §3. But it is still a change that must happen before the first message team five cares about ever arrives.

5.2 What does Asha find when she asks for last year?

Eight months in, Asha's prediction-model team arrives. Her queue is created today. It receives messages from today. The 300 million scans from before are gone — not archived, not slow to fetch, deleted, because deletion was the design.

The workaround everyone reaches for is to also write every scan to a database or a data lake, and let latecomers read that instead.

Look at what Arjun has just built. The scan service writes to two places, which is the dual write again. The two disagree during incidents. And "the stream" and "the history" are now two systems, with two schemas and two sets of bugs.

5.3 Why does one retry scramble a parcel's story?

This is the failure that most surprises people, so trace it with real values.

Kabir's fraud consumer reads the board for parcel P-1001, whose four scans were enqueued in order. It takes PICKED_UP (09:02) and starts work. Then its JVM hits a long stop-the-world pause, the kind you measured in jvm-garbage-collectors.

Meanwhile the same service takes AT_HUB (09:41) and OUT_FOR_DELIVERY (11:15) on other threads, and finishes both. The PICKED_UP message times out unacked. The board, doing exactly its job, redelivers it.

Kabir's service now sees, in this order:

AT_HUB (09:41) → OUT_FOR_DELIVERY (11:15) → PICKED_UP (09:02)

A parcel that was picked up after it went out for delivery. His impossible-route detector fires on a parcel that is perfectly fine.

The cause is structural, not a bug. A broker that redelivers on failure cannot also preserve order, because redelivery is reordering. You may have one or the other, never both.

Classic brokers are task-oriented, so they chose redelivery. The usual mitigation is one consumer, prefetch 1, no concurrency — which throws away all your throughput to buy the ordering back.

5.4 Why is the bookkeeping itself the bottleneck?

Every message on the board is a small mutable record with a state machine (ready → delivered → acked → deleted), and it lives per consumer.

That is random-access, read-modify-write work. It is precisely the access pattern btree-and-lsm taught you is the expensive one.

This is why classic brokers are typically comfortable in the thousands-to-tens-of-thousands of messages/sec range per broker. It is also why they start hurting when the queue gets deep: millions of pending messages means millions of live records to index, to page in, and eventually to rewrite.

🔮 Predict: Which of the four failures above would be fixed by simply buying a bigger broker machine?

Reveal Only 5.4, and only for a while. 5.1, 5.2 and 5.3 are consequences of the **semantics**: "acknowledged means deleted", and "the broker owns delivery state". No hardware changes semantics. If you guessed 5.3, notice that a faster machine makes the GC pause less likely but does not remove redelivery, and redelivery is the reordering. This is the moment to stop optimising and start questioning the model. It is exactly the moment LinkedIn was at in 2010 when they wrote Kafka.

6. What does Arjun actually need? The real requirement

In one breath: Strip the four failures down to what they demand, and you get six requirements. Three of them, taken together, force the design before you have written a line of it.

Arjun needs a thing where:

  1. The fact is written once, and that one write serves every audience, present and future. This kills 5.1's write amplification and §3's fan-out.
  2. Reading does not consume. Two hundred readers can read the same fact and none of them removes it for the others. This kills 5.1.
  3. Facts are kept for a declared time, not until handled. History is the default, not a separate system. This kills 5.2, and it is Asha's answer.
  4. Order is preserved for facts about the same thing, even under failure and retry. This kills 5.3, and it is Kabir's answer.
  5. Each reader's position is its own. Sameer can be 2 seconds behind while Devi is 3 hours behind, and neither affects the other or the scanner.
  6. The broker's per-reader bookkeeping must be tiny, so that adding readers is nearly free. This kills 5.4.

Now put requirements 2, 5 and 6 side by side, because together they leave no choice at all.

If reading does not consume, the broker cannot track per-message state. If each reader has its own position, that position must live somewhere. If the bookkeeping must be tiny, that somewhere cannot be a big structure.

So a reader's entire state must be a position — a single number.

That is Kafka. The rest is engineering.

7. What if nobody ever deletes anything? The ledger and the bookmark

In one breath: Throw away the pigeonhole board. Put a ledger in the dispatch office instead: a book where every fact is written on the next free line and nothing is ever crossed out. Each team keeps a bookmark saying which line it has reached.

Replace the pigeonhole board with a ledger. The board was a pile of tasks the broker handed out and destroyed. The ledger is an append-only book where every fact is written on the next line. Each reader keeps a bookmark: the line number they have reached.

Two consequences follow, and both are worth saying out loud, because together they are the shift.

The broker gets simpler, not smarter. It appends bytes to the end of a file, and it serves byte ranges out of the middle. It does not know whether anyone processed anything. When Sameer's service crashes, the ledger does not notice, does not care, and has nothing to redeliver — because it never delivered anything in the old sense. It answered a read.

The consumer gets the responsibility. "Where am I?" is now Sameer's problem, not the ledger's. And as §10.8 shows, when he saves that bookmark decides whether his system loses records or duplicates them. Kafka does not remove that choice. It hands it to you, explicitly, in one config.

Here is the whole shape, before any of the machinery arrives:

flowchart TD
    S["Deodar scanner<br>one fact, written once"] --> L
    L["The ledger<br>append-only, nothing<br>is ever crossed out"]
    L --> A["Sameer<br>bookmark: line 402"]
    L --> B["Kabir<br>bookmark: line 388"]
    L --> C["Devi<br>bookmark: line 12"]
    L --> D["Asha, in eight months<br>bookmark: line 0"]

Notice what the picture does not contain. There is no arrow from a reader back to the ledger saying "done, you may delete this". That arrow was the whole of the old design, and it is gone.

The engineer's name for this ledger is a log. Not the printf sense of the word. The write-ahead-log sense, from acid and change-data-capture.

You have now met this same structure four times. The write-ahead log that makes a database durable. The replication log that makes a follower a copy. The event store in event-sourcing. The append-only segments of an LSM tree.

Kafka's insight was to notice that the thing databases hide inside themselves is the most useful thing to expose between systems.

Say it back. Without using the words log, offset or broker, tell a friend what changed between §4's pigeonhole board and §7's ledger, and why Kabir stopped missing Sameer's messages.

A good answer sounds like "The old office gave each note to one person and then burned it, so whoever got there second got nothing. The new office writes every note into a book that nobody is allowed to tear pages out of. Reading a page does not use it up. So Kabir and Sameer both read the same page, each keeps their own bookmark, and neither can take anything away from the other."

8. Which words do you now need, and what did each one cost?

In one breath: A handful of words carry the rest of the lab. Each one names something you have already watched happen to Deodar, so meet the thing first and the word second.

Only the load-bearing terms live here. The rest arrive when the mechanism needs them, and all of them are defined precisely in the glossary (§16).

One line in the ledger is a record. In the story it is one parcel scan. To Kafka it is one immutable entry: (key, value, timestamp, headers, offset). Kafka does not care what the value is; it is bytes. People also call a record a message or an event, and all three words mean the same line in the book.

The thing written at the front of the line is the key. The key is not an identifier, however much it looks like one. Its single job is choosing which ledger the record goes into (§10.3), and therefore which records it is ordered against. P-1001 is a good key. A UUID that is unique per scan is usually a bad one, and §10.3 shows why.

The name on the ledger's spine is the topic, for example parcel-scans. A topic is purely a name. The storage is the partitions underneath it. It is not a queue, despite the shape of most tutorials.

The actual append-only book is a partition. A topic is a set of them. Ordering exists inside a partition and nowhere else. That is the single most important sentence in this lab, and §14.2 is the misconception it exists to kill.

The line number is the offset. It starts at 0 and increases by one per record, forever. The triple (topic, partition, offset) names a record uniquely and permanently. Offsets are not comparable across partitions: partition-0/offset-500 and partition-1/offset-500 have no relationship whatsoever.

Here is the first group of words, with the section where you watched each thing happen:

Word The thing in Deodar Where you met it
Record one parcel scan, written on one line §2's scan trace
Key P-1001, the parcel a scan is about §5.3, when P-1001's story got scrambled
Topic the name on the ledger's spine, parcel-scans §7's ledger
Partition one physical book that only grows §7's ledger, split in §10.2
Offset the line number, permanent and per-book §7's bookmarks

The second group is the people and the places. Producer and consumer are client libraries in your application, not servers. That surprises people, and it matters: a surprising amount of Kafka's cleverness lives on the client side, which is why Kafka client versions matter as much as broker versions.

Retention is the rule that stops the ledger growing forever. It is declared per topic, and its default retention.ms is 7 days. Two things about it are worth fixing in your head now.

The broker deletes by age or size, never by "everyone has read it". And a consumer that falls behind by more than the retention period does not slow anything down. It loses data, silently. §11.6 is that failure in full.

Checkpoint — the beginner model is complete. You can now explain what a log-based broker is and why it is not a queue. You cannot yet explain how it goes faster than one machine, survives a crash, or lets ten consumers share work. Those are §10's eight refinements, and each one is forced by a failure in the one before.

Say it back. Explain to Priya, who runs billing and writes no code, why a brand-new team can read last month's scans out of Arjun's ledger but could not out of the pigeonhole board. Do not use the words offset, partition or retention.

A good answer sounds like "The old office destroyed each note the moment someone collected it, so everything that happened before you joined is simply not there any more. The new office writes every note into a book and keeps the book for a set number of days, whether anybody read it or not. A new team just opens the book at page one. 9. What is a topic really, and where does the picture lie to you?

In one breath: Think of a newspaper archive: issues filed in order, kept for seven days, read by anyone who walks in. The picture is useful, and it lies to you in four specific places you need to know before it hardens.

The newspaper archive — what does the simple model say?

A topic is a newspaper, and each partition is one edition printed continuously. The broker is the archive. It files every issue in order and keeps them for seven days.

Subscribers do not receive papers. They come to the archive and read from where they left off, noting the page number as they go. A hundred subscribers cost the archive nothing extra, because they are reading the same shelf.

Nobody's reading removes an issue. After seven days the archive sends the old ones for pulping, whether or not anybody read them.

The engineering model — what is actually on the disk?

topic "parcel-scans", 3 partitions, replication factor 3

  partition 0  [ 0 ][ 1 ][ 2 ][ 3 ][ 4 ][ 5 ]         ← leader on broker 1
  partition 1  [ 0 ][ 1 ][ 2 ]                        ← leader on broker 2
  partition 2  [ 0 ][ 1 ][ 2 ][ 3 ][ 4 ]              ← leader on broker 3
                 ↑              ↑
        group "analytics"    group "notifications"
        committed offset 1   committed offset 4

Four facts about that picture:

  • a partition is a directory of segment files on one broker's disk, plus two copies elsewhere;
  • writes go only to the leader replica of a partition, and followers pull from it;
  • a record is bytes at a byte-offset in a segment file, found through a sparse index;
  • a consumer's state is a small number per partition — and even that number lives in a Kafka topic (§10.8), so the broker still stores nothing bespoke.

Which archive word maps to which Kafka word?

The shelf and the files first:

Newspaper archive Kafka
Newspaper title topic

Seven-day shelf, then pulped retention.ms

Then the readers and the copies:

Three archives in three towns, one authoritative replication factor 3, one leader

Where does the archive story lie to you?

Read this before the analogy hardens. Four lies, each one a bug waiting for you.

  1. There is no single chronological "newspaper". Three partitions are three independent editions. Record X in partition 0 and record Y in partition 1 have no defined order, ever, even if X was produced an hour earlier. The archive analogy quietly suggests a global timeline. There isn't one.
  2. Readers are not passive. A Kafka consumer pulls, in batches, on its own schedule. Nothing is pushed. This is why Devi being three hours behind cannot hurt the broker or the scanner. She simply asks less often. Compare the classic broker of §4, where a slow consumer makes the broker's backlog grow.
  3. The archivist has no idea who read what. Not "does not tell you" — does not know. Consumer lag is computed by subtracting a number the consumer wrote down from a number the broker knows. Nobody tracked a delivery.
  4. Pulping is not "when everyone's done". Retention is a clock, not a reference count. The archive throws away issues that nobody has read yet, on schedule. That is a feature, because it bounds storage. It becomes §11.6's failure when Devi's consumer is down for a long weekend.

🔮 Predict: Devi's analytics group is 3 hours behind. Sameer's notification group is 2 seconds behind. What does Devi's lag cost Sameer?

Reveal Nothing at all. They read the same files at different positions, and neither group's bookmark is visible to the other. If you said "the broker must hold three hours of extra state for Devi", you are still picturing the pigeonhole board: the broker holds the *records* anyway, for everyone, until retention expires. Devi's entire cost to the cluster is read bandwidth, and her records are old enough that they may come from disk rather than page cache. That is the one real cost, and it is small.

10. How is the machine actually built, one failure at a time?

In one breath: Start with the simplest ledger that could possibly work. Break it. Fix it. Repeat eight times, and you have Kafka. Nothing here is invented for its own sake; every refinement is forced by the failure of the one before.

We build Kafka the way it was built: the simplest thing that could work, then the failure that breaks it, then the fix.

10.1 Version 1 — why is one file on one machine already fast?

In one breath: Arjun's first ledger is a single file. Producers append to the end, consumers read from a position, and it is far faster than "writes to disk" makes it sound.

Arjun's first ledger is one file, parcel-scans.log, on one machine. The scanner appends. Sameer, Kabir, Devi and Priya read from a byte offset.

Look at §6's list. Requirements 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 are already satisfied by this trivial design. And requirement 4, order, is satisfied completely: there is one file, so there is one order, and it is the order of arrival.

Why this is fast, which is not obvious. People assume "writes to disk" means "slow", so it is worth being precise. Kafka's throughput is not magic. It is four choices.

  • Sequential writes. btree-and-lsm established the gap between sequential and random I/O — orders of magnitude, on both spinning disks and SSDs. Appending to the end of a file is the best case there is. It is all Kafka ever does on the write path.
  • The page cache does the caching. Kafka keeps no in-process record cache of its own. It writes into the OS page cache and lets the kernel flush. A consumer reading recent records is served from RAM without Kafka doing anything clever. Importantly, those bytes never live in the JVM heap, which is why Kafka brokers run comfortably with modest heaps on enormous machines. (jvm-garbage-collectors: bytes you never put on the heap are bytes the collector never scans.)
  • Zero copy. Serving a consumer is "send bytes 4,096–69,632 of this file to this socket". That is the kernel's sendfile path: no copy into user space, no deserialisation. Kafka never looks inside a record it is serving. One caveat worth knowing: TLS encryption forces the data back through user space, so sendfile no longer applies, and encrypted clusters give up this optimisation.
  • Batching. Producers group records into batches and compress the batch as a unit. The batch stays compressed on disk and is sent compressed to consumers. Compression ratios are far better on a batch of similar records than on one record, and the CPU is spent once.

None of that is a trick. It is what you get by choosing an access pattern the hardware likes, and then refusing to add cleverness on top of it.

🔮 Predict: One file on one machine. Deodar grows tenfold. Name the two different ceilings this design hits — and note that they are not the same ceiling.

Reveal

Ceiling 1 — the write side. One partition lives on one machine: its disk bandwidth, its NIC, its CPU. You can buy a bigger machine exactly once.

Ceiling 2 — the read side, and this one no hardware fixes. Records in a single log must be processed in order by the audience that cares about order. Kabir's fraud detector cannot run 20 threads over one log and keep parcel order. Even with infinite disk bandwidth, one log means one ordered reader.

If you named only ceiling 1, you are still thinking about hardware. Ceiling 2 is the interesting one, because it means the fix cannot be "make the log bigger". It has to be "have more logs". Which immediately raises the question that decides everything else: which records go in which log?

10.2 Version 2 — what does splitting into many logs cost?

In one breath: One topic becomes N independent books. You get parallel writing and parallel reading, and you immediately lose the thing you did not know you were relying on: a single global order.

A topic becomes N independent logs. Now N brokers write in parallel, and N consumers read in parallel.

And instantly you have paid the price named in §9's four lies: there is no longer a total order. Kafka does not try to hide this or soften it. The guarantee is stated exactly, and it is the one you will quote for the rest of your career:

Records are ordered within a partition. Across partitions, there is no order.

So the design question is not "how do I keep ordering". It is "what is the smallest unit of my data that genuinely needs ordering?"

For Deodar Dispatch that unit is the parcel. P-1001's four scans must stay in sequence. P-1001 and P-2002 have nothing to do with each other and may interleave freely.

That unit is called the key.

10.3 Which book does a parcel go into, and why the modulo you were warned about?

In one breath: The producer, not the broker, picks the book. It hashes the key and divides by the number of books. Same parcel, same book, ordered forever — until somebody changes the number of books.

The producer computes the partition. In the Java client's default partitioner:

partition = murmur2(serialized_key) % number_of_partitions       // key present

Same key → same partition → same log → ordered, forever. Different keys spread across partitions.

Trace it, with parcel-scans at 3 partitions:

P-1001 PICKED_UP        murmur2("P-1001") % 3 = 2   → partition 2, offset 40
P-2002 PICKED_UP        murmur2("P-2002") % 3 = 0   → partition 0, offset 88
P-1001 AT_HUB           murmur2("P-1001") % 3 = 2   → partition 2, offset 41
P-1001 OUT_FOR_DELIVERY murmur2("P-1001") % 3 = 2   → partition 2, offset 42
P-1001 DELIVERED        murmur2("P-1001") % 3 = 2   → partition 2, offset 43

P-1001's story is offsets 40 to 43 of partition 2, in order, permanently.

Now look at what Kabir just got. His detector can run three consumers in parallel, one per partition, and still never see a parcel out of order, because a parcel never spans partitions. §5.3's scrambled parcel cannot happen here.

This is the trick that makes ordered parallel processing possible at all, and it is worth sitting with. Kafka did not solve "ordering versus parallelism". It made you declare the granularity at which you need order, and then gave you parallelism across that granularity.

When the key is null, the producer has no ordering constraint to honour, so it optimises for batching instead. The modern Java client uses sticky partitioning: fill a batch for one partition, send it, then switch to another. Older clients round-robined per record, which made batches small and throughput worse; the change was KIP-480. Note the consequence. A null key means no ordering guarantee for anything, which is fine for independent events and catastrophic for a parcel's scan sequence.

Now the modulo. In consistent-hashing you established at length that hash(key) % n makes every key's home depend on a global property, so that changing n remaps almost everything. You learned the ring as the fix. Kafka uses the modulo anyway. Why?

Because the ring's fix does not apply here. Consistent hashing moves keys to new homes cheaply. But a Kafka partition is not a bucket you can rehash. It is an ordered, immutable, append-only log, with consumers holding positions inside it.

You cannot move record 41 of partition 2 into partition 3 without destroying the offsets that every consumer, every committed bookmark and every replica depends on. So Kafka makes the opposite trade:

Partition count is (effectively) permanent. You may increase it, but existing records never move, and from that moment a key may live in two partitions.

Watch it hurt Deodar. Arjun increases parcel-scans from 3 to 4 partitions at 14:00. P-1001's scans from before 14:00 sit in partition 2 at offsets 40 to 43. But murmur2("P-1001") % 4 = 1, so scans from 14:01 onward go to partition 1.

A consumer of partition 1 may process DELIVERED while OUT_FOR_DELIVERY is still unread in partition 2. Ordering for that key is broken across the boundary, permanently, with no error, no warning, and no way to repair it.

This is the single most common self-inflicted Kafka wound. Three operational rules follow from it:

  • over-provision partitions at topic creation, and §13.3 says how to pick the number;
  • if you must add partitions to a keyed topic, either accept a bounded window of disorder (often fine — pause producers, drain consumers, then add) or create a new topic and migrate;
  • never add partitions to a compacted topic used as a table (§10.9) without a full rebuild, because the "latest value per key" invariant breaks the same way.

🔮 Predict: Devi points out that 80% of Deodar's scans come from 5 large retail customers, and suggests keying by customer_id instead of parcel_id, "so each customer's data stays together." What happens?

Reveal

A hot partition, of exactly the kind you measured in partitioning-and-rebalancing. With 12 partitions and 5 heavy customers, at most 5 partitions carry ~80% of the traffic, and one of them probably carries 30% on its own.

That partition's leader broker saturates while others idle. And here is the part people miss: the consumer assigned to that partition becomes the lag bottleneck for the whole pipeline, because a partition is consumed by exactly one member of a group (§10.10). You cannot add consumers to fix it. There is nothing to add them to.

If you answered "it balances better because big customers get their own partition", you have the causality backwards. A partition is not sized to fit its customer. It is one of a fixed set, and a big customer simply lands on one.

The general rule, straight from the partitioning lab: choose the key by the ordering requirement, then check its cardinality and skew. parcel_id has millions of values and near-uniform traffic. customer_id has hundreds, with a power-law distribution. Same topic, same code, completely different operational life.

Checkpoint — you have the log. You can now explain the log, offsets, partitions and keys. Everything so far assumed the broker never crashes. It does, and the next three refinements are entirely about that.

Say it back. Tell Arjun, in one minute and with no jargon, why he must decide the number of books on the day he opens the ledger. Then tell him what it costs if he changes his mind next year.

A good answer sounds like "Each parcel is assigned to a book by a sum that divides by the number of books. Change the number of books and the sum gives a different answer, so tomorrow's pages about a parcel go into a different book from yesterday's. The old pages cannot be moved, because everyone's bookmarks point into them. So for every parcel that was mid-journey, its story is now split across two books that nobody reads in step. Nothing warns you. It just quietly stops being in order."

10.4 Version 3 — what does "written" mean when there are three copies?

In one breath: One disk dies and P-1001's story is gone, so each book gets three copies. Now "written" splits into three different meanings, and the whole of Kafka's durability argument is about which one you were promised.

One machine holds partition 2. Its disk dies. P-1001's story is gone, along with Devi's year of history.

Meet Meera, who runs Deodar's cluster. Her fix is the one from quorums-and-replication, specialised: each partition gets R replicas (typically 3) on different brokers. One is the leader, and all reads and writes for that partition go to the leader. The others are followers.

One detail is worth noticing. Followers replicate by sending the leader an ordinary fetch request, the same protocol consumers use. A follower is, mechanically, a consumer that writes down what it reads.

Three numbers per replica govern everything that follows. Meet each as a thing first, then as a sentence, then as its acronym.

The thing on Meera's whiteboard In words The name
how far this copy has got the next offset this replica will write LEO, log end offset
the line below which every copy agrees the highest offset that every in-sync replica has, and the limit of what consumers may read HW, high watermark
the list of copies still keeping up replicas that have fetched up to the leader's LEO within the last replica.lag.time.max.ms (default 30s) ISR, in-sync replica set

That third row hides something important. The in-sync test is a time lag, not a message-count lag. A replica two million records behind but fetching hard is still in-sync, because it is keeping up with the rate.

Why can consumers not read above the high watermark? Suppose the leader has written offset 105 but only it has that record. If a consumer could read 105 and the leader then died, the new leader (which has up to 104) would assign offset 105 to a different, later record.

A consumer would have read a record that never officially existed. Worse, the offset would be reused for different content. The high watermark is the line between "written on the leader" and "impossible to lose without losing every copy". Kafka calls records below it committed.

That is a mechanism you have seen before under another name. The high watermark is Kafka's commit point, and the ISR is a dynamic quorum rather than the fixed majority of quorums-and-replication. That difference matters, and §12.4 is where it is paid for.

How much of this does the scanner wait for? That is the producer's acks setting. In story terms it is Arjun deciding how many books must hold the scan before the scanner is told "yes". It is the config people get wrong most often after partition count.

acks The producer waits for It survives It costs
0 nothing (fire and forget) nothing; you will not even learn about a failure fastest; unbounded loss
1 the leader wrote to its log the leader staying alive one crash between write and replication loses acknowledged records
all (-1) every replica in the ISR has it any failure that leaves an ISR member alive one extra round trip of latency

Since Kafka 3.0 the default is acks=all, with idempotence on (§10.7). That is the right default, and it reverses a decade of tutorials that say otherwise.

And now the trap. acks=all means all in-sync replicas, not all replicas. Trace it on Meera's cluster:

partition 2, RF=3, acks=all, min.insync.replicas=1 (the default!)

10:00:00  ISR = {B1(leader), B2, B3}         healthy
10:00:12  B2 and B3 both fail
10:00:42  30s of replica.lag.time.max.ms elapse → leader shrinks ISR = {B1}
10:00:43  producer sends record R, acks=all
10:00:43  B1 writes R, and "all in-sync replicas have it" is TRUE — ISR is {B1}
10:00:43  producer receives SUCCESS
10:00:50  B1's disk fails
          → R is gone. The producer was told it was durable.

The ISR shrank to a single replica, and acks=all degraded silently to acks=1. Nobody lied. The sentence "all in-sync replicas have it" was true.

The fix is the second half of the setting. In story terms it is the rule Meera writes on the wall: refuse the scan unless at least two books have it. In words, it is the smallest number of in-sync copies that must hold a record before the broker may say yes. As a config key it is a topic or broker setting, not a producer one:

min.insync.replicas = 2      # with RF=3 → tolerate one failure, refuse at two

With min.insync.replicas=2, the write at 10:00:43 fails immediately with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS instead of succeeding falsely.

The durable configuration is acks=all AND min.insync.replicas=2 AND replication.factor=3 — all three, or none of them.

Notice what Meera just did. She chose unavailability over silent loss during a partition. That is cap-theorem's choice, made in two config lines per topic. Kafka lets her make it differently for each topic, which is a genuinely nice piece of design: Priya's billing topic and Devi's metrics topic need not answer the question the same way.

🔮 Predict: RF=3, min.insync.replicas=3, acks=all. How many broker failures can this topic tolerate while still accepting writes?

Reveal

Zero. Any single broker failure drops the ISR to 2, below min.insync, and every produce request fails.

If you said "two, because there are three copies", you counted copies that can be lost rather than copies that must be present. RF=3 with min.insync=3 is the classic "maximum-durability" misconfiguration. It maximises the durability of what is written by making writing impossible the first time anything sneezes.

The arithmetic to remember: failures tolerated for writes = RF − min.insync.replicas. So 3 − 2 = 1 is the standard production choice. If you want to survive two failures while still writing, you need RF=5 and min.insync=3 — which is quorums-and-replication's majority, and precisely what Kafka's own internal controller quorum uses.

10.5 What happens when the leader dies, and what is a leader epoch for?

In one breath: The survivors must agree on what the book contains. Kafka got that wrong until 2017, in a way that lost acknowledged records, and the fix is an object you already own under seven other names.

The leader of partition 2 dies. The controller (§13.1) picks a new leader from the ISR, and every remaining replica must now agree on what the log contains.

Followers can be at different LEOs, so the new leader's log may be shorter than a surviving follower's. Records above the new leader's LEO were never committed, because they were above the high watermark. So the rule is: followers truncate to match the new leader, then resume fetching. Uncommitted records are discarded, which is correct, because nobody was ever told they were durable.

Here is where Kafka had a real, shipped, data-losing bug until 2017. The fix is a concept you already own.

Kafka's followers originally truncated to their high watermark on becoming a follower. But the high watermark propagates to followers one fetch round behind: a follower learns the new value in the response to its next fetch. That one-round lag is enough to lose data.

Setup: partition 2, replicas A (leader) and B. Both have offsets 0..9. HW = 9.

t1  A appends record X at offset 10.        A: LEO=11 HW=9
t2  B fetches, receives X, writes it.       B: LEO=11 HW=9   (B has not yet
                                             learned the HW moved to 10)
t3  A advances HW to 10 (all replicas have X).
t4  B crashes before its next fetch — so B never learns HW=10.
t5  B restarts. Rule: truncate to my HW (9). B drops X.  B: LEO=10
t6  A crashes before B re-fetches.
t7  B becomes leader with LEO=10, appends a *different* record Y at offset 10.
t8  A restarts as follower, truncates to its HW... and the two logs disagree
    about what offset 10 contains.

Record X was committed. The producer was told acks=all succeeded. And it is gone, replaced by a different record at the same offset.

Two logs now permanently disagree about the content of one offset. That is worse than loss. It is divergence, and every consumer that read X saw something that no longer exists.

The fix, KIP-101 (Kafka 0.11, 2017), is the object split-brain told you has seven names: a leader epoch. Every leadership change increments a monotonic integer. Every record is stamped with the epoch of the leader that wrote it. Each broker keeps a small leader-epoch-checkpoint file mapping epoch to first offset.

On restart, a follower no longer guesses from its own stale number. It asks:

B → A:  OffsetsForLeaderEpoch(partition 2, my last epoch = 5)
A → B:  "epoch 5 ended at offset 10"
B:      truncate to exactly 10 — no more, no less

Truncation becomes a fact obtained from the authority, rather than an inference from a stale local number.

Take the general lesson, because it recurs across a dozen labs. A node must sometimes decide how much of its own state is still valid after a leadership change. That answer has to come from the new authority, and it has to be tagged with a generation number. Never inferred from a stale local one.

You have now met that object under seven names: Raft's term, ZAB's epoch, CockroachDB's lease, the ownership epoch of partitioning-and-rebalancing, the fencing token of distributed-locking, and here Kafka's leader epoch.

And what if every in-sync copy is gone? Suppose only an out-of-sync replica survives. Meera has exactly two choices, and Kafka makes her declare which:

Setting What happens to the partition What Meera chose
unclean.leader.election.enable = false (the default) goes offline — no reads, no writes, until an ISR member returns consistency, paid for in downtime
unclean.leader.election.enable = true the stale replica is promoted and the partition returns instantly availability, paid for with committed records that are silently gone, and offsets reused for new records

That is cap-theorem again, per partition, as a boolean. Leave it false unless you are handling a stream where a gap is genuinely cheaper than downtime. Some metrics pipelines qualify. Nothing that bills anyone does.

10.6 What does the producer see when the network eats an acknowledgement?

In one breath: The scanner sends a scan, the broker writes it, and the "yes" is lost on the way back. The scanner cannot tell that from "the scan never arrived", so it retries — and that is how duplicates are born.

Now zoom out to the client. Arjun's scan service sends a record, and the network does what networks do:

producer → [record] → leader:  leader writes it at offset 41, sends ack
                               ← ack lost in the network
producer times out, retries    leader writes it AGAIN at offset 42

Partition 2 now contains P-1001's AT_HUB scan twice, at offsets 41 and 42. Sameer texts the customer twice.

This is the idempotency lab's opening problem, verbatim: the sender cannot distinguish "my request was lost" from "the response was lost", so it must retry, and retrying is how duplicates are born.

Worse, retries can reorder. With multiple requests in flight, batch 1 may fail and be retried while batch 2 succeeds. The log then holds batch 2 before batch 1. You have lost the ordering guarantee §10.3 sold you, without touching the keys.

10.7 Version 4 — how does the broker recognise a repeat?

In one breath: Give every batch a name the broker can check: who sent it, which generation of that sender, and which number in the sequence. A repeat is then obvious, and so is an out-of-order arrival.

The fix is the idempotency lab's answer, moved into the protocol: give the request a key that lets the server recognise a repeat.

  • On startup, the producer gets a PID (producer ID) and an epoch from a broker.
  • Every batch carries (PID, epoch, partition, sequence number), with the sequence increasing by one per batch per partition.
  • The leader remembers the last 5 sequence numbers per (PID, partition). A batch whose sequence it has already seen is acknowledged but not written. A batch arriving with a sequence more than one ahead is rejected with OUT_OF_ORDER_SEQUENCE_NUMBER.

That buys two guarantees for the price of one.

No duplicates from producer retries, because the retry is recognised and dropped. And no reordering from retries, because the strict sequence check makes it impossible for batch n+1 to land before batch n.

That second one is why up to max.in.flight.requests.per.connection = 5 batches can be in flight and ordering still holds. Before idempotence, ordering under retries required max.in.flight=1, which cost you most of your throughput. The idempotent producer is a throughput feature as much as a correctness one.

Since Kafka 3.0, enable.idempotence=true is the default, which also forces acks=all and retries=Integer.MAX_VALUE. Modern defaults are the safe ones. Most Kafka folklore predates them.

Be precise about the boundary, because §14.4 depends on it:

The idempotent producer does stop The idempotent producer does not stop
duplicates from a retry inside one producer session duplicates from your application re-sending after its own restart
reordering from retries duplicates when the producer restarts and gets a new PID
— duplicates seen by the consumer after a rebalance (§10.8)
— duplicated side effects — the text message Sameer already sent

It de-duplicates a network conversation, not your business process. The business process still needs idempotency's idempotency key, applied at the point where the effect happens.

Checkpoint — producer to broker is complete. Scans are partitioned, keyed, replicated, durable by explicit config, and exactly-once onto the log within one session. Nothing so far has said how a consumer remembers where it is, or how ten consumers split the work. That is the second half of Kafka, and it has more failure modes than the first.

Say it back. Explain to Sameer why the broker can throw away a repeated batch safely, but cannot throw away a repeated text message.

A good answer sounds like "The broker gave the scanner a badge, and it numbers every packet it sends. So when packet 7 turns up twice, the broker knows it is the same packet and just says 'yes, got it' again. The text message has no badge and no number. It is an effect out in the world. The only place that can spot a repeat is the thing that sends texts, and it has to keep its own list of who it has already texted."

10.8 Version 5 — where does the bookmark live, and when do you write it?

In one breath: The bookmark cannot live inside the consumer, or a crash loses it. Kafka stores it as an ordinary record in an ordinary topic. Then it hands you one unavoidable choice: save the bookmark before you do the work, or after.

Sameer's notification consumer reads partition 2 from offset 40 to 43, texts four customers, and crashes. It restarts. Where does it resume?

The bookmark must be durable and outside the consumer process. Otherwise every restart replays from the beginning of retention.

Kafka's answer is pleasingly self-referential. The consumer commits its offset by producing a record to an internal topic called __consumer_offsets, keyed by (group, topic, partition), whose value is "next offset to read". That topic is compacted (§10.9), so it collapses to the latest bookmark per key and stays small forever.

Notice what did not happen. The broker did not record a delivery. It received an ordinary write to an ordinary topic. Requirement 6 from §6 survives intact.

Now the choice. Commit before processing, or after?

COMMIT BEFORE PROCESSING ("at-most-once")
  poll offsets 40..43 → commit 44 → crash while texting offset 42
  restart at 44. Customers for 42 and 43 are never texted. RECORDS LOST.

COMMIT AFTER PROCESSING ("at-least-once")
  poll offsets 40..43 → text all four → crash before committing
  restart at 40. Customers for 40..43 are texted AGAIN. DUPLICATES.

There is no third option available to a consumer whose side effects live outside Kafka. This is not a Kafka limitation. It is the two-generals shape of the whole field, and you have met it as at-most-once versus at-least-once in idempotency and transactional-outbox.

Kafka's default, and the only sane choice for almost everyone, is at-least-once, with duplicates neutralised at the point of effect: a dedup table, an idempotency key, or natural idempotence.

Say the whole sentence whenever someone asks about delivery semantics: at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent consumer equals effectively-once processing.

Auto-commit is a third, sneakier position. In story terms it is Sameer letting the library move his bookmark on a timer, whether or not he has finished reading. In words: the client commits the offsets returned by the previous poll, from inside poll(), every few seconds. As a config key that is enable.auto.commit=true — the default! — with auto.commit.interval.ms at 5s.

So it commits records you may still be processing, which is a bounded at-most-once window. And it also allows duplicates on a crash inside that 5s window. It is the convenient option, and the one to turn off in anything that matters.

What if the committed offset is gone? Devi's analytics consumer is off for a long weekend. Retention is 7 days, but her lag was already 6 days. It restarts, asks for offset 1,240,000, and that offset has been deleted.

The broker answers OFFSET_OUT_OF_RANGE, and the consumer applies auto.offset.reset:

Value What the consumer does What it costs Devi
latest (the default) jumps to the end of the log everything in between is skipped silently; her model is trained on a hole she cannot see
earliest jumps to the oldest surviving record everything in between is reprocessed, which for a non-idempotent consumer is a different disaster
none throws an exception loud, and usually correct for anything that bills

This config is also what decides what a brand new group does on first start, and that is the reading of it people actually know. Both readings share one default, which is why "we deployed a new consumer group and it silently ignored the last seven days of data" is a rite of passage.

🔮 Predict: Sameer turns enable.auto.commit off and commits by hand after each batch. He now believes he cannot lose a text. Is he right?

Reveal

He is right about loss and wrong about duplicates, and he has probably not noticed the swap. Committing after processing is at-least-once: nothing is skipped, and a crash between the last text and the commit re-sends every text in that batch.

If you said "he is right, it is now exactly-once", look for the third option in the trace above. There isn't one. The only way to stop the second text is to make the texting idempotent, which is a change in Sameer's service, not in his Kafka config.

10.9 Version 6 — the log is not infinite, so what goes and what stays?

In one breath: Deodar writes hundreds of gigabytes a day, so something must delete. Kafka offers two policies: throw away old pages by the clock, or keep only the latest page per parcel. The second turns a stream into a table.

Start with the arithmetic, because it is what forces the whole section.

Quantity Number What it means
Deodar's peak scan rate 4,000 scans/sec how fast the ledger grows at the busiest hour
average record size 400 bytes one scan, serialised
raw write rate ≈ 1.6 MB/s bytes arriving at the leader
one day of scans ≈ 138 GB/day the topic's own growth
the same day with RF=3 ≈ 414 GB/day disk actually consumed across the cluster

Something must delete.

Physically, a partition is a directory of segment files, whose default size segment.bytes is 1 GiB. Exactly one of them is the active segment, the one being appended to. The rest are closed and immutable.

Deletion happens by removing whole closed segments. Never by editing a file. Never by removing one record. This is why "delete this one record" is not a Kafka operation, and why GDPR erasure on a Kafka topic is a design problem rather than an API call. (event-sourcing covered the crypto-shredding answer.)

Two cleanup policies, and they answer different questions.

cleanup.policy=delete is the default, and it is the stream policy. It drops whole segments once every record in them is older than retention.ms (default 7 days), or once the partition exceeds retention.bytes. Kafka is a buffer of recent history, and the size is bounded by a clock you choose.

cleanup.policy=compact is the table policy. It keeps, for every key, at least the most recent record, forever. Older values for the same key are eventually removed by a background log cleaner.

before compaction (offsets are permanent, gaps are normal):
  0:(P-1001,PICKED_UP) 1:(P-2002,PICKED_UP) 2:(P-1001,AT_HUB)
  3:(P-1001,DELIVERED) 4:(P-2002,AT_HUB)
after compaction:
  1:(P-2002,PICKED_UP)?  3:(P-1001,DELIVERED)  4:(P-2002,AT_HUB)
        └─ removed once superseded; offsets 0 and 2 are simply absent

A compacted topic is a table. Replay it from offset 0 and you have the current value of every key that ever existed.

That is the log–table duality you met in change-data-capture and cqrs. It is how __consumer_offsets stays small, how Kafka Connect stores connector state, and how Kafka Streams backs its state stores.

Deletion in a compacted topic is a null value — a tombstone. It is kept for delete.retention.ms (default 24h) so that a consumer reading through has a chance to observe the deletion, then removed. Absence cannot be shipped: it is the same result the partitioning lab reached. A gap is indistinguishable from never-heard-of-it, so deletion must travel as a record.

Three things about compaction surprise people, and each one is a real incident.

  1. It is not deduplication, and it is not prompt. The cleaner only processes closed segments. It only runs when a partition's "dirty ratio" exceeds min.cleanable.dirty.ratio (default 0.5, meaning half the log is stale). And min.compaction.lag.ms can hold records back deliberately. A consumer reading a compacted topic will see multiple values for a key. Compaction bounds growth; it does not give you a unique-key view.
  2. It is per-partition, so it is per-key only if the key picks the partition. Adding partitions to a compacted topic scatters one key's history across two partitions, and the "latest value per key" invariant dies.
  3. delete and compact can be combined as cleanup.policy=compact,delete, which compacts and still enforces retention. It is used when you want a table that also forgets — Kafka Streams' windowed state, for example.

10.10 Version 7 — how do ten consumers share the work without doing it twice?

In one breath: Consumers that share a group name split the books between them, one book to one reader. Everything good and everything painful about Kafka's consumer side falls out of that single rule.

Sameer's single consumer cannot keep up with 4,000 scans/sec. He wants ten consumer processes sharing the work, and he wants them not to do the same work twice.

A consumer group is a set of consumers sharing a group.id. The rule is one sentence, and every consumer-side property follows from it:

Each partition of a subscribed topic is assigned to exactly one consumer in the group at a time.

Not each record. Each partition. That is why a slow record is never handed to somebody else, so §5.3's reordering cannot occur here. It is why ordering per key survives parallel consumption. And it is why the broker still tracks nothing per record.

Three consequences are worth memorising, because interviews and incidents both live here.

Parallelism is capped by partition count. With 12 partitions and 20 consumers, 8 consumers sit idle, holding no partitions and doing nothing. This is the second reason partition count is your most important sizing decision (§13.3).

Groups are independent. Sameer's group at offset 400,000 and Devi's group at offset 12,000 read the same files with no interaction at all. That is requirement 2 from §6, delivered. Adding Devi's group costs the cluster only more read bandwidth, usually from page cache, so it is nearly free.

A rebalance is not a data move. Be careful here, because the word means something very different in partitioning-and-rebalancing. That rebalance copies terabytes between nodes. This one reassigns who reads what, and moves zero bytes. Same word, different universe. Confusing them is §14.7.

How is the group coordinated? One broker acts as the group coordinator for a group, chosen by hashing group.id onto a __consumer_offsets partition. Members send heartbeats to it.

Two independent clocks decide whether a member is alive, and mixing them up is the most common consumer misdiagnosis there is.

The clock Default What it measures It is blown when
session.timeout.ms 45 s time since the last heartbeat, sent by a background thread every heartbeat.interval.ms (3 s) the process died, is network-partitioned, or is in a long stop-the-world GC pause
max.poll.interval.ms 5 min time between poll() calls on your application thread your processing of one batch is too slow: the process is alive and heartbeating, but stuck

A consumer that hangs for 90 seconds processing stays in the group, because heartbeats continue, and gets kicked out at 5 minutes. A consumer whose process is frozen for 50 seconds is kicked out at 45.

Different symptoms, different fixes. The first is "reduce max.poll.records or speed up processing". The second is "fix your GC or your network".

When membership changes, the group rebalances. There have been three generations of that protocol, and knowing which one you are running matters.

  1. Eager, the original. Everyone revokes all partitions, then the leader computes a new assignment and everyone re-joins. It is a stop-the-world pause for the entire group, on every single membership change. Rolling- restart 10 consumers and you trigger up to 20 full pauses.
  2. Cooperative incremental (KIP-429, Kafka 2.4, CooperativeStickyAssignor). Members keep the partitions that are not moving. Only the reassigned ones are revoked, in a second round. Consumers that aren't affected never stop.
  3. The new consumer group protocol (KIP-848). The broker computes assignments and reconciles them incrementally with each member. There is no group-wide barrier and no client-side "group leader" at all. It is GA on the broker since Kafka 4.0, but as of Kafka 4.3 the client default group.protocol is still classic. You opt in with group.protocol=consumer, and 4.3 logs a warning telling you classic is on its way out.

Static membership (KIP-345) is the other half of the answer. In story terms it is giving each of Sameer's ten machines a permanent name badge. In words: a member with a stable instance identity that leaves and returns quickly keeps its own partitions. As a config key it is group.instance.id, and a member that returns within session.timeout.ms gets its same partitions back with no rebalance at all. This is what makes rolling restarts of a large consumer fleet cheap.

🔮 Predict: Devi's analytics group has 12 consumers on 12 partitions. One consumer's host has a 40-second network blip, and the group uses the eager protocol. How much processing does the group lose?

Reveal

Far more than one consumer's share for 40 seconds — which is the answer most people give, and it is wrong by an order of magnitude.

At 45s session.timeout the blip is just survivable. But if it tips over, the coordinator declares the member dead and triggers a rebalance in which all 12 consumers revoke all 12 partitions and stop. Nobody processes anything until the new assignment completes: typically seconds, but tens of seconds with large groups, slow onPartitionsRevoked handlers, or a group that must first commit offsets.

Then the blipped consumer comes back and rejoins, triggering a second full-group rebalance. One flaky NIC on one host stopped a 12-machine pipeline twice.

This is the entire motivation for cooperative rebalancing and static membership. It is also why "why did our lag spike to 4 minutes when nothing was wrong?" is usually answered by grepping consumer logs for Revoking previously assigned partitions.

10.11 Version 8 — what does "exactly-once" actually cover?

In one breath: Kabir reads a scan, writes an alert, and saves his bookmark. Three separate acts, and a crash between any two is a bug. Kafka can make all three atomic, as long as every one of them happens inside Kafka.

One case remains, and it is the one that made Kafka an application platform rather than a pipe.

Kabir's fraud service is a consume-process-produce loop. It reads from parcel-scans, computes, writes to fraud-alerts, and commits the input offset. Three actions, and a crash between any two of them is a bug:

crash after writing the alert, before committing the offset
  → restart reprocesses the scan → duplicate alert
crash after committing the offset, before writing the alert
  → the scan is never reprocessed → missing alert

The idempotent producer of §10.7 cannot help, because these are two different resources. What is needed is atomicity across a set of partitions, including the __consumer_offsets partition holding Kabir's bookmark.

Kafka's answer arrived in 0.11 (2017), and the machinery should feel familiar from two-phase-commit and saga:

  • The producer declares a stable transactional.id. On initTransactions() a transaction coordinator assigns it a PID and bumps its epoch. That coordinator is a broker, selected by hashing the id onto the internal __transaction_state topic. The bump fences any older instance with the same id. A zombie that wakes from a GC pause and tries to write is rejected with ProducerFenced. That is the fencing token again, doing exactly what distributed-locking said it must.
  • Records written during the transaction go into their real partitions immediately, marked as part of the transaction. The offset commit is sent to the coordinator via sendOffsetsToTransaction, and becomes part of the same transaction.
  • commitTransaction() runs a two-phase commit. The coordinator writes the decision to __transaction_state. It then writes a control record, which is a commit or abort marker, into every partition the transaction touched.
  • Consumers with isolation.level=read_committed never return records from open or aborted transactions. They read only up to the LSO, the last stable offset, which is the offset of the earliest still-open transaction. Aborted records are physically present in the log and filtered out by the client.

Two costs decide whether you should use any of this.

Latency. A read_committed consumer cannot advance past an open transaction. One hung transactional producer stalls its consumers until transaction.timeout.ms (default 60 s) aborts it. And read_uncommitted is still the client default in 4.3, which has no such wait. So enabling transactions on the producer changes nothing for consumers until you also change the consumer.

Scope. This is the sentence to keep: Kafka's exactly-once is exactly-once within Kafka. Read-from-Kafka → write-to-Kafka → commit-offset is atomic. Read-from-Kafka → charge a credit card → commit-offset is not, cannot be, and no configuration will make it so.

The moment an effect leaves Kafka, you are back to idempotency's answer: an idempotency key at the sink, or a transactional outbox on the way out.

Kafka Streams sets all of this up for you with one setting, processing.guarantee=exactly_once_v2. That is why most people who use exactly-once successfully are using Streams rather than assembling it by hand.

Checkpoint — the machine is complete. Append-only logs, partitioned by key, replicated with an ISR and a high watermark. Fenced by leader epochs and written idempotently. Read by groups holding their own bookmarks. Bounded by retention or collapsed by compaction, and optionally transactional inside the cluster. Everything from here is what it looks like when it goes wrong.

Say it back. Kabir asks you to promise that his fraud pipeline will never raise a duplicate alert and never call the police API twice. Which half of that can you promise, and why is the other half not yours to give?

A good answer sounds like "I can promise the alert. Reading the scan, writing the alert and moving the bookmark all happen inside the same building, so they can be made one act that either all happens or none of it does. The police API is outside the building. If we crash after calling it, nobody in here knows the call happened. The only fix is for the call itself to carry a reference number the police side recognises as a repeat."

11. What does it look like when it goes wrong?

In one breath: Nine failures worth carrying into an on-call rotation. Three of them arrive as silence: no error, no alert, and dashboards that look correct. Those three are the ones to read twice.

Meera is on call for Deodar's cluster. Every scenario below is one of her nights.

11.1 The leader broker dies — why is that a non-event?

A broker holding the leadership of partition 2 stops. The controller (§13.1) notices that the broker's session is gone. It picks a new leader from the ISR for each partition that broker led, and propagates the new metadata.

Arjun's producers and Sameer's consumers get NOT_LEADER_OR_FOLLOWER, refresh their metadata, and retry against the new leader. That is the whole event.

On Meera's screen it shows up as a latency spike of roughly seconds on the affected partitions, UnderReplicatedPartitions above 0 until the dead broker returns and catches up, and possibly a burst of producer retries.

Everything below the old high watermark survives. That is everything acknowledged under acks=all with min.insync.replicas ≥ 2. Records that were only on the dead leader are discarded by truncation, and no producer was ever told those were durable.

The point of this scenario is how boring it is. With correct settings it is a non-event, handled by client retries. Most so-called "Kafka outages" are really this one, plus a client configured with retries=0 or an unwisely short delivery.timeout.ms.

11.2 How does a garbage-collection pause eject a healthy broker?

A follower hits a 20-second stop-the-world pause and stops fetching. It is the exact failure mode you measured in jvm-garbage-collectors.

If the pause exceeds replica.lag.time.max.ms (30 s), the leader drops that follower from the ISR. If the pause happens to the leader instead, its controller session lapses and its partitions fail over. All of that for a machine that is perfectly healthy 20 seconds later.

Meera sees IsrShrinksPerSec spike and then IsrExpandsPerSec recover. That shape is the flap. With min.insync.replicas=2 and RF=3, one flap is survivable. Two overlapping flaps make producers fail with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS.

The lesson is one you already own: the failure detector cannot distinguish slow from dead. That is split-brain's central point, arriving in Kafka as ISR flapping. It is also why Kafka brokers run modest heaps and lean on the page cache. Fewer heap bytes means fewer long pauses, which means fewer false ejections.

11.3 Why is an ISR of one the most dangerous green on the dashboard?

This is the first silent one.

Two of three replicas go slow. A degrading disk, a saturated NIC, a noisy neighbour — the cause hardly matters. The ISR quietly becomes just the leader. With the default min.insync.replicas=1, Arjun's producers keep receiving successful acks=all acknowledgements that mean nothing at all. That is §10.4's trace, happening for real.

Why does nobody notice? Because throughput is fine. Latency actually improves, since there is nobody left to wait for. Nothing errors.

The only visible signal is UnderReplicatedPartitions and UnderMinIsrPartitionCount, which is exactly why those two sit on every Kafka runbook's alert list.

Ordering and offsets are intact throughout. Durability is gone. Deodar is one disk away from losing acknowledged data, and the dashboard is green.

11.4 What does an unclean leader election destroy?

The second silent one, and the destructive one.

Every ISR member for a partition is down. unclean.leader.election.enable is true. A stale replica is promoted at LEO 8,400, while the true log had reached 9,000.

Exactly 600 committed records vanish. New records then reuse offsets 8,400 to 9,000 with completely different content.

Consumers already past 8,400 hold offsets that now point at different records. Consumers still behind will read a log with a hole in it. And in a compacted topic, values silently revert to older ones.

Detection from the outside is almost nil. Downstream teams discover it as reconciliation mismatches, days later.

11.5 Consumer lag is growing — what shape is it?

Lag is how far behind a group has fallen, counted per partition: lag = log end offset − committed offset. It is the number Kafka operators watch above all others, because it is the only place where "the business is behind" becomes visible.

Read it per partition, never only in aggregate. The shape names the cause.

The shape Meera sees What it means What actually helps
all partitions lag equally the consumers are globally too slow add consumers, up to the partition count, or make processing faster
one partition lags, the rest flat a hot key (§10.3's prediction) or a poison record wedging one consumer fix the key or the record; adding consumers changes nothing
lag sawtooths every few minutes rebalances grep for Revoking previously assigned partitions
lag flat and non-zero, throughput normal an idle consumer holding a partition it never processes, or a stuck offset commit find the member and restart or fix it
lag falls to zero suddenly and permanently not good news — see the prediction below investigate before celebrating

The queueing view. From queueing-theory: consumers are servers with a service rate. If the arrival rate exceeds it, lag grows without bound, and no amount of patience fixes it. If arrival is 95% of capacity, lag is finite, but recovery time from any blip is brutal, on the order of 1/(1−ρ). Size consumers for the peak plus the catch-up rate you need, never for the average.

🔮 Predict: Devi's lag chart has been climbing for two days. This morning it is flat at zero and has stayed there. What happened?

Reveal

Almost certainly not "the consumer caught up". Two days of climb does not reverse overnight without someone adding capacity.

Either the consumer skipped ahead via auto.offset.reset=latest after its committed offset expired (§11.6), or someone reset the offsets by hand. In both cases the records between are unread and unreachable.

If you said "great, the fix worked", you have just made the mistake this whole lab is trying to prevent. A sudden green is the most dangerous green there is, because a metric that measures "how far behind are we" reads zero both when you have caught up and when you have given up.

11.6 What happens when retention outruns a consumer?

The third silent one, and the data really is gone.

Devi's consumer is down over a long weekend. retention.ms is 7 days and her lag was already large. On restart, her committed offset no longer exists, and auto.offset.reset=latest (the default) jumps her to the end.

What Meera sees is lag dropping to zero. The consumer runs perfectly. Dashboards are green. Three weeks of model training later, somebody notices a hole.

Three defences, in the order you should apply them. Set auto.offset.reset=none on anything that matters, so it fails loudly. Alert on lag as a fraction of retention, rather than on lag alone. And remember the Kafka-specific version of the rule: retention is your recovery-time budget. If you cannot repair a broken consumer inside retention.ms, then retention.ms is too short.

11.7 What does one poison record do to a partition?

One record cannot be deserialised or processed. A schema change, a corrupt payload, a null that somebody swore was impossible.

Kabir's consumer throws. It restarts. It reads the same offset. It throws again.

Because there is no redelivery and no dead-letter concept in the broker, the partition stops advancing forever, while its 11 siblings run perfectly. On the dashboard it is exactly the "one partition lags, the rest are flat" signature from §11.5, accompanied by a CrashLoopBackOff.

The fix is architectural, and it is on you. Kafka has no dead-letter queue, so you build one: catch the failure, produce the offending record to parcel-scans.DLQ along with its error and its original coordinates, commit the offset, and move on.

Kafka Connect and Spring Kafka both ship this pattern. The broker does not — and now you know why. A broker that knew which records had failed would be back to per-message state, which is §5.4, which is the thing Kafka was built to escape.

11.8 How does a rebalance storm start?

It starts with something outside Kafka entirely. Processing gets slower than max.poll.interval.ms, usually because a downstream API degrades.

Then the loop closes. A consumer is evicted mid-batch. Its partitions move to already-busy peers. Those peers get slower and are evicted in turn.

With the eager protocol, every eviction stops the whole group. Throughput collapses to near zero, and the group spends its life rebalancing rather than consuming.

No data is lost, because offsets were either committed or not. But you will see duplicates, since every eviction reprocesses whatever was uncommitted.

Five fixes, in order of effect:

  1. reduce max.poll.records, so each batch is completable in time;
  2. raise max.poll.interval.ms to a bound that reflects reality;
  3. switch to CooperativeStickyAssignor, or to group.protocol=consumer;
  4. give planned restarts a group.instance.id;
  5. move slow I/O off the poll thread, using the pause/resume API rather than heartbeating a lie.

11.9 What stops a zombie producer that wakes up?

Kabir's transactional producer freezes, from a GC pause or container throttling. Its orchestrator gives up and starts a replacement with the same transactional.id. Then the original wakes up and tries to write.

Nothing bad happens, and that is the point. The coordinator bumped the producer epoch when the replacement called initTransactions(). The zombie's writes are rejected with ProducerFenced, and its in-flight transaction is aborted.

It is the fencing token from distributed-locking, working exactly as designed. And it is the one protection that requires transactions: a plain idempotent producer that restarts gets a new PID and is not fenced at all.

12. What exactly does Kafka promise, and what does it refuse to promise?

In one breath: Most Kafka arguments are two people using one word for different things. Here are the promises stated precisely, the non-promises stated just as precisely, and the one design decision that explains the shape of both lists.

12.1 What does Kafka guarantee?

Learn to state these as carefully as they are written.

  1. Per-partition order of writes. Records appended to a partition are read by every consumer in the same order, with the same offsets, forever. (Assumes no unclean leader election.)
  2. Per-key order, given a stable partition count and a keyed producer. All records with key k land in one partition, so they are ordered relative to each other. P-1001's four scans, in Kabir's hands, in sequence.
  3. Per-producer order within a partition, even under retries, when enable.idempotence=true (the default since 3.0) with max.in.flight ≤ 5.
  4. Durability of committed records. A record acknowledged under acks=all with min.insync.replicas=m survives up to m−1 simultaneous broker failures, provided unclean leader election is off.
  5. At-least-once delivery to a consumer, by default and in practice.
  6. Atomicity across partitions within one Kafka cluster for transactional writes plus offset commits, observed by read_committed consumers.
  7. Immutability. A record at (topic, partition, offset) never changes. It may be deleted wholesale by retention or compaction. It is never edited.

12.2 What does Kafka not guarantee?

  1. Any order across partitions. No global order, no cross-partition timestamps, ever. Sameer cannot ask "what happened first" across two parcels.
  2. Order across a partition-count change for a given key (§10.3).
  3. Exactly-once delivery. Delivery is at-least-once. Processing can be exactly-once, only inside Kafka, only with transactions.
  4. Deduplication of application-level retries. Two send() calls with the same payload are two records. Nothing looks at content.
  5. That an acknowledged record is on disk. Kafka acknowledges when replicas have the record in their page cache, not after fsync, and flush.messages/flush.ms default to effectively never. Durability comes from replication, not from syncing. A simultaneous power loss across all replicas can lose acknowledged records; a normal process crash cannot, because the page cache survives it. This is a deliberate trade, and the thing to check before writing "durable" in a design doc. (Contrast acid's WAL, where durability meant exactly fsync.)
  6. Delivery to a specific consumer. There is no addressing. Consumers pull.
  7. Time ordering. The timestamp field is producer-supplied by default (message.timestamp.type=CreateTime), so offsets and timestamps can disagree. Set LogAppendTime if you need broker-stamped times, at the cost of losing the original event time.

12.3 What is assumed underneath?

  • Non-Byzantine. Brokers do not lie. A corrupt disk that returns wrong bytes is outside the model, and CRCs catch some of it, not all.
  • The failure detector is a timeout, so slow is indistinguishable from dead (§11.2). FLP is the reason this cannot be fixed, only tuned.
  • Clocks matter only for retention and timestamps, never for the correctness of ordering. Kafka orders by append, not by time. This is why Kafka needs nothing like Spanner's TrueTime.
  • The controller quorum is a real consensus group. In KRaft mode, cluster metadata is itself a replicated log kept by a Raft quorum of controllers. It is the raft lab, running inside Kafka.

12.4 What is the one trade-off that explains Kafka's character?

Kafka's ISR is a dynamic quorum, unlike the fixed majority of quorums-and-replication.

With RF=3 and majority quorums you need 2 acknowledgements, always. With the ISR you need "all of whoever is currently keeping up", which is 3 acknowledgements when healthy and 1 when degraded.

The benefit is real: a slow replica is removed rather than tolerated, so latency tracks the healthy replicas instead of the median one. Meera's cluster does not run at the speed of its sickest disk.

The cost is also real: the size of your durability guarantee is a runtime variable. Which is precisely why min.insync.replicas exists. It is the floor you place under a quorum that would otherwise shrink to nothing.

That one design decision explains most of Kafka's character. It prefers to keep running with fewer copies and make you declare the minimum, rather than to stop whenever the ideal is unavailable.

Checkpoint — the engineering model is complete. You can now reason about duplicates, ordering, rebalances and transactions, and you can name the nine failures including the three silent ones. What is left is the gap between this lab's model and the cluster Meera actually operates.

In one breath: The model you now hold is correct and incomplete. A real cluster adds a metadata quorum, tiered storage, security, quotas and a dozen knobs. Four decisions in this section are the ones that are expensive to change later.

13.1 What is a cluster made of, now that ZooKeeper is gone?

ZooKeeper is gone. For a decade Kafka kept cluster metadata in ZooKeeper, whose protocol you learned in the zab lab. KRaft replaced it. The controllers form a Raft quorum, and metadata becomes just another Kafka log that brokers consume.

KRaft went production-ready in 3.3, and ZooKeeper support was removed entirely in Kafka 4.0 (March 2025).

Meera gets three wins from that. Fewer moving parts. Much faster failover with large partition counts, because brokers cache metadata locally and apply deltas instead of re-reading ZooKeeper. And one security model instead of two.

If you read a pre-2024 Kafka guide, mentally delete every ZooKeeper paragraph.

13.3 How many partitions, and why can you not take it back?

Partition count sets your maximum consumer parallelism (§10.10) and is painful to change (§10.3). It is the decision Arjun must get roughly right on day one.

The working method has four steps.

  1. Measure or estimate per-consumer throughput t: records per second that one consumer instance can process, including its slowest downstream call.
  2. Take the peak arrival rate λ, and add the catch-up headroom you want. Twice the peak is typical. Recall queueing-theory: with no spare capacity, recovery time is unbounded.
  3. Compute partitions ≥ 2λ / t, then round up for future growth.
  4. Sanity-check the other direction. Partitions are not free. Each one is files, memory and a replication stream on some broker, and each adds controller metadata and failover work. Modern KRaft clusters handle hundreds of thousands cluster-wide, but "one partition per customer" designs still die of it.

Run it for Deodar:

Input Value Where it comes from
peak arrival rate λ 4,000 records/s §2's scan rate at the busiest hour
per-consumer throughput t ~350 records/s one consumer instance, including its slowest call
headroom multiplier 2× catch-up capacity, from queueing-theory
computed floor 2×4000/350 ≈ 23 the minimum that can keep up and catch up
chosen partition count 24 or 32 rounded up for growth

Not 3, and not 1,000.

🔮 Predict: Arjun's board asks why the topic cannot start at 3 partitions and grow "when we need to, like a database". What is the one-sentence answer, and what is the second-order cost they have not thought of?

Reveal

One sentence: a database can move a row to a new shard, but Kafka cannot move a record to a new partition without destroying the offsets everyone is holding (§10.3).

The second-order cost is the one people miss. Growing later does not merely break ordering for in-flight parcels. It breaks it silently and permanently, with no error and no repair. And if the topic is compacted, the "latest value per key" invariant dies with it, so anything rebuilt from that topic is quietly wrong.

If you answered "you can just pause producers", you are half right, and that is genuinely the standard mitigation. It works because it bounds the window of disorder, not because it prevents it.

13.4 Which four metrics would you watch if you could only watch four?

  1. UnderMinIsrPartitionCount — partitions currently unable to satisfy min.insync.replicas. Producers to them are failing, or about to. Page on it.
  2. Consumer lag, per group and per partition — the business-level "how far behind are we", and the only one a product owner can read. Alert on trend and on lag ÷ retention, never on an absolute number.
  3. UnderReplicatedPartitions plus IsrShrinksPerSec — durability erosion and flapping. This pair is §11.3's silent failure made visible.
  4. RequestHandlerAvgIdlePercent — broker saturation. Below roughly 30% you are on queueing-theory's cliff, and every latency percentile is about to move together.

After those four: ActiveControllerCount, which must be exactly 1 cluster-wide; produce and fetch latency percentiles; disk free versus retention; and OfflinePartitionsCount, which should be 0, because anything else means a partition has no leader.

13.6 What are the ecosystem pieces, in one line each?

  • Kafka Connect — a cluster that runs source and sink connectors, so you do not write another "poll DB, push to Kafka" service. It is where Debezium (change-data-capture) lives.
  • Kafka Streams — a Java library, not a cluster, for stateful stream processing. Its state stores are local (RocksDB) and backed by compacted Kafka topics, and it is the easiest correct way to get exactly-once.
  • Schema Registry — versioned schemas plus compatibility rules. It is the answer to "what stops team five breaking every consumer with a field rename". It is not part of Apache Kafka.
  • Share groups (KIP-932) — queue-style, per-record acknowledgement inside Kafka, so several consumers can share one partition and individual records can be re-delivered. Early access in 4.0, preview in 4.1, and reported production-ready in 4.2. This is Kafka growing a second, queue-shaped consumption model alongside the log-shaped one. Worth watching, because it removes the main reason to run RabbitMQ alongside Kafka.

13.7 When is Kafka the wrong answer?

Say this out loud in design reviews. It is the fastest way to be trusted with the choice.

  • A task queue with per-message retry, priorities, delays or per-message acking. That is RabbitMQ, SQS or Sidekiq shaped, and until share groups mature it is not Kafka shaped. Kafka's unit is the partition, not the message.
  • Request/response. Kafka has no correlation, no addressing and no timeout semantics. Use RPC (communication-protocols).
  • Fewer than a handful of events per second, one producer, one consumer. You are paying a cluster's operational cost for a database table and a poller.
  • Random access by key. Kafka reads by offset or timestamp, never by key. "Give me parcel P-1001's current state" is a table question, and the answer is a compacted topic materialised into a database, or a Kafka Streams state store.
  • Very large payloads. The practical message limit is about 1 MB (message.max.bytes). Put the blob in S3 and the pointer in Kafka.
  • Strict global ordering across everything. That needs one partition, which caps you at one consumer and one machine. If you truly need it, you need a different design, not a bigger Kafka.

Checkpoint — the production model is complete. You can size a cluster, name the four metrics, say what KRaft replaced, and refuse Kafka when the shape is wrong. One thing is left: the beliefs that survive all of this and still cause incidents.

14. Which beliefs about Kafka are wrong, and what kills each one?

In one breath: Ten beliefs that sound right, are widely repeated, and cause incidents. Each one dies to a single small scenario, and the scenario is worth more than the correction.

14.1 "Kafka is a message queue" — what is the smallest thing that disproves it?

It feels right because Kafka sits between producers and consumers, and half the documentation on the internet calls it one.

The smallest counterexample is two consumer groups on one topic. Sameer's group and Kabir's group both receive every record. A queue that gives every message to every reader is not a queue.

The correct model is the one you built in §7: Kafka is a replicated, partitioned, append-only log with consumer-held positions. Consumption is a read, not a take. Everything else follows from that single difference: replay, multiple audiences, no redelivery, no dead-letter queue, no priorities.

14.2 "Kafka guarantees ordering" — ordering of what, relative to what?

It feels right because Kafka is a log, and logs are ordered.

Take a topic with 2 partitions and no key. Produce A, then B. They land in different partitions, and two consumers read them in either order.

Ordering is per partition, therefore per key, and only while the partition count is unchanged. So when somebody says "we need ordering", the only useful reply is "ordering of what, relative to what?" Kabir needed ordering per parcel. He never needed it per town.

14.3 "acks=all means all replicas have it" — which replicas, exactly?

You already have the counterexample: §10.4's trace, where the ISR shrank to {leader} and acks=all succeeded with one copy.

acks=all means all currently in-sync replicas, and that is a set which shrinks under failure. min.insync.replicas is what puts a floor under it. Three settings, or none.

14.4 "Exactly-once means my side effects happen once" — where does the transaction end?

It feels right because the feature is genuinely called exactly-once semantics.

Consume a scan, POST /charge, then crash before committing the offset. On restart the scan is reprocessed and Priya's customer is charged twice. No Kafka setting prevents this, because the charge is outside the transaction.

Exactly-once semantics is atomicity of Kafka reads, Kafka writes and offset commits, within one cluster. Every effect outside Kafka needs idempotency's answer: an idempotency key at the sink.

14.5 "More partitions means more throughput" — what does a partition actually buy?

It feels right because partitions are the parallelism unit, so more of them must be faster.

Now picture a topic with 5,000 partitions on a 3-broker cluster. Producer batches fragment across many partitions, so batches are smaller, compression is worse, and there are more requests. Each partition costs files and memory on a broker. End-to-end latency rises, and failover has vastly more work to do. Meanwhile consumer throughput is completely unchanged, if your consumers are the bottleneck.

Partitions set a ceiling on consumer parallelism. Throughput is set by the actual bottleneck, which is usually consumer processing or broker disk. Size with §13.3, then stop.

14.6 "Adding consumers makes the group faster" — faster than what?

Take 12 partitions, 12 consumers, and one hot partition. Add 12 more consumers. All 12 new ones sit idle, because there are no partitions left to assign them. And the hot partition is exactly as slow as it was before.

Parallelism is min(partitions, consumers), and within one partition it is bounded by exactly one consumer. Skew is a key-design problem, not a scaling problem. Devi's customer_id idea from §10.3 is the same mistake wearing a different hat.

14.7 "Rebalancing moves data" — which rebalance do you mean?

It feels right because you learned rebalancing in partitioning-and-rebalancing, where it moves terabytes.

A Kafka consumer group rebalance reassigns which consumer reads which partition. Zero bytes move, and it takes milliseconds to seconds.

The operation that actually moves data is partition reassignment: moving a replica between brokers, done with kafka-reassign-partitions or Cruise Control. That is the partitioning lab's rebalance, throttled and slow.

Two different operations, and only one of them is what your consumer logs mean.

14.8 "Compaction removes duplicates" — when does the cleaner actually run?

Take a compacted topic where you write key k twice per second, and read it back immediately. You see both values.

The cleaner runs on closed segments, past a dirty-ratio threshold, and never on the active segment.

Compaction eventually keeps at least the latest value per key. It is a storage-bounding policy, not a uniqueness constraint, and a consumer must be idempotent regardless.

14.9 "Kafka is slow because it writes to disk" — or fast because it doesn't?

Both beliefs are wrong, symmetrically, and people hold them in the same conversation.

Kafka writes every record to disk and is fast anyway. It only ever writes sequentially. It serves reads from the page cache via sendfile. And it moves batches rather than records. Nothing is held in the JVM heap for caching.

The speed comes from the access pattern, not from skipping the disk. That is precisely btree-and-lsm's central result, applied to a network service.

14.10 "If we lose data we can increase the replication factor" — which loss?

Look at where Deodar's losses actually came from. §11.6's silent skip after retention expiry. A consumer bug. An unclean leader election. RF=5 prevents none of the three.

Four different defences guard four different failures:

The defence The failure it stops The failure it does nothing about
replication factor broker or disk failure anything a consumer does
retention length slow recovery, because it is your repair budget a broker losing its disk
idempotent consumers duplicates at the point of effect records that were never read
schema compatibility poison records from a field rename a stale replica being promoted

People reach for the first one because it is a single number in a config file.

16. Glossary — what does each word mean, precisely?

Grouped by theme rather than alphabetically, and within each group in the order you met the thing in Deodar.

The ledger and its parts

  • Log — an append-only, ordered, immutable sequence of records. Kafka's only data structure.
  • Record — (key, value, timestamp, headers, offset); immutable once written. One parcel scan, in this lab.
  • Topic — a named collection of partitions, such as Deodar's parcel-scans. A name, not a storage unit.
  • Partition — one physical append-only log; the unit of ordering, parallelism, replication and assignment.
  • Offset — a record's permanent integer position within its partition. Meaningless across partitions.
  • Key — the record field that selects the partition (murmur2(key) % partitions), and therefore the unit of ordering. Not an identifier.
  • Sticky partitioning — the null-key strategy of filling one partition's batch before switching, for better batching (KIP-480).
  • Segment — a file holding a contiguous offset range; the unit of deletion. One per partition is "active" (being appended to) and never compacted.

The people and the servers

  • Producer / consumer — client libraries in your application, not servers.
  • Broker — a server holding partitions and serving fetch/produce requests.

Replication, and what "written" means

  • Replica / leader / follower — copies of a partition; all reads and writes go to the leader; followers replicate by fetching, exactly like consumers.
  • LEO (log end offset) — the next offset a replica will write; how far it has got.
  • High watermark (HW) — the highest offset held by all in-sync replicas; the boundary of what consumers may read. Kafka's commit point.
  • Committed (record) — replicated to all in-sync replicas, i.e. below the HW. Distinct from a committed offset (a consumer's bookmark) — one word, two meanings, constantly confused.
  • ISR (in-sync replica set) — replicas that have fetched within replica.lag.time.max.ms (30 s). A dynamic quorum: it shrinks under failure, which is why min.insync.replicas must floor it.
  • acks — how much replication a producer waits for: 0 / 1 / all (all in-sync, not all).
  • min.insync.replicas — the minimum ISR size for acks=all to succeed; below it, produce fails with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS. Default 1, which is wrong for production.

Failover, and the fencing that makes it safe

  • Truncation — a replica discarding records above the new leader's log on failover; correct because those records were never committed.
  • Leader epoch — a monotonic generation number per partition leadership; stamped on records, used via OffsetsForLeaderEpoch to truncate exactly. Kafka's name for the fencing token (KIP-101, fixing real divergence).
  • Log divergence — two replicas permanently disagreeing about the content of one offset; the pre-KIP-101 bug, worse than loss.
  • Unclean leader election — promoting an out-of-sync replica when the ISR is empty: availability bought with silent, unrecoverable data loss. Default off.

The producer's own guarantees

  • Idempotent producer — (PID, epoch, sequence) per partition lets the broker drop retried batches and reject out-of-order ones. Default on since 3.0; per-session only.
  • PID (producer ID) — broker-assigned id for a producer session; a new session gets a new PID, which is why idempotence does not survive a restart.

Consumers, bookmarks and groups

  • Committed offset — the consumer's bookmark, stored as a record in the compacted __consumer_offsets topic.
  • At-most-once / at-least-once — commit before processing (loses) versus after (duplicates). Kafka is at-least-once by default and by preference.
  • Effectively-once — at-least-once delivery plus an idempotent consumer. The honest name for what most systems achieve.
  • auto.offset.reset — what to do with no valid committed offset: latest (skip, default), earliest (reprocess), none (fail loudly).
  • Consumer group — consumers sharing a group.id; each partition is assigned to exactly one member. Groups are independent readers of the same log.
  • Group coordinator — the broker managing one group's membership and offsets.
  • Rebalance (consumer group) — reassigning partitions to members. Moves no data. Eager (stop-the-world) → cooperative incremental (KIP-429) → broker-driven (KIP-848).
  • Static membership — group.instance.id, letting a member restart within session.timeout.ms without triggering any rebalance (KIP-345).
  • session.timeout.ms vs max.poll.interval.ms — two independent liveness clocks: "are you alive?" (heartbeat thread) versus "are you still processing one batch?" (your thread).
  • Consumer lag — log end offset − committed offset, per partition. Read per partition; the shape names the cause.

Keeping the ledger finite

  • Retention — age- or size-based deletion of whole segments, independent of who has read them.
  • Log compaction — retaining at least the latest value per key; makes a topic table-shaped. Eventual, segment-based, never touches the active segment.
  • Tombstone — a record with a null value, meaning "this key is deleted"; retained for delete.retention.ms (24 h) so readers can observe the deletion.
  • Log–table duality — a stream of keyed changes and a table of current values are the same information in two shapes; compaction is the conversion.

Transactions and exactly-once

  • Transaction / transaction coordinator — atomic multi-partition writes plus offset commits within one cluster, decided by a coordinator and marked with control records.
  • transactional.id — a stable producer identity whose epoch bump fences zombies (ProducerFenced).
  • Control record — an internal commit/abort marker written into each participating partition; filtered out by clients but consuming an offset, which is why offsets can appear to skip.
  • LSO (last stable offset) — the read limit for read_committed consumers: the earliest still-open transaction. An open transaction stalls them.
  • isolation.level — read_uncommitted (default) sees aborted and in-flight transactional records; read_committed does not.
  • Exactly-once semantics (EOS) — atomic consume-process-produce inside Kafka. Never covers external side effects.

What goes wrong, and what runs the cluster

  • Poison record — a record that always fails processing, wedging its partition forever; needs an application-level dead-letter topic, since Kafka has none.
  • Rebalance storm — the slow-processing → eviction → slower-peers → eviction feedback loop.
  • KRaft — Kafka's built-in Raft metadata quorum; replaced ZooKeeper (production-ready 3.3, ZooKeeper removed in 4.0).
  • Controller — the elected quorum leader owning cluster metadata: leader election, ISR changes, topic creation. ActiveControllerCount must be exactly 1.
  • Tiered storage — offloading closed segments to object storage while keeping them readable (KIP-405, shipped 3.6).
  • Follower fetching — consumers reading from the nearest replica via client.rack to avoid cross-AZ cost (KIP-392).
  • Quota — per-client/user produce, fetch and request-rate limits; Kafka's built-in load shedding.
  • Share group — KIP-932's queue-style consumption with per-record acknowledgement, allowing multiple consumers per partition. Early access 4.0 → preview 4.1 → production-ready 4.2.
  • Purgatory — the broker structure holding delayed requests: a fetch waiting for fetch.min.bytes, a produce waiting for ISR acks.
  • UnderMinIsrPartitionCount / UnderReplicatedPartitions — the two metrics that make §11.3's silent durability loss visible.

Solutions — Apache Kafka

Attempt EXERCISES.md first. Where a question has no single right answer (rungs 7 and 8), what follows is a worked reasoning process and the tradeoffs a reviewer would expect you to name — not the only acceptable answer.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Kafka gave up the broker's knowledge of who has handled what. A classic broker tracks every message's delivery state per consumer and deletes it once acknowledged; that bookkeeping is the service it sells. Kafka refuses to do it: it appends facts to a file in order, keeps them for a fixed time, and makes each reader remember its own position. What it buys with that refusal is everything that per-message state made impossible — any number of independent audiences reading the same records without copying them, a new audience able to start from the beginning of history, no redelivery and therefore no reordering, and a broker whose per-reader cost is one integer instead of one record of state per message per consumer. The price is real and is paid by you: nothing tells you a record was processed, retention deletes data whether or not anyone read it, and "retry just this one message" is not an operation the broker offers.

1.2 Two consumer groups on one topic. Produce one record; both groups receive it. In a queue, one consumer takes the message and the other never sees it. No amount of throughput turns one into the other — it is a different answer to "what does consuming mean".

1.3 RabbitMQ is a post office: it holds a letter until someone collects it, then it is gone. Kafka is a newspaper archive: every day's edition is filed on a shelf for a fixed period, and anyone can walk in and read from any date. A new team is a new reader walking into the archive — the shelves do not care how many readers there are, and nothing they read is removed for anyone else.

1.4 If reading does not consume, the broker cannot mark anything as handled, so it cannot hold per-message state per reader. If each reader has its own position, the reader's progress must be represented somehow. If the bookkeeping must be tiny regardless of how many messages exist, that representation cannot grow with the message count. The only thing that satisfies all three is a single position in an ordered sequence — which is why the records must be ordered and addressable by position, i.e. a log with offsets. Any per-message representation (a set of handled ids, a bitmap) grows with the backlog and fails the third requirement.

1.5 A committed record is a broker-side durability fact: it has been replicated to every in-sync replica, so it is below the high watermark and consumers may read it. A committed offset is a consumer-side progress fact: this group has written "next read starts here" into __consumer_offsets. They involve different actors, different topics and different failure modes. A record can be committed and never read; an offset can be committed for records that were never successfully processed (§10.8).

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 All four land in the same partition — the one the routing table shows for P-1001 — because the partition is hash(key) % partition-count and both inputs are unchanged. The only things that would move them are a different key or a different partition count. (Deleting and recreating the topic with a different partition count is the same thing wearing a disguise.)

2.2 notifications has read up to 2 records per partition, analytics 1 per partition. The broker deleted nothing, and stores exactly one copy of each record per replica — not one per group. That is the whole economic argument for the log: a new audience costs read bandwidth, not storage and not write amplification.

2.3 State pending; high watermark 0; a consumer polling right now sees nothing. The record is on the leader's disk and is still invisible and unacknowledged, because "written on the leader" is not "committed".

2.4 B3 is dropped at the second round (the simulator's stand-in for replica.lag.time.max.ms). Afterwards the high watermark is the minimum LEO over the remaining ISR members only — which is why it can suddenly leap forward at the moment a lagging replica is ejected. That leap is worth internalising: the ISR shrinking makes the system look faster, which is exactly why §11.3 is silent.

2.5 "Partition-seconds stopped" differs; the final assignment is the same under both protocols (they agree on where partitions end up, not on how much of the group stops on the way). On a dashboard it is the area under the consumer-lag spike: partitions that stopped × how long they stopped.

2.6 Only the records in closed segments are eligible, and only where a later record for the same key exists. So the last P-1001 record survives (nothing supersedes it), everything in the active segment survives regardless of policy, and the surviving record from the active segment may well not be the latest value for its key — it is simply untouchable. Compaction is eventual; a consumer sees duplicates.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 The three A records are always processed in their produced order: same key → same partition → one log → one consumer in the group. A and B have no defined order unless they happen to hash to the same partition; two consumers read two partitions concurrently and either can win. This is the whole of Kafka's ordering guarantee in one exercise.

3.2 During the first restart: nothing visible. ISR drops to 2, which still satisfies min.insync.replicas=2, so writes succeed. During the second restart before the first has caught up: ISR drops to 1, below the minimum, and every produce fails with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS (a retriable error — producers retry until delivery.timeout.ms, so a short overlap is absorbed and a long one becomes a produce outage). This is the rolling-restart rule: wait for UnderReplicatedPartitions to reach zero between brokers, or you will take the outage that the setting exists to warn you about.

3.3 Sixteen rebalances — one when each consumer leaves, one when each returns. Under eager, every rebalance stops all 8 partitions: 16 × 8 × 3 s ≈ 384 partition-seconds of stopped processing to restart 8 processes. With CooperativeStickyAssignor only the moved partitions stop (roughly 1–2 per event), and with static membership (group.instance.id) plus a restart inside session.timeout.ms, the count is zero rebalances. That is the entire business case for both features.

3.4 The trap: with the standard loop — poll(), then process the whole batch, then poll() again — auto-commit does not fire during the 20 s of processing, because it only runs inside poll(). So at the crash at second 12 nothing has been committed, all 500 records are reprocessed, and nothing is skipped. Under that loop shape auto-commit behaves like a coarse commit-after-processing.

The at-most-once window appears the moment processing is not inline: hand the batch to an executor and return to poll() promptly, and the next poll commits offsets for records still sitting in your queue — crash then and they are skipped silently. Auto-commit is not dangerous because of what it does; it is dangerous because what it does depends on the shape of code far away from the config.

3.5 Friday evening lag is 3 h and rising, because nothing consumes over the weekend: by Monday it is roughly the whole weekend plus 3 h. Retention is 1 day, so from Saturday evening the group's committed offset points at deleted data. On Monday's restart the fetch returns OFFSET_OUT_OF_RANGE, auto.offset.reset=latest jumps to the end, and lag falls vertically to ~0. The graph is a ramp, then a cliff. A dashboard-watcher concludes "it caught up in seconds — Kafka is amazing". Roughly two days of records were never processed and no metric ever said so.

3.6 The read_uncommitted consumer sees the records immediately, including ones that will later be aborted — it is reading uncommitted data by definition. The read_committed consumer sees nothing at or after the transaction's first offset: its read limit is the LSO, which is pinned by the open transaction. At transaction.timeout.ms (default 60 s) the coordinator aborts the transaction, writes abort markers, the LSO advances past them, and the read_committed consumer resumes — having skipped the aborted records entirely. One hung producer stalled every read_committed consumer of that partition for a minute.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Impossible: an acknowledged acks=all write surviving on one replica only and then being lost to a single disk failure (§10.4's trace). Newly possible: producers being rejected with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS when two brokers are down or restarting together — a write outage. Given the choice, explain the write outage: it is loud, bounded, and recoverable, and the customer's data is intact. Silent loss of acknowledged data is the conversation nobody recovers from.

4.2 Any key that is stable under a partition-count change — i.e. one whose partition you compute yourself and store, rather than one derived by hashing. In practice: assign each parcel to a partition explicitly (a custom partitioner with a persisted mapping, or a key that encodes the partition). The cost is that you have now built and must operate your own partition map — the routing table of partitioning-and-rebalancing, with its staleness and rebalancing problems, which is precisely the work Kafka avoided by freezing the modulo. Usually the better answer is "over-provision partitions and never change the count".

4.3 max.in.flight=1 means one unacknowledged batch per connection: throughput drops sharply (you pay a full round trip per batch), latency per batch is unchanged, and ordering under retries is guaranteed by serialisation. What the idempotent producer makes of it: it makes the change pointless. With enable.idempotence=true the sequence-number check already guarantees ordering up to 5 in flight, so setting 1 buys nothing and costs most of your throughput. It is pre-2017 advice that survived into a lot of internal wikis.

4.4 Latency: worse (up to 100 ms added before a batch is sent). Batch size: bigger. Compression ratio: better (more similar records per block). Broker request rate: lower — often the real win, since broker CPU is frequently spent on request handling rather than bytes. Consumer lag: slightly better if the broker was request-bound, unchanged otherwise. If the team wanted throughput they got it; if they wanted lower end-to-end latency they made it worse. linger.ms is the clearest throughput-vs-latency dial in the client.

4.5 It guarantees a total order over every record in the topic, and processing by exactly one consumer in each group, in that order. Costs, in the order they hurt: (1) one consumer — your parallelism is 1 forever, so throughput is capped by a single process, and this bites first; (2) one broker holds the whole topic — its disk and NIC are the ceiling and no cluster growth helps; (3) recovery time — a single partition with a year of data means one replica to re-sync, one consumer to catch up, and no way to spread the catch-up. And the requirement is almost always false: the business rarely needs "all events in one order", it needs "events for one customer in one order", which is a key, not a partition count.

Rung 5 — Trace

5.1

step A (leader) B C ISR HW producer told
start LEO 5 5 5 A,B,C 5 —
1. produce off 5 LEO 6 5 5 A,B,C 5 waiting
2. B fetches 6 6 5 A,B,C 5 still waiting — C holds the HW back
3. C frozen 6 6 5 A,B,C 5 still waiting
4. 30 s pass 6 6 5 A,B 6 success for offset 5
5. produce off 6 7 6 5 A,B 6 waiting (ISR=2 ≥ min 2, so accepted)
6. B fetches 7 7 5 A,B 7 success for offset 6
7. A crashes dead 7 (leader, epoch+1) 5 B 7 new writes rejected: ISR 1 < min 2
8. C returns dead 7 catches up → 7 B,C 7 writes accepted again

Both records exist; nothing was lost. The two things worth noticing: at step 2 the producer is still waiting even though two replicas have the record — the high watermark is the minimum over the ISR, not a majority; and at step 7 the topic became read-only, which is min.insync.replicas choosing consistency over availability exactly as designed.

5.2 Cooperative reconciliation, target one partition each:

  1. C3 sends JoinGroup. The coordinator starts a rebalance; the assignor computes the target assignment (C1:{p0}, C2:{p2}, C3:{p1}) and compares it with the current one.
  2. Round 1 (revocation only): C1 is told to revoke p1. It runs onPartitionsRevoked — committing its offset for p1 — and stops reading it. C1 keeps reading p0 and C2 keeps reading p2 throughout. No global pause.
  3. C1 re-joins, triggering a second, short rebalance round.
  4. Round 2 (assignment): p1 is assigned to C3, which calls onPartitionsAssigned, fetches the committed offset for (group, topic, p1) from __consumer_offsets, and starts reading from there.

The offsets for the moved partition come from the group's committed offsets — which is exactly why the revoking consumer must commit before it lets go, and why a rebalance during uncommitted work produces duplicates.

5.3 First pass — only offsets 0–2 (the closed segment) are eligible: 0:(a,1) superseded by 5, 1:(b,1) superseded by the tombstone at 4, 2:(a,2) superseded by 5. Remaining: 3, 4, 5.

Append 6:(d,1); now 3–5 is closed and 6 is active. Second pass: 3:(c,1) — no later c, kept. 4:(b,null) — a tombstone with no later b; kept until delete.retention.ms has passed since it was written, then removed. 5:(a,3) — latest a, kept. Remaining: 3, 4, 5, 6.

A consumer replaying from 0 builds {c:1, a:3, d:1} and, on reading the tombstone at 4, removes b from its map (or never adds it). If it ignores null values it will carry a phantom b forever — the single most common bug when materialising a compacted topic.

5.4 Order of events:

  1. beginTransaction() — local only; nothing on the wire yet.
  2. First send() to out-p0 — the producer first sends AddPartitionsToTxn to the coordinator (recorded in __transaction_state), then the record is written into out-p0's log immediately, marked transactional. It is physically present; a read_uncommitted consumer can already see it; a read_committed consumer cannot, because the LSO has stopped there.
  3. sendOffsetsToTransaction(101, groupId) — AddOffsetsToTxn followed by a TxnOffsetCommit write into the group's __consumer_offsets partition, as part of the same transaction.
  4. commitTransaction() — the coordinator writes PREPARE_COMMIT to __transaction_state, then writes a commit control record into every participating partition (out-p0 and the __consumer_offsets partition), then marks the transaction complete.
  5. Only now does the LSO advance past the record, and the read_committed consumer sees it — together with the offset commit, atomically.

Note the shape: it is two-phase-commit with the coordinator's decision durable in a log before any participant is told, which is why a coordinator crash mid-commit is recoverable rather than a blocking hole.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 The nightly repartition destroys per-customer ordering forever. Smallest scenario: customer C7's events are in partition 2; at 02:00 the count goes 12→16 and C7 now routes to partition 5; at 02:05 C7's "address changed" lands in partition 5 while "order placed" is still unread in partition 2 — two consumers, either order. Violated: per-key ordering. Secondary flaw: splitting by size does nothing for a hot key, because the hot key stays whole (§10.3's prediction).

6.2 Idempotence protects against duplicates, not against gaps. Smallest scenario: the consumer hands each batch to a thread pool and returns to poll(); auto-commit commits offsets for records still queued; the process is killed; those records are never processed. Violated: at-least-once processing. An idempotent consumer has no opinion about records it never saw.

6.3 min.insync.replicas is unset, so it is 1, and acks=all degrades silently the moment the ISR shrinks (§10.4). Smallest scenario: two brokers slow, ISR = {leader}, write acknowledged, leader's disk fails, record gone. Secondary: retries=MAX_INT is bounded by delivery.timeout.ms (2 minutes) and by the process staying alive — a producer that dies with records in its accumulator loses them, which is why the transactional-outbox pattern exists at all.

6.4 Committing on failure converts every processing error into silent data loss, and pins lag at zero so nothing ever alerts. Smallest scenario: the fraud scoring service returns 503 for 90 seconds; every scan in that window is logged, committed and never scored. Violated: at-least-once processing — and worse, the one metric anyone watches has been engineered to lie. The fix is a dead-letter topic (§11.7): commit the offset and preserve the record somewhere retriable.

6.5 Two flaws. (a) "After replay" is doing a lot of work: the map is correct only once the consumer has read to the log end offset of every partition of the topic. Serve traffic while still catching up and you answer with a partially built map — no error, just missing and stale parcels. (b) Tombstones: the replay must treat a null value as remove, and the process must finish within delete.retention.ms of a tombstone being written, or it can miss the deletion entirely (the tombstone is gone, the old value may be gone too — fine — but a long-running replay that started before the tombstone and cached the old value keeps it). Compaction guarantees "at least the latest value per key"; it does not guarantee your reader reached the end, and it does not interpret nulls for you.

6.6 EOS covers Kafka-read → Kafka-write → offset-commit atomically. The payment API is none of those. Smallest scenario: transaction includes the API call; the call succeeds; the process dies before commitTransaction(); the transaction aborts; the record is reprocessed; the card is charged twice. Violated: nothing Kafka promised — the promise was misread. The fix is an idempotency key at the payment API.

6.7 Lag misses every failure that is not about speed. Three counterexamples, each real: §11.6's silent skip makes lag fall to zero; §11.3's ISR shrink to one leaves lag perfectly flat while durability is gone; an unclean leader election deletes committed records without moving lag at all. Lag is a superb business metric and a poor infrastructure one. Pair it with UnderMinIsrPartitionCount, and alert on lag as a fraction of retention rather than in absolute records.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 (a) Next ten minutes. Confirm the shape first — one partition stuck, all others fine — from per-partition lag. Then choose between two levers and say which you are pulling and why: skip the record by advancing the group's committed offset past it (kafka-consumer-groups --reset-offsets --to-offset N+1 for that one partition, consumers stopped), recording the offset and the parcel id so it can be recovered later; or deploy a config that catches the deserialisation error and routes it aside. The first is faster and loses one record's processing; the second is slower and loses none. Under business pressure, skip, but write down the coordinates before you skip — an offset you did not record is a record you cannot recover.

(b) The rebuild. An error-handling boundary in the consumer: catch on the processing of a single record; produce the raw record, its headers, its (topic, partition, offset) and the exception to parcel-scans.DLQ; commit the offset; continue. Then: a metric dlq_records_total with an alert on any non-zero rate (a DLQ nobody watches is a delete with extra steps), a retention on the DLQ long enough to reprocess (7–30 days), and a documented replay path — a consumer of the DLQ that can re-inject after a fix. What it still does not cover: a record that deserialises fine but is semantically wrong, which will not throw and will silently produce bad output; and a poison record that kills the process before your handler runs (an OOM on a huge payload), which needs max.partition.fetch.bytes and a size limit at the producer.

7.2 Within an hour: processing time per batch crosses max.poll.interval.ms (5 min); the coordinator evicts a consumer mid-batch; its partitions move to peers already saturated; those slow further and are evicted in turn; under the eager protocol each eviction stops the entire group. Throughput collapses toward zero while lag climbs, and every eviction reprocesses uncommitted records, so the downstream API — already the problem — receives duplicate load. That is the rebalance storm (§11.8), and note its shape: a downstream latency problem became a Kafka outage through a timeout, not through Kafka.

Three independent breaks: (1) reduce max.poll.records so one batch is completable well inside the interval — the smallest, safest, fastest change; (2) switch to CooperativeStickyAssignor / group.protocol=consumer so an eviction stops two partitions instead of all of them; (3) add a circuit breaker and bounded concurrency around the downstream call so its degradation shows up as lag rather than as eviction. Do (1) first: it is a config change, it takes effect on restart, and it directly removes the trigger. Then (3), because it is the actual cause. Then (2), because it reduces the blast radius of the next one.

7.3 Split the guarantee honestly.

Kafka's part: at-least-once delivery of the scan to your consumer, in order per parcel. That is all. Kafka transactions do not help, because the charge is outside Kafka.

Your part: an idempotency key derived deterministically from the record — (topic, partition, offset) is tempting but wrong if you ever replay from a different topic; prefer a business-derived key such as payment:{parcel_id}:{invoice_id}. Send it to the payment API as its idempotency key (every serious payment API supports one). If yours does not, keep the dedup state yourself: a table keyed by the idempotency key, written in the same database transaction as the record of the charge, with a claim row inserted before calling the API (idempotency's claim/settled shape).

Timeout without an answer: this is the case the whole design exists for. Do not retry blindly and do not assume failure. Re-send with the same idempotency key — the API either performs it once or returns the original result. If your own table holds a claim in RUNNING state with no outcome, reconcile by querying the payment provider for that key before deciding.

Lifetime of the dedup state: at least as long as any record can be replayed — so retention.ms of the topic, plus the longest outage you would replay after, plus a margin. Sizing it as "24 hours" because that felt like a lot is the usual mistake; a topic with 7-day retention needs at least 7 days of dedup state.

What remains uncovered: a duplicate produced upstream — the same physical scan published twice as two different records with different ids — which no consumer-side key can detect. That has to be solved where the scan is created.

7.4 (a) min.insync.replicas=1: producers notice nothing. Latency may improve. Consumers notice nothing. UnderReplicatedPartitions goes non-zero and UnderMinIsrPartitionCount stays at zero. You are one further failure away from losing acknowledged data and no user-visible signal says so.

(b) min.insync.replicas=2: if the ISR drops to 1, producers fail with NOT_ENOUGH_REPLICAS and retry; if the disk fills for long enough, produce requests start timing out and the upstream service feels it. Consumers keep reading committed data normally.

Prefer to be paged for (b). It is loud, it is bounded, it does not lose data, and the remedy (free disk, or restore the replica) is the same in both cases. In (a) the page you get is the one three weeks later titled "we cannot reconcile January".

7.5 Sketch: producers in both regions write to their local cluster; MirrorMaker 2 (or Cluster Linking) replicates topics asynchronously to the other region; consumers run in the primary and are started in the secondary on failover.

The hard part has nothing to do with the data: offsets are not the same in the two clusters. A mirrored topic is re-produced into the target cluster, so partition 3, offset 918,204 in the source is some other offset in the target. A consumer failing over cannot simply resume from its committed offset. MirrorMaker 2 maintains an offset-translation topic to map them approximately, and approximately is the operative word — the failover consumer typically restarts at a translated offset that is behind where it was, and reprocesses. So the real requirement of cross-region failover is not replication: it is that every consumer be idempotent, because failover means replay. Everything else is plumbing.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 Consumers and partitions. Steady-state need: 40,000 ÷ 800 = 50 consumers. Catch-up requirement: 6 h of backlog is 6 × 3,600 × 40,000 ≈ 864 M records, to be cleared within 1 h while live traffic continues. Required rate = 40,000 + (864,000,000 ÷ 3,600) = 40,000 + 240,000 = 280,000 records/s → 280,000 ÷ 800 = 350 consumer instances → partitions ≥ 350. Round to 384 (a friendly multiple for even assignment across brokers) and note the consequence honestly: you are provisioning 384 partitions for a 50-consumer steady state because of a recovery-time requirement, not a throughput one. If the business will accept a 4-hour catch-up instead of 1, the number drops to ~120 partitions and the cluster gets much cheaper — that is a conversation to have before creating the topic, because it cannot be had afterwards.

Storage. 40,000 × 500 B = 20 MB/s = 1.73 TB/day; × 3 days = 5.2 TB logical; × RF 3 = 15.6 TB on disk; plus 30% headroom ≈ 20 TB, before compression (zstd on JSON-ish payloads typically 3–5×, so the real figure is often 4–7 TB — but size the disks for the uncompressed case if you cannot verify the ratio).

Least confident, and how to measure. (1) 800 records/s per consumer — it is whatever the slowest downstream call allows, and it is usually measured on an idle staging system with a warm cache. Measure it by replaying a real hour of production traffic through one instance against a production-like downstream, at p99 not mean. (2) 500 B average record — measure directly from an existing topic (bytes-in ÷ records-in from broker metrics), not from a schema, because headers, keys and batch overhead are easy to forget.

8.2 Five rules, each expensive to change later:

  1. Partition count is set at creation using §13.3's arithmetic, with a stated catch-up requirement. Wrong → either a permanent throughput ceiling or a partition explosion; repairing it means a new topic and a dual-read migration.
  2. Every topic declares a key, and the key is the ordering unit. Wrong → no ordering where the business needed it, or a hot partition; repairing it means re-keying, i.e. a new topic.
  3. RF=3, min.insync.replicas=2, acks=all on anything whose loss is noticeable; unclean.leader.election.enable=false everywhere. Wrong → silent loss; unrepairable after the fact.
  4. Retention is declared as a recovery-time budget, in writing ("we can be broken for N days and still catch up"). Wrong → §11.6's silent skip; unrepairable, because the data is gone.
  5. Every topic has a registered schema with a compatibility mode, and no producer writes without one. Wrong → poison records across every consumer of that topic; repair is a DLQ plus a coordinated redeploy of teams you have never met.

A sixth, softer one worth having: naming and ownership — a topic with no named owning team becomes undeletable within a year, because nobody can prove nobody is reading it.

8.3 For "just Kafka": the data is already there, a compacted topic gives you the latest value per parcel with bounded storage, and it removes a database and a sync path (one source of truth instead of two that can disagree).

Against: the query is 5,000 point lookups per second by key, and Kafka has no key lookup at all. Serving it from Kafka means replaying the entire compacted topic into memory or RocksDB on every instance at startup (200 M rows — minutes to hours of startup time, and a large per-instance memory or disk footprint), then keeping it current. You have not removed a database; you have written one, without indexes, backups, or ad-hoc queries.

Recommendation: keep the table; feed it from Kafka (cqrs's read model, change-data-capture's sink). Kafka carries changes; the database answers questions. That is the log–table duality used correctly rather than as a dare.

The measurement that would change my mind: the actual access pattern. If those 5,000 QPS turn out to be a small number of services each wanting the whole state periodically, or if the working set is small enough to sit in memory per instance and startup time is acceptable, then a Kafka Streams state store (which is exactly this design, done properly, with standby replicas) becomes the better answer.

8.4 Per consumer group: lag per partition, alerted on (i) lag ÷ retention > 25% — page, because you are approaching irreversible loss; (ii) lag rising monotonically for 15 minutes — warn, capacity or downstream degradation; (iii) lag == 0 for a group that normally has some, and a drop of more than 90% in one scrape — warn, because that is the §11.6 signature. Action for (i): stop the bleeding by raising retention first (it is a topic config and it is instant), then fix the consumer. For (iii): compare the group's committed offset with the partition's earliest offset before assuming good news.

Per cluster: UnderMinIsrPartitionCount > 0 — page; action: find the lagging/dead replica, and do not restart another broker until it is zero. UnderReplicatedPartitions > 0 for 15 min — warn; action: identify whether it is a slow disk, a GC problem, or a dead broker. ActiveControllerCount ≠ 1 — page immediately; a cluster with zero controllers accepts no metadata changes and one with two is a split brain. OfflinePartitionsCount > 0 — page; a partition with no leader is a hard outage for its producers.

Per broker: RequestHandlerAvgIdlePercent < 30% — warn; you are on the queueing cliff and latency is about to move everywhere at once. Disk free vs projected retention — warn at 7 days of headroom.

The silent one that no other metric covers: UnderMinIsrPartitionCount. Lag, throughput and latency all look better during the failure it detects.

8.5 Without a high watermark: leader A has records 0–104 replicated and writes 105 locally. A consumer reads 105 and acts on it — say it texts a customer. A dies before any follower fetches. B becomes leader with LEO 105 and assigns offset 105 to a different record. The consumer has now (i) processed a record that no longer exists anywhere, and (ii) holds a committed offset of 106 that skips the new record at 105. For downstream systems to remain correct, every consumer would have to treat every read as provisional until some later signal confirmed the record's durability — i.e. every consumer would have to implement its own two-phase commit against the broker's replication state. The high watermark is the cheap alternative: publish nothing until it cannot be unpublished. It costs one round trip of staleness and removes an entire class of impossible-to-write application code.

8.6 Three things that depend on offsets being permanent positions in one log:

  1. Committed offsets. A group's entire state is "next offset per partition". If records could move between partitions, every committed offset in __consumer_offsets would become a pointer to something else.
  2. Replica truncation and leader epochs. Failover works by asking "where did epoch 5 end?" and truncating to that offset. Both the question and the answer are meaningless if offsets do not identify records.
  3. Compaction, and the idempotent producer's sequence numbers. "Later offset wins" is how compaction decides which value survives; "sequence n+1 must follow n" is how duplicate batches are detected. Both are ordering arguments over a fixed sequence.

Add the sparse index and sendfile: serving a consumer is "send bytes X–Y of this file", which is only possible because the file is the log and position is identity.

So the inconvenience — a frozen partition count, a key routing that breaks when you change it, no way to move a hot partition's records — buys: a consumer's state being one integer, replication being a byte-range copy, failover being an integer comparison, and reads being a kernel call with no deserialisation. Kafka is fast and simple because the offset means exactly one thing, and it is willing to be inflexible to keep it that way. That is the trade at the bottom of the whole system, and every awkward operational rule in this lab is a payment on it.
