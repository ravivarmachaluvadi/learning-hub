Consistent hashing — how to spread data over machines without moving it all every time

Tell a friend: Priya's coat is in a school cloakroom with four attendants, Amara, Bao, Chidi and Dev, and there is no list and nobody to ask. Every child finds their attendant by a rule, and the first rule anyone thinks of, "divide by how many attendants there are", sends three-quarters of the children to the wrong desk the moment Dev goes home sick. The fix is to take the number of attendants out of the rule: stand each attendant at a fixed door on a circular corridor, and let every child walk clockwise from their own door until they meet one. Now Dev leaving moves only Dev's coats, and Amara, Bao and Chidi keep every coat they had. That one missing number is the whole of consistent hashing; the rest of this lab is what you must add so the shares stay even, a dead attendant's coats spread out instead of landing on one neighbour, and copies end up on different machines.

Watch it before you read it: The Clockwise Rule, also saved as visualization.html. Section 9 says what to click; section 19 says how the lab was built.

1. Why does this problem exist at all?

In one breath: When your data no longer fits on one machine, every part of your system has to know which machine holds each item, without asking anyone. The obvious rule for that works perfectly until the number of machines changes. Then it sends almost everything to the wrong place at once.

You have more data than fits on one machine, or more traffic than one machine can serve. So you buy several and split the work between them.

That creates a question straight away. It has to be answered millions of times a second, by every part of your system, without anybody asking anybody: given this item, which machine has it? Every one of your twenty application servers must reach the same answer, alone, instantly, from nothing but the item's name.

There is an obvious rule that does exactly this. It costs nothing, and it spreads the work perfectly evenly. It is what everybody writes. And it is right up until the moment the number of machines changes. That means your first deploy, your first autoscale, or your first hardware failure.

When that happens, the obvious rule sends almost every item to a different machine at once. If those machines were a cache, your cache is now empty. Your database is receiving your full unfiltered traffic with nothing in front of it. That is not a slowdown. It is an outage, and it happens at the exact moment you were trying to add capacity.

This lab is about why that happens and what to do instead.

2. The cloakroom: how does every child find their coat with nobody to ask?

That constraint is the whole problem. Remember it: no lookup, no asking, everyone computes the same answer alone.

3. What does a reasonable person try first?

In one breath: Count the letters in your name, divide by four, and the remainder is your attendant. It is fast, it needs no list, and it spreads the coats evenly. There is nothing wrong with it. Yet.

The school's rule:

Count the letters in your name. Divide by the number of attendants. The remainder is your attendant.

Priya has 5 letters. 5 divided by 4 leaves 1, so Priya's coat is with attendant number 1, Bao. Every child can do this in their head. It needs no list, and the coats spread evenly across all four.

This is a genuinely good rule. It is fast, it needs no memory, and it is perfectly balanced. Every property you would ask for, it has.

🔮 Predict: Is there any property this rule lacks? Think about what the cloakroom will look like at lunchtime, not just at nine o'clock.

Reveal At nine o'clock it lacks nothing. If you said "it is unbalanced" or "it is slow", both are false, and experiment 6 in section 10 shows it: the remainder rule is the fastest scheme in this lab and among the best balanced. The missing property only shows itself when the *number of attendants changes*. That is the next section.

4. Why does the remainder rule break when Dev goes home?

In one breath: Dev goes home sick, so everyone divides by three instead of four. Now three-quarters of the children walk to the wrong attendant, even though only a quarter of the coats were ever Dev's.

Dev goes home sick at lunchtime. Now there are three attendants, so everyone divides by 3 instead of 4.

Priya: 5 divided by 3 leaves 2. That is attendant number 2, Chidi. But Priya's coat is with Bao, because Bao is who the old rule chose this morning.

🔮 Predict: 500 children, one attendant of four goes home. How many children find their coat with the attendant their new calculation points at?

Reveal

About one in four. The lab's Java, run over 200,000 items, prints:

4 machines -> 3 :  the remainder rule moved 75.0% of items

| What changed | Coats sent to a new attendant | What it means | |---|---|---| | 4 attendants → 3 | 75.0% | three-quarters of the cloakroom is now unfindable | | 100 attendants → 99 | 99.0% | nearly everything moves, to redistribute the 1% that had to |

If you said "three in four find it", you were counting the coats Dev never held and assuming they stayed findable. They did not. Notice what makes this so much worse than it first sounds: Dev only ever held a quarter of the coats. Losing Dev should have disturbed a quarter of the children. Instead it disturbed three-quarters. The extra half is children whose coats never moved at all, and who are now looking in the wrong place.

It gets worse as the school grows, not better. At 100 attendants, losing one moves 99.0% of the coats, to redistribute the 1% that genuinely had to move.

Sit with what just went wrong, because it is not "the rule is inaccurate". The rule is perfectly accurate. The problem is what it depends on.

"The remainder after dividing by the number of attendants" makes every child's answer depend on a fact about the whole cloakroom: how many attendants are on duty. Change that one number and every answer changes at once. That includes the overwhelming majority of children whose own situation did not change in any way.

Engineers have a name for this rule. You will meet it everywhere, including in this lab's code and experiment output: modulo hashing, after the remainder operation at its heart. In words: turn the item's name into a number, divide by the number of machines, keep the remainder. Written as code it is hash(key) % numberOfMachines, the key's number divided by the machine count, keep the remainder. The % sign means "the remainder of".

That is the actual disease. It is worth stating in a form you can carry to other problems: a rule whose output depends on a global count will produce a global change whenever the count changes. Any rule with the total in it has this, no matter how clever the rest of it is.

5. What do we actually need?

In one breath: Keep the part that works: every child decides alone. Fix the part that does not: when one attendant leaves, only that attendant's coats may be affected.

Stated plainly, before any solution:

Every child must work out their attendant alone, from their own name and the list of who is on duty. No queue, no lookup, no asking.

And when one attendant joins or leaves, only that attendant's share of the coats may be affected, roughly one in four of them. No other child's answer may change.

The first clause is what the remainder rule already gives us, and we must not lose it. The second is the one it fails. Everything that follows is the pursuit of both at once.

6. What idea fixes it?

In one breath: Rebuild the cloakroom as a circular corridor of numbered doors. Each attendant stands at a fixed door. Each coat has a door number. Walk clockwise until you meet an attendant. The number of attendants is nowhere in that rule.

Rebuild the cloakroom as a circular corridor with a thousand numbered doors, 0 to 999, running all the way round.

  • Every coat gets a door number from the child's name. Some fixed calculation that always gives the same number for the same name.
  • Every attendant stands at some door, at a spot decided the same way from their own name. Amara at 91, Bao at 305, Chidi at 502, Dev at 780.
  • The rule: walk clockwise from your coat's door number until you meet an attendant. That attendant has your coat.

Priya's name gives door 340. She walks 341, 342, ... and meets Chidi at 502. Chidi has her coat.

🔮 Predict: Dev goes home again. He stood at door 780. Which children have to walk somewhere new, and where do they end up?

Reveal Only the children whose door numbers fall between Chidi at 502 and Dev at 780. They keep walking past 780, run off the end at 999, come back in at 0, and reach Amara at 91. Priya at 340 is not one of them; she still meets Chidi. If you said "everyone past 780 as well", look again: a coat at 900 walked to Amara *before* Dev left too, because Dev at 780 was already behind it.

Now Dev goes home. Dev stood at 780, so the coats affected are exactly those whose numbers fall between Chidi at 502 and Dev at 780. They now keep walking past 780 and reach Amara at 91, wrapping round the end. Every other child's walk is unchanged, because nothing between the other attendants moved.

Look at the rule again and notice what is missing from it: how many attendants there are. It does not appear. There is nothing global left to change.

That is the whole invention. Everything else in this document is consequences.

7. What do engineers call all these things?

In one breath: The door-number calculation is a hash function. The corridor is the hash space, and bent into a circle it is the ring. Attendants are nodes, coats are keys, and "the first attendant clockwise" is the successor. You have already felt every one of them.

You have earned all of these. Each names something you have already felt in the corridor. Walk it once more, naming things as we pass them.

The calculation that turns a name into a door number

The thing that turned "Priya" into door 340 is a hash function. In words: a calculation that takes any input and gives back a fixed-size number. For our purposes it must have two properties. It must be deterministic: same input, same output, on every machine, forever. And it must be uniform: outputs spread evenly over the whole range, with no clumping.

Why does the term exist separately from, say, encryption? To separate the two jobs a hash can do. Cryptographic hashes promise you cannot work backwards or find collisions. We need neither of those. We need only spread and determinism. That is why this world uses fast non-cryptographic hashes like MurmurHash rather than SHA-256.

In this lab's code, src/Hashing.java has hash64("user:100000"), which always produces the same 64-bit number, on every machine, in every process.

One trap, and the lab shows it happening: uniformity is a property of the hash and of the range you use it in. Section 11 F6 shows a perfectly good hash destroying a cluster because its output was 32 bits wide and the space it was being placed into was 64.

The corridor, and why it is a circle

All the door numbers, laid out in a circle, are the hash space, treated as a ring. In words: the full range of numbers the hash function can produce, with the largest value declared to wrap round to the smallest. For a 64-bit hash that range is 0 to 2⁶⁴−1, two to the power sixty-four, minus one.

Why name it? Because it is the thing that stays fixed. The number of machines changes constantly; the hash space never does. Putting both the items and the machines into that one unchanging space is the substitution that solves the problem, and the ring is that space.

In the code, Ring.successorIndex binary-searches a sorted array of positions. When it runs off the end, it returns index 0. That single line is where the corridor becomes a circle.

Attendants and coats

An attendant is a node: a machine that can hold data. A coat is a key: the identifier of one item. Both are hashed into the same space, which is the point. They are the same kind of thing as far as the ring is concerned.

The words are deliberately generic. The same mechanism places cache entries on cache servers, database rows on database nodes, and live HTTP requests on backend servers. Naming them "key" and "node" keeps the mechanism separate from any one use.

The first attendant you meet

The attendant Priya meets walking clockwise is the key's successor, its clockwise owner. In words: the node whose position is the smallest one greater than or equal to the key's position, wrapping to the first node if there is none.

"Successor" is the word the original paper and every implementation uses. It names a relative relationship rather than an absolute slot. That is precisely the difference from the remainder rule. A key does not have a slot number that must be recomputed. It has a neighbour.

Concretely: key at 340, nodes at 91/305/502/780, so the owner is the node at 502. That is Priya and Chidi again.

The share of coats that have to move

When Dev went home, some share of the coats had to find a new attendant. That share is the remapping fraction. In words: the proportion of keys whose owner changes when the membership changes.

It is the number the whole field is optimising, and it has a floor that people forget. Suppose there are some number of machines, call that number n, and one more joins. For all n+1 machines to share the keys evenly, at least one share in n+1 of the keys must end up somewhere new. That movement is not waste. It is the job. Everything above the floor is waste, and that gap is what to measure.

Symbol In words Example

1/(n+1) the floor: one share in n+1 must move so the newcomer gets a fair share 1/11, which is 9.1%
hash(key) % n the remainder rule: the key's hash, divided by the machine count, keep the remainder 5 % 4 = 1, so Bao

8. What two pictures should you carry in your head?

In one breath: The simple picture is the corridor. The engineering picture is a sorted array of numbers inside every client, searched with binary search. They are the same thing, and there are four places the corridor picture lies to you.

The simple model (carry this around)

A circular corridor of numbered doors. Attendants stand at fixed spots decided by their names. Every coat has a door number decided by its owner's name. Walk clockwise until you meet an attendant.

The engineering model (this is what actually runs)

Read it top to bottom. It is Priya's walk, done by a client program.

flowchart TD
  subgraph client [Inside every client, independently]
    K["key<br>user:100000"] --> H["hash function<br>-> 14,382,905,163,..."]
    H --> B["binary search a sorted<br>array of node positions"]
    B --> O["the first position<br>at or after it"]
    O --> N["that position's node"]
  end
  M[["the list of machines,<br>however it reaches<br>this client"]] -.rebuilds.-> B
  • State: a sorted array of positions, and a parallel array naming which node each position belongs to. That is all. There is no server, no table, and no lookup service.
  • Lookup: hash the key, binary search, take the successor. No network involved. The cost is a binary search over all the positions, written O(log(nodes × positions)). In words: the time grows only with the logarithm of how many positions there are.
  • On a membership change: rebuild the array. Nothing else in the system is told anything.
  • The invariant everything rests on: a node's positions depend only on that node's own name, never on how many other nodes exist.

Where the story lies to you

Four places, and each one matters later:

  1. The corridor has 1,000 doors; a real ring has 2⁶⁴ positions. With a thousand doors, two children would constantly share one and you would wonder what happens. In a 64-bit space, positions are so astronomically sparse that collisions never come up. The sparseness is not a detail. It is why the hash's width is load-bearing (section 11 F6).
  2. An attendant stands in one place; a machine occupies hundreds of positions at once. The single most important refinement in section 10 has no counterpart in the story, because a person cannot be in two places. Machines can, and must.
  3. The children can see who is on duty. Real machines find out late. Whatever mechanism tells them takes time to reach everyone, and during that window different clients hold different lists and compute different owners. That is a real failure mode with real consequences (section 11 F4), and the story simply does not contain it.
  4. Coats do not walk to their new attendant by themselves. This is the big one. Consistent hashing decides where an item belongs. It does not move anything. For a cache you do not need it to: the item is simply absent and gets fetched again. For stored data, something else entirely must notice the change and copy the data. That machinery is a separate system with its own failure modes (section 11 F8).

10. How do you build it, and where does it break on the way?

In one breath: Build exactly what section 6 describes, with real numbers, and watch it fail five times. Each fix is a scar: many positions per machine, distinct machines for copies, more positions for bigger machines, an optional hard cap. Then meet two rivals that skip the ring entirely.

Let us build exactly what section 6 describes, and watch it fail. The four attendants become four machines, named by their initials. Amara is machine A, Bao is B, Chidi is C, Dev is D.

v1: what does the rule look like with real numbers?

Four machines. Hash each machine's name to get its position:

hash("A") = 91      hash("B") = 305     hash("C") = 502     hash("D") = 780

Sorted, that is the ring: [91:A, 305:B, 502:C, 780:D]. In words: Amara at 91, Bao at 305, Chidi at 502, Dev at 780, exactly where they stood in the corridor. Now place some keys. Each key is a shopping cart, and cart:17, at 340, is Priya's.

cart:31 954 (none, so wrap to the first) A

cart:31 is the interesting one. Its position, 954, is past every machine, so the walk runs off the end of the array and comes back to the start. That wrap is one line of code. It is what makes this a circle rather than a line. Without it, every key above 780 would have no owner at all. The test ring_wraps_around_past_the_top_of_the_number_space pins it.

🔮 Predict: A fifth attendant, Esha (machine E), arrives and stands at door 400. Which of the four keys above change owner, and where do they go?

If you said `cart:45` moves too, check: 610 walks to 780 both before and after, because Esha stood *behind* it, not in front of it. Nothing can move between A, B, C and D, and the paragraph after the table says why that is not luck.

Now add machine E at position 400. The ring becomes [91:A, 305:B, 400:E, 502:C, 780:D]. Recompute:

key position owner before owner now
cart:17 340 C E ← moved
cart:22 88 A A
cart:31 954 A A

One key moved, and it moved onto the new machine. Look at why that is not luck. Inserting a position into the ring can only put a new candidate in front of some keys. It cannot reorder any of the machines that were already there. So no key can be pushed from one existing machine to another.

That property has a name, monotonicity, and it is the formal statement of what consistent hashing guarantees: adding a node only ever moves keys onto the newcomer, never sideways between existing nodes. The middle column is the whole lab in one number. Modulo's 163,679 sideways moves achieved nothing whatsoever. Those keys were fine where they were.

Break 1: why is the load so uneven?

🔮 Predict: eight machines, one position each. Eight random points on a circle. Do they cut it into eight roughly equal slices?

Reveal

No, and it is much worse than "roughly". Experiment 2, 200,000 keys over 8 machines, where a fair share is 25,000:

positions       busiest     quietest      imbalance
1               118,515        1,657          4.74x

The busiest machine holds 4.74 times its fair share and the quietest holds under 7% of one. If you said "roughly equal, give or take", you were picturing evenly spaced points. Random points are not evenly spaced. And no hash function can fix this, because nothing is wrong with the hash. Random points on a circle simply leave gaps of wildly differing size, and the gap is the machine's share.

Back in the corridor: Amara at 91, Bao at 305, Chidi at 502, Dev at 780. Bao's stretch runs from door 92 to door 305, which is 214 doors. Chidi's runs from 306 to 502, which is 197. Dev's runs from 503 to 780, which is 278. Amara's, with the wrap, runs from 781 round to 91, which is 311. Nobody chose those shares. The names did.

The fix: put each machine at many positions instead of one. In the story, Bao no longer stands at one door. Bao stands at two hundred doors at once, scattered round the corridor, and a coat is Bao's if the first door it reaches is any of them. Machine A appears at hash("A#0"), hash("A#1"), ... hash("A#199"): two hundred unrelated points scattered right round the circle. Its share is now the sum of two hundred independent gaps. The relative variation of a sum of many independent values shrinks as the square root of how many there are. Written 1/√n, one over root n: with 100 positions the wobble is about a tenth of what it was with one.

These extra positions are called virtual nodes (or vnodes, or tokens, depending whose documentation you are reading). In words: extra positions for one machine, so its share is an average rather than a lottery. Experiment 2 sweeps the count:

Break 2: a machine dies. Who inherits its keys?

Virtual nodes were introduced above to fix steady-state balance. They turn out to fix something more dangerous, and this is the reason that actually matters.

🔮 Predict: eight machines, one position each, and one of them fails. Its keys have to go somewhere. Do they spread over the seven survivors, or land on one of them?

Reveal

All on one, and it is chosen by an accident of the circle rather than by who has room. Experiment 3:

n7 was the quietest machine in the cluster and is now by far the busiest, in one step, with no warning. If you said "they spread out", you were thinking of the many-positions ring. With one position each, a dead machine has exactly one clockwise neighbour, and that neighbour gets everything.

Now think about what happens if the cluster was near capacity. The inheritor is not chosen by who can cope, so it may well be a machine that cannot. When it falls over, its entire share lands on its single neighbour, which is now taking three machines' worth. That is a cascading failure with a concrete mechanism, and it is a real way clusters die.

A uniform 10–19% bump on everyone, instead of +483% on one victim. This, not the balance, is the argument for virtual nodes that would survive a design review.

Break 3: where do the copies go?

One machine holding the only copy of a key is not a design anyone ships. In the corridor: if Chidi loses Priya's coat, Priya has no coat. So you want each coat with several attendants. Engineers call the number of copies the replication factor. In words: how many distinct machines hold a copy of each key. It is written r, so r = 3 means three machines each hold the key. The obvious extension: take the next r positions clockwise.

🔮 Predict: the ring has 200 positions per machine. You take the next 3 positions clockwise and call them your three replicas. What is wrong with that?

Reveal

With 200 positions each, adjacent positions are frequently the same physical machine. So all three "copies" can land on one box. Your data reads as replicated, is not, and you find out the day that machine dies. If you said "nothing, three positions are three places", remember that Bao now stands at two hundred doors, and two of the next three doors may both be Bao.

The fix is one line: walk clockwise collecting distinct nodes, skipping further positions belonging to a machine already chosen. It is the !out.contains(n) check in Ring.owners, and BrokenRing bug 2 removes it so you can watch the failure.

Real systems go one step further than distinctness, and it is worth knowing why. Three distinct machines in the same rack share a power supply and a top-of-rack switch. They fail together, and your replication factor of 3 is really 1. Cassandra and DynamoDB therefore make the walk rack- and datacentre-aware: keep walking until you have three machines in three different failure domains. Consistent hashing gives you the ordering. What counts as "different enough" is yours to define.

Break 4: what if the machines are not the same size?

You bought five machines two years ago and three more last month with four times the memory. Equal shares would waste most of the new hardware. In the corridor: Dev is a much faster attendant than Bao, and should hold more coats.

One more thing: do you need the ring at all?

Everything above builds the ring, because the ring is what the libraries implement and what people mean by "consistent hashing". But two other algorithms give you the same core guarantee, and one of them is simpler than anything on this page.

Rendezvous hashing, also called highest random weight, is from Thaler & Ravishankar. It was published in 1996, a year before the consistent hashing paper, and is far less famous. In the corridor: instead of standing at doors, every attendant rolls a die for every coat, and the coat goes to whoever rolled highest for it. The whole algorithm is: for a given key, score every machine by hash(key + machine), the hash of the key and the machine's name joined together, and give the key to the highest scorer.

That is it. No circle, no sorted array, no virtual positions, no tuning knob. And it inherits the properties you spent this section earning. A machine leaving only affects the keys it was winning. Because every survivor is re-scored independently, those keys spread perfectly across all of them rather than piling onto one successor. Replication is free too: take the top r scorers instead of the top 1.

Jump consistent hash (Lamping & Veach, Google, 2014). About twenty lines, literally zero memory, and the best balance of anything here. It has one hard limitation: it maps a key to a bucket number, so buckets are positions in an ordered list and you can only add or remove at the end. There is no way to express "machine 3 died". In the corridor: attendants are numbered in a line, a new one may join at the end and the last one may leave, but Chidi, in the middle, is never allowed to go home.

11. What goes wrong in production, and what survives each time?

In one breath: Nine things go wrong. The ring prevents some, shrinks some, and does nothing about others. In every one of them the placement stays correct. What breaks is balance, capacity, agreement, or data that never moved.

Each failure below is told first as something that happens in the cloakroom, then as what you would see on a real cluster. The comparison table at the end of the section lines them all up.

F1: what if there are too few positions?

Run java src/Experiments.java 2, or drag the positions slider to 1 in the visualization.

In the corridor with one door per attendant, Amara owns 311 doors and Chidi 197. Nobody chose that. The names did. On the cluster it is section 10 break 1: few random points cut a circle into wildly unequal gaps, and the gap is the share. The busiest machine sits at 4.74× its fair share.

Correctness is untouched. Every key still has exactly one well-defined owner and every client agrees on it. What you lose is your ability to size the cluster, because you must provision for the worst machine, not the average one.

On call, watch per-node key count or request rate as a ratio to the mean, not in absolute terms. An alert on max/mean > 1.3, the busiest node at more than 1.3 times the average, catches both a bad position count and the hot key in F5.

F2: a machine dies and one neighbour inherits all of it. Why?

Run java src/Experiments.java 3, or the third scenario in the visualization.

Dev goes home with one door each, and every one of his coats walks to Amara. On the cluster it is section 10 break 2. With one position each, the whole share lands on a single successor chosen by geometry rather than by capacity. The run shows +483% on a machine that was the quietest in the cluster.

Correctness again survives. What is lost is the assumption that failures are independent. One machine's death is now a targeted load spike on one specific other machine, and if that one falls over the pattern repeats.

This is why nobody runs one position per node. It is also why you should test a node failure under load rather than at rest. The failure mode is a capacity event, not an availability one, and it is invisible on an idle cluster.

F3: what is the miss storm when you add capacity?

🔮 Predict: a 10-machine cache is running at 92% hit ratio. You add an 11th machine mid-traffic. What does your database experience in the next few seconds?

Reveal

Under the ring, almost nothing. Under the remainder rule, close to your full unfiltered request rate. If you said "a small dip either way", the remainder rule's dip is not small: it is the cache going cold. Experiment 5 tracks hit ratio in windows of 25,000 requests:

Modulo's hit ratio collapses from 92% back to 59%. The ring dips two points. Both then climb: modulo 59, 85, 89, 90, 91, 92; the ring 90, 93, 94, 95, 95, 96. The warm-up before the add is the same for both: 55, 85, 88, 90, 91, 92.

In the corridor: a new attendant, Esha, arrives with an empty rail. Every child whose walk now ends at Esha's desk finds no coat there, and every one of them goes to the office for a replacement at the same moment. On the cluster: every key that moves is a cache entry now sitting on a machine that does not have it, so the next request for it misses. Nothing is incorrect. A cache miss returns the right answer. What is lost is the protection your cache was providing, at the exact moment you were adding capacity, which is a particularly cruel piece of timing.

Note that both rows recover within a minute. The storm is a transient, which is what makes it dangerous. It is over before you finish reading the alert, it vanishes into a five-minute average, and the thing it took down was your database rather than your cache. This is caching's cold-cache problem with a specific trigger: your own deploy.

F4: what if two clients disagree about who is alive?

Run java src/Experiments.java 9.

In the corridor: Esha has just arrived, but only half the children have been told. Those who know walk to Esha. Those who do not walk past her to Chidi. Two children with the same door number now go to two different desks. On the cluster: a machine is added; client A has heard, client B has not yet.

Membership never changes everywhere at the same instant. It propagates, by gossip, by a config push, by a watch firing. During that window your clients hold different lists and therefore compute different owners.

Every individual client remains self-consistent. What is lost is agreement between clients, so a write may land on one machine while a read looks on another.

This is the failure that makes consistent hashing an operational win rather than merely an efficient one, and it is the one people miss. You do not control the length of the propagation window. You do control the blast radius, and it is exactly the remapping fraction. For a cache the cost is extra misses. For stored data a write to the wrong owner is invisible until the reader who needed it comes looking. Section 15 covers the alternative design that removes this window entirely by accepting a coordinator.

F5: what if one key gets all the traffic?

In the corridor: one coat, and five hundred children asking for it. It hangs on one hook, with one attendant, and hiring more attendants does not help. On the cluster: consistent hashing distributes keys. A single key lives on one machine. If one key is 30% of your traffic, 30% of your traffic is on one machine, and adding machines does nothing at all.

Correctness is fine. Horizontal scalability for that workload is gone.

You met this in distributed-rate-limiting as the hot-key problem and again in redis as F8, and the remedies are the same. Split the key into key:0..key:9 and combine. Put a short-TTL local cache in front of it. Or route by a different dimension. Worth saying plainly: no placement algorithm on this page helps with a hot key. They all agree that one key has one owner, which is the thing hurting you.

F6: what if the hash is narrower than the space you put it in?

F7: what if two client libraries build different rings?

In the corridor: the Java children and the Python children were given different instructions for turning a name into a door number. Each group is internally consistent, and each group looks for coats where the other group never hung them. On the cluster: a client's ring is built from three things. The hash function, the string format used to name virtual positions, and the number of positions. Ours uses hash64(node + "#" + i): the machine's name, a hash sign and the position's index, all hashed together. memcached's ketama uses MD5 over node + "-" + i and takes four positions from each digest. Neither is more correct. They are simply different, and two clients that disagree on any of the three compute different owners for the same key.

Each client is internally consistent and confidently wrong about the other. This is F4 made permanent instead of transient.

This genuinely happens when a polyglot fleet uses one memcached cluster from a Java service and a Python service with libraries that are not ketama-compatible. The symptom is a persistently poor hit ratio with no other explanation, because each language's clients keep re-populating keys the other language's clients cannot find. Pin the library and the settings, and check by hashing a known key list in both and diffing.

F8: the ring says where data belongs. Who moves it?

In the corridor: Esha arrives and stands at door 400. Priya's coat now belongs with Esha. It is still hanging on Chidi's rail, because coats do not walk. On the cluster: the ring is a pure function. When a machine joins, keys instantly belong somewhere new, and not one byte has been copied. For a cache that is fine. The data is simply absent and gets fetched again, which is F3. For stored data, a separate process must detect the change and stream the affected ranges.

Placement is correct immediately. Durability and completeness are not. During the transfer the new owner is missing data it is responsible for, so reads must either be served from the old owner or accept a gap.

This is what Cassandra's bootstrap/decommission and DynamoDB's partition splits actually spend their time doing. It is throttled precisely because it competes with live traffic, and it is where "we added a node and latency got worse for two hours" comes from. Consistent hashing shrank the volume from ~100% to ~1/n, about one share in n. It did not make it free.

F9: what happens when the ring itself gets big?

In the corridor: every child carries a list of every door every attendant stands at. With two hundred doors per attendant and a thousand attendants, that list is long, and every child rewrites it whenever anyone joins or leaves. On the cluster: every client holds nodes × positions entries, the machine count times the positions per machine, and re-sorts them on every membership change.

12. What is guaranteed, what is not, and what did it cost?

In one breath: The ring promises five things, and the important one is that adding a machine never moves a key sideways. It does not promise exact balance, help with a hot key, agreement during a change, or that data has moved. Against the remainder rule it loses four rows out of five, and wins the only one that matters.

What is guaranteed?

Every one of these is something you watched happen to Priya's coat.

  1. Monotonicity. Adding a node moves keys only onto that node, never between two existing nodes. Esha arriving took Priya's coat from Chidi, and nothing else changed. Experiment 4 counted exactly zero sideways moves. This is the formal property, and it is the one the remainder rule lacks.
  2. Bounded remapping. A membership change disturbs about 1/n of the keyspace, one share in n, close to the theoretical floor. Dev leaving disturbed Dev's quarter.
  3. No coordination. Every client computes ownership alone, from the membership list, with no lookup and no network call. Every child works it out in their head. Pinned by two_clients_building_the_ring_separately_agree_on_every_key, which builds two rings by adding the same machines in opposite orders and checks all 20,000 keys.
  4. Determinism. Same key, same membership, same answer. In every process, every language, forever, provided the hash and the position-naming match (F7).
  5. Spread and death-spread. With enough positions, both the steady-state load and a dead node's inherited load are shared across all survivors.

What is NOT guaranteed?

  • Exact balance. The guarantee is statistical. With 100 positions per node you get about 1.06× on the busiest. You never get 1.00×, and no setting gives you a hard ceiling. Bounded loads gives you one, at the cost of becoming stateful.
  • That a hot key is helped. It is not, at all (F5).
  • That clients agree during a membership change. They do not. The ring only bounds how much they disagree about (F4).
  • That data has actually moved. Placement is not migration (F8). Priya's coat is still on Chidi's rail.
  • Compatibility between implementations (F7).

What did it cost, against the rule it replaced?

🔮 Predict: Five rows: memory, lookup, tuning, balance, movement on change. How many does the plain remainder rule win?

Reveal Four. If you said "none, that is why we replaced it", look at the table: the remainder rule is simpler, smaller, faster and better balanced. It loses only on movement, and that is the only row that turns a deploy into an outage.
remainder rule ring what it means
memory none nodes × positions entries, per client the ring costs memory in every client
lookup one modulo binary search, ~0.14 µs at 100 nodes both are cheap; modulo is cheaper
tuning nothing to get wrong a position count you must choose the ring adds a knob
balance exact ~1.06× at 100 positions the ring is a little rougher
movement on change 99.0% 0.9% the only row anyone cares about

Read that table top to bottom and notice that the remainder rule wins four rows out of five. It is simpler, smaller, faster and better balanced. It loses one row, and that row is the only one anybody cares about, because it is the difference between a routine deploy and an outage.

That is worth internalising as a general shape. The naive solution is usually not naive about the things people check. It is naive about the thing nobody checked until it happened.

Where does this sit formally?

The ring is from Karger et al., Consistent Hashing and Random Trees (1997), written to distribute web pages across caches. That work became Akamai. Rendezvous hashing (Thaler & Ravishankar) predates it by a year and is less famous despite being simpler. The classical result is that with Θ(log n) virtual positions per node, in words "a number of positions that grows like the logarithm of the machine count", the load is within a constant factor of the mean with high probability. That is the theory behind the curve you saw in experiment 2.

🔮 Predict: Ring.owner is three lines long. Which of those lines is consistent hashing?

  6. Placements.Jump.removeNode — throws, on purpose. An algorithm that cannot express "machine 3 died" should say so loudly rather than quietly do something else.

15. What do real systems do, and which of them chose something else?

In one breath: There are two ways to keep the machine count out of the formula, and the ring is only one of them. The other is to fix the number of slots forever and let a coordinator publish who owns each slot. Redis and Kafka picked the second one.

The design space, honestly

Both designs solve section 4's disease. They pay for it differently.

consistent hashing fixed partitions + an assignment table
the idea keys map to a position; the position maps to whoever is next clockwise keys map to one of a large, never-changing number of slots; a table says which machine owns each slot
why modulo's bug is absent the machine count is not in the formula the slot count is in the formula and it never changes
who decides every client, alone a coordinator, which publishes the table
rebalancing implicit; the ring shifts explicit; move slot 4,102 from A to B
clients disagreeing mid-change yes, bounded by 1/n (F4) no — there is one authoritative table version
operator control statistical exact, and you can move one slot by hand
used by Cassandra, DynamoDB, Riak, memcached clients, Envoy, HAProxy, Akamai Redis Cluster (16,384 slots), Kafka (partitions), Elasticsearch (shards)

🔮 Predict: Redis Cluster spreads keys over many nodes and survives nodes joining. Is it using consistent hashing?

Reveal No. It computes `CRC16(key) mod 16384`, which is the remainder rule from section 3, unchanged. If you said yes, the thing to notice is what number the remainder is taken over: 16,384 slots, not the machine count. The slot count never changes, so section 4 never fires.

This is the correction to make now, while redis is fresh. Redis Cluster does not use consistent hashing. CRC16(key) mod 16384 is a plain modulo. It is safe only because 16,384 never changes, and a separate table maps slots to nodes.

Kafka is the same shape: hash(key) % numPartitions. That is why adding partitions to a Kafka topic breaks key-to-partition affinity, and why it is treated as a significant operation rather than a routine one.

The trade is coordination for control. Consistent hashing removes the coordinator, and accepts statistical balance and a disagreement window. Fixed slots accept a coordinator, and get exact placement, hand-tunable rebalancing and no ambiguity. Neither is more advanced. Pick by whether you already have a reliable coordinator. If you are running one anyway, fixed slots are usually the easier system to operate.

What does an operator actually watch?

Five signals. The first one catches three separate failures on its own.

  • Keys or requests per node as a ratio to the mean. Absolute counts hide everything. Alert above ~1.3×. It catches a bad position count (F1), a hot key (F5) and a narrow hash (F6) with one signal.
  • Nodes receiving zero traffic. The single loudest symptom of F6 and F7, and silent in every other metric.
  • Rebalance and streaming throughput and backlog during a membership change (F8).
  • Cache hit ratio around deploys. The transient in F3 disappears from any average wide enough to be convenient.
  • The ring version or membership epoch each client is on, so you can see the F4 window rather than infer it.

When does none of this help?

Four situations. In each one the ring is not the wrong choice; it is simply not the thing that is hurting you.

  • One key is your bottleneck (F5). Priya's coat is one coat, on one hook.
  • You need range queries. Hashing destroys ordering by design. That is exactly why Cassandra's default partitioner cannot do range scans on partition keys, and why HBase and CockroachDB use range partitioning instead.
  • The item's location must be chosen by something other than its identity: by geography, by tenant, by regulation.
  • You have three machines and no growth plans, and modulo's failure would cost you a brief cache miss.

Commonly confused — the one sentence that separates each:

  • Consistent hashing vs sharding. Sharding is the goal, which is splitting data across machines. Consistent hashing is one way to decide the split.
  • Consistent hashing vs Redis Cluster's hash slots. Slots are plain modulo over a count that never changes, plus a coordinator-published table. It is a different solution to the same problem, with opposite trade-offs.
  • Consistent hashing vs a hash table's buckets. A hash table rehashes everything when it grows, which is exactly the behaviour this exists to avoid.
  • Virtual nodes vs replicas. Virtual nodes are extra positions for one machine, to even out the load. Replicas are extra copies of data, for durability. They are unrelated, and confusingly some documentation calls virtual nodes "replicas".
  • Consistent hashing vs rendezvous hashing. Same guarantees. Rendezvous scores every node per lookup instead of keeping a ring, so it is simpler and O(n).
  • Hash partitioning vs range partitioning. Hashing spreads evenly and destroys ordering. Ranges preserve ordering and invite hot spots.

Consistent hashing — solutions

Try the exercises first. Rungs 7–8 are worked discussions, not single answers.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 "It makes every item's location depend on the total number of machines, so changing that total changes almost every location at once."

The trap in the question is real: modulo is not slow (0.051 µs, the fastest thing in the lab) and not unbalanced (1.07×, as good as anything else). It wins four of the five columns in §12's table. It loses one, and that one is the only one that matters.

1.2 "Adding a machine will never disturb an item that is not moving to the new machine." Or, operationally: "a machine joining cannot make any other machine's job change."

The reason to be able to say this without the algorithm is that monotonicity is what you promise, and the ring is merely how you keep the promise. Rendezvous hashing keeps the same promise with no ring at all.

1.3 Virtual nodes solve:

  1. Steady-state balance — few random points cut a circle into wildly unequal gaps (4.74× at one position each, 1.06× at a hundred).
  2. Failure blast radius — with one position each, a dead machine's entire share lands on one arbitrarily-chosen neighbour (+483% on the machine that happened to be next, which in the measured run was the quietest in the cluster).

Lead with the second. The first is a capacity-planning inconvenience; the second is the mechanism of a cascading failure, and it is the one a reviewer will not have thought of. It also answers "why not just use 10 positions?" — 10 fixes most of the balance and very little of the blast radius.

1.4 Because a lookup would put a service on the path of every single data access in your entire system. That service is now a hard dependency with your full request rate pointed at it, it must be replicated, and you have to decide what happens when it is slow — at which point you have recreated a harder version of the problem you started with.

If you did allow one lookup, you would build the fixed-slot design in §15: a table mapping slot → machine, published by a coordinator. That is a completely legitimate answer, and it is what Redis Cluster and Kafka chose. The point is that it is a different design with different costs, not a cheaper version of this one.

1.5 CRC16(key) % 16384 is modulo hashing — it has §4's disease in full. It escapes the consequences because 16,384 never changes. The count in the formula is a constant, not a property of the cluster, so no cluster event can alter any key's slot. What varies instead is a separate table saying which machine owns each slot, and moving a slot is an explicit operation.

The cost is a coordinator: something must own that table, agree on it, and publish it. Consistent hashing's entire selling point is not needing one. So the two designs are a trade of coordination for control — with a coordinator you get exact placement, hand-tunable rebalancing and no disagreement window (§11 F4); without one you get statistical balance and a window bounded by 1/n.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 Whatever the panel says — the point is that it says all four without you doing any arithmetic. If you had to guess at "which machine would own it under modulo", the page failed.

2.2 Ring.successorIndex, the line return lo == positions.length ? 0 : lo;.

Without it you get something worse than a crash: BrokenRing bug 3 clamps to the last index instead, so every key past the top of the ring is silently handed to whichever machine happens to sit at the end of the sorted array. No exception, no error, every client agreeing on the same wrong answer. And its severity depends on ring density — 79.6% of keys misplaced on a 2-machine cluster, 0.0% at 8×200 — so it hides in production shape and appears in test shape.

2.3 Placements.Modulo.owner:

int i = (int) Math.floorMod(Hashing.hash64(key), nodes.size());

nodes.size(). That is the whole thing.

2.4 You can see which machine won, and no, you could not have predicted it, and you should not be able to. The winner is decided by where hash("n3#0") happens to fall relative to hash("n5#0") — an arbitrary consequence of the hash function with no meaning at all.

That is worth noticing as a property rather than a curiosity: consistent hashing's balance is statistical, not designed. Nobody chose which machine gets the big slice, nobody can predict it, and it changes completely if you rename a machine. If you need placement you can reason about or control by hand, you want the fixed-slot design.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 Modulo 99.0%, ring 0.9%, and the floor is 1.0% — one hundredth of the keys must move for 101 machines to share them evenly.

The ring is at the floor. Modulo does a hundred times the work to achieve the same result, and the ratio gets worse as you grow: the waste is proportional to the cluster size.

3.2 One machine inherits all of them, chosen by an accident of the circle rather than by who has capacity. Measured: n3's 7,996 keys all went to n7, taking it from 1,657 keys to 9,653 — a +483% step change on the quietest machine in the cluster.

With 200 positions each, all seven survivors share it and each takes a 10–19% bump.

3.3 The ring: never. Modulo: constantly.

The reason, which is better than the number: a key's owner is the first machine clockwise. Inserting a new position can only place a new candidate in front of some keys — it cannot reorder machines that were already on the ring. So the only change possible is "onto the newcomer".

Measured: of the 181,668 keys modulo moved, 163,679 went sideways between machines that had not changed at all.

64-bit key hash: {n0=2636, n1=2385, n2=2539, n3=2378, n4=2546, n5=2509, n6=2349, n7=2658}
32-bit key hash: {n0=0, n1=0, n2=0, n3=0, n4=0, n5=20000, n6=0, n7=0}

Nothing collided. A 32-bit value spans ~4×10⁹ and the ring spans ~1.8×10¹⁹, so every key lands in about one hundred-millionth of the circle and one machine's slice contains all of them. If you predicted "somewhat lumpy", the thing to update is that this is a range mismatch, not a quality problem — a perfect hash of the wrong width fails identically.

3.5 r=3 returns 3 machines. r=4 returns the same 3 — not an error.

r=3 -> [c, b, a]   r=4 -> [c, b, a]

Silently returning fewer is defensible for a placement function, whose job is "where does this belong" rather than "is your cluster big enough". But it is exactly the kind of quiet degradation that hides a misconfiguration: a cluster that shrank below your replication factor keeps working and keeps reporting success, while your actual durability has quietly dropped. Log it at minimum. See 5.2.

3.6 Modulo 90.8%, ring 9.5%. Same propagation window, same everything else — the only difference is the blast radius, and it is exactly the remapping fraction from 3.1.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Measured, 8 machines, 200,000 keys:

positions imbalance ring entries rebuild
200 1.070× 1,600 4.8 ms

2,000 1.035× 16,000 21.1 ms

Look at the 2,000 row: it is worse than the 500 row. That is not a measurement error and it is the most useful thing in this table. The guarantee is statistical, so the curve has noise in it, and past a few hundred positions you are deep in diminishing returns where the noise is larger than the trend. Chasing 1.02× to 1.01× is chasing something you cannot reliably observe.

Why production sits at 100–200 anyway: rebuild cost. The ring is machines × positions entries, sorted on every membership change, in every client. At 1,000 machines × 200 that is 200,000 entries; at 5,000 positions it is 5,000,000. The rebuild time above already quadruples between 500 and 2,000. This is exactly why Cassandra moved its default from 256 to 16 and added a deliberate allocation algorithm — placing few positions well beats placing many at random.

Correctness never moved. This dial is pure efficiency.

4.2 Measured, from 10 machines:

+1 at once  ->  9.4% moved   (floor  9.1%)
+3 at once  -> 24.0% moved   (floor 23.1%)

one at a time:  step 1 -> 9.4%,  step 2 -> 8.9%,  step 3 -> 7.8%
                26.1% of keys moved in TOTAL, for a net result of 24.0%

Adding three at once moves less total data — 24.0% against 26.1% — because doing it incrementally makes some keys move twice, landing on machine A in step 1 and machine C in step 3. That is 2.1 percentage points of pure waste.

But three separate steps produce three smaller storms (9.4%, 8.9%, 7.8%) instead of one 24% one, and you can pause between them. So the real answer is a genuine trade rather than a winner: batch if total transfer volume is your constraint (F8), stagger if the miss storm is (F3) — and for a cache it is almost always the storm, so stagger.

4.3 See the table in 2.5. Only rendezvous moves in the ranking, and the lookup column moves it. It is competitive at 10 machines (0.384 µs vs the ring's 0.254 µs) and hopeless at 1,000 (34 µs).

The crossover is somewhere around 30–50 machines, but pick it by budget rather than by the graph: at 3 µs a lookup, a service doing 10 placement decisions per request has spent 30 µs on arithmetic, which is fine; at 34 µs it has spent a third of a millisecond, which is not.

4.4 Measured, with weights 1, 1 and 20:

200 positions: small 0.90x  mid 0.99x  big 1.01x    (1.00 = exactly its weight)
  5 positions: small 1.01x  mid 1.68x  big 0.97x

At 200 positions the weights hold well. At 5, "mid" gets 68% more than its weight entitles it to — the ratios have stopped meaning anything.

The reason: a machine's share is the sum of its gap widths, and a ratio between shares is only as precise as the number of samples underlying it. With 5 positions the small machines have 5 gaps each; their totals are dominated by luck, not by weight. Weighting does not need "some" positions, it needs enough for the law of large numbers to apply — which is the §10 warning, quantified.

4.5 Every test passes because the ring is internally consistent either way. The positions are different, but they are consistently different — one client using - produces a perfectly valid ring.

It is an incident because a client's ring is determined by three things — the hash function, the virtual-position naming format, and the position count — and two clients that differ on any of the three compute different owners for the same key (§11 F7). Ship this change to half your fleet and the two halves silently disagree about every key, permanently. Symptom: a hit ratio that halves with no other explanation, because each half keeps populating keys the other half cannot find.

This is why memcached's ketama is a compatibility contract rather than an implementation detail, and why a change to any of the three is a fleet-wide coordinated deploy, not a refactor.

Rung 5 — Implement

5.1 Ring.arcShare() already does this; the exercise is to check it against reality. It should track the measured key counts closely, because the arc share is the probability a uniformly-hashed key lands on that machine.

Why it is worth having: it measures balance without traffic. You can evaluate a proposed membership change — adding five nodes, removing a rack — before you make it, and you can detect a bad position count on an idle cluster. A metric that needs production traffic to reveal a problem is a metric that reveals it too late. It is also the honest way to separate the two causes of imbalance: if arc share is even but measured load is not, you do not have a ring problem, you have a hot key (F5).

5.2 The walk becomes: collect nodes clockwise, skipping any whose rack is already represented.

The hard part is the fallback, and there are three options:

  • Return fewer than r — silent, and it means your durability quietly dropped below what you configured, with everything still reporting success. This is 3.5's problem made worse.
  • Fail the write — loud and safe, and it turns a partial rack outage into a total write outage. Rarely what you want.
  • Degrade explicitly — place the remaining copies on distinct nodes even though the racks repeat, and emit a metric saying you did.

The third is what production systems do (Cassandra's NetworkTopologyStrategy behaves this way), and the metric is not an afterthought — it is the whole point. The failure you are guarding against is not "we cannot place the data", it is "we placed the data less safely than you believe and nobody told you". Whichever you choose, the requirement is that it is visible.

5.3 The ranges that changed owner are exactly the arcs between the new machine's positions and the position that preceded each of them. For each such arc, the data must stream from the machine that used to own it (the old successor) to the new one.

While it is in flight, a read has three options and they are the design decision:

  • Read from the old owner until the transfer completes — correct, and it means the new machine's arrival changes nothing until it is ready. This is the right default.
  • Read from the new owner — fast to implement and returns "not found" for data that exists, which is data loss from the caller's point of view.
  • Read from both and merge — correct and doubles the read cost for the duration.

The subtlety worth stating: the transfer is not finished when the last byte arrives, because writes kept happening during it. You need either a hand-off point where writes go to both owners, or a final catch-up pass over what changed while you were copying. This is why joining a Cassandra node is a multi-phase operation with an explicit state machine rather than a copy.

Why each is hard to find in production:

  • Bug 1 looks perfect in the operation you actually test. See 6.4.
  • Bug 2 produces correct data. Reads work, writes work, nothing errors. You discover it the day a machine dies and takes the only copy of 3.7% of your keys.   • Bug 3 never crashes and every client agrees on the same wrong answer, so no consistency check fires. Its severity is inversely proportional to ring density, which means it is loudest in your test environment and quietest in production — the exact opposite of the pattern people's instincts are tuned for.

6.4 — why the monotonicity test passes. Because the bug only bites when an index shifts, and appending a machine to the end of the list does not renumber anybody. n10 becomes index 10; n0 through n9 keep indices 0 through 9; every existing machine's positions are unchanged; monotonicity holds perfectly.

The bug appears on removal (every index after the gap shifts down) and on different insertion orders across clients (100% disagreement).

What that tells you generally: a test named after a property does not test the property — it tests the property under one operation. The suite had a test called ring_never_moves_a_key_between_two_existing_machines and a bug that destroys exactly that guarantee, and they coexisted happily, because the test only ever added.

The transferable habit: for every invariant, ask which operations could violate this? and cover each of them — not just the one that is easiest to write. Here that means add, remove, reorder, and rebuild-from-scratch, and the bug dies on three of the four.

6.5 Test the property, not a symptom. The wrap is what makes the space circular, so assert circularity directly rather than hoping a key lands past the last position:

static void the_space_is_a_circle_at_every_density(MiniRing unused) {
    for (int[] cfg : new int[][]{{2,1},{4,1},{8,10},{8,200}}) {
        Ring r = new Ring(cfg[1]);
        for (int i = 0; i < cfg[0]; i++) r.addNode("n" + i);
        List<Object[]> layout = r.layout();
        long lastPos  = (Long) layout.get(layout.size() - 1)[0];
        String first  = (String) layout.get(0)[1];
        // Probe positions we KNOW are past the end, instead of hoping a key lands there.
        for (long probe : new long[]{lastPos + 1, Long.MAX_VALUE, lastPos + 1000000L})
            eq("a position past the last point wraps to the first machine, at "
               + cfg[0] + "x" + cfg[1], first, r.ownerOfPosition(probe));
    }
}

Two changes make it density-independent: it constructs the positions it wants to test instead of sampling keys and hoping, and it runs across densities so it cannot pass by being lucky about ring shape. (It needs a small ownerOfPosition(long) accessor, which is a one-line addition worth making — a test that has to route through the key hash is testing two things at once.)

6.6 — a fourth bug the suite would miss. The hint is the real answer: hunt for a property nothing asserts, not for a cleverer bug.

Worth reporting an attempt that failed, because it teaches more than the success. My first candidate was: sort the positions but build the parallel ownerOf array from the unsorted list, so every key gets a valid machine name that is simply the wrong one. The reasoning was that the suite only checks aggregates — counts, ratios, distributions — and a permutation preserves all of them.

It does not survive contact:

   FAIL  ring_never_moves_a_key_between_two_existing_machines
   FAIL  ring_removal_moves_only_the_dead_machines_keys

   17 passed, 5 failed

Five tests, because the permutation is not stable: inserting a position shifts the mis-mapping for every entry after it, so the membership-change tests catch what the steady-state tests cannot see. That is a genuinely useful thing to learn about this suite — it is stronger than it looks, precisely because so many of its tests assert behaviour across a change rather than at rest.

A bug that does survive — and it is in Ring.java right now. Ring has two ways to add a machine: addNode(name) and addNode(name, weight). The first does weights.put(node, 1). So when a machine that was registered with a weight re-registers through the plain path — a heartbeat, a reconnect, a config reload, a node rejoining after a blip — its weight is silently reset to 1:

after setup            : small=41,841  big=158,159  (ratio 3.78x)
after big re-registers : small=101,424  big=98,576  (ratio 0.97x)

Your 4× machine just became a 1× machine. Nothing throws, nothing logs, the ring stays perfectly valid and every client agrees on it. What you observe in production is that a large expensive box is mysteriously half-idle while the small ones run hot — with no event to correlate it against, because the re-registration was routine.

All 22 tests pass, because not one of them adds the same machine twice. That is the uncovered property: the suite tests add, remove and rebuild, but never re-add.

The test:

static void re_registering_a_machine_keeps_its_weight() {
    Ring r = new Ring(200);
    r.addNode("small", 1);
    r.addNode("big", 4);
    Map<String, Integer> before = counts(r, KEYS);
    r.addNode("big");                       // a heartbeat re-registers it
    Map<String, Integer> after = counts(r, KEYS);
    double ratioBefore = before.get("big") / (double) before.get("small");
    double ratioAfter  = after.get("big")  / (double) after.get("small");
    ok("a machine that re-registers keeps the weight it had (" + ratioBefore
       + " -> " + ratioAfter + ")", Math.abs(ratioAfter - ratioBefore) < 0.5);
}

And the fix is a design decision, not a one-liner: either addNode(name) preserves an existing weight (weights.putIfAbsent), or the single-argument overload is deleted so a caller must always state the weight. The second is better — the bug exists because two methods quietly disagree about what "add" means, and removing the ambiguity removes the whole class of mistake rather than this one instance of it.

The general lesson, which is the point of the rung: the bugs that survive a good suite are not the clever ones. They live in the sequences of operations nobody wrote a test for. This suite covers add, remove and rebuild thoroughly, and re-add not at all — so that is exactly where something was waiting.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 — Surviving the miss storm anyway

The ring cuts the storm from ~99% to ~1/n. On a large fleet 1/n is still enormous, so:

technique what it buys what it costs what it cannot do
Add nodes one at a time, with a pause turns one 24% storm into three ~9% ones (measured in 4.2) slower expansion; ~2 points more total data movement help if even 1/n exceeds your database's headroom
Pre-warm the new node before it joins the ring near-zero storm you must know which keys it will own — computable from the ring in advance — and pay to fetch them protect against an unplanned join, i.e. a node returning after a failure
Serve from the previous owner during a grace period zero misses; the new node fills in behind clients need two rings (old and new) and a rule for reading both help if the previous owner is the one that died
Rate-limit misses to the origin (single-flight, a queue, a circuit breaker) hard ceiling on database load some requests get slow or shed keep your hit ratio up — it protects the database by sacrificing the request

What to build first: the last one, because it is the only one that bounds the damage from failures you did not plan, and because it protects you from every other cause of a miss storm too. Note that single-flight is already familiar from caching — one expired hot key turning 200 concurrent readers into 200 database calls is the same shape at a smaller scale.

The honest summary: consistent hashing turns "your database is about to fall over" into "your database will notice". That is a large improvement and it is not a guarantee.

7.2 — Being correct with a stale ring

Reads and writes need different answers, which is the main insight.

Reads can be made correct cheaply: on a miss, also ask the machine the other plausible ring says. During a membership change a key has at most two candidate owners (the old successor and the new one), so a miss can fall back to the other before going to the origin. Cheap, bounded, and it converts the whole failure into a slightly slower read.

Writes cannot be patched this way — a write to the wrong owner is not detectably wrong, and it stays invisible until a reader who needed it comes looking. The options:

  • Write to both candidates during a membership change. Correct, doubles write cost, needs everyone to agree a change is in progress.
  • Version the ring and have the server reject writes carrying a stale version, so the client refreshes and retries. This is the fencing-token idea from redis's F7, applied to membership: the receiver checks, because only the receiver knows the truth.
  • Accept it, if the data is a cache and a lost write is a future miss.

What to build first on a fixed budget: ring versioning on writes. It is the only one that turns a silent wrong answer into a loud retry, and silence is the actual problem — extra latency you will find in a dashboard; a write that went to the wrong machine you will find in a customer complaint six weeks later.

7.3 — Moving the data

A safe join, in order:

  1. Announce the joiner as "joining", not "a member". It gets no reads and appears in no client's ring yet.
  2. Compute what it will own — from the ring, in advance, which is exercise 5.3.
  3. Stream those ranges from the current owners, throttled so it does not compete with live traffic.
  4. Double-write during the transfer: writes to those ranges go to both the current owner and the joiner. Without this, everything you copied is stale by the time you finish.
  5. Catch up on anything that changed before double-writing began.
  6. Flip membership, so clients begin routing to the joiner.
  7. Only now let the old owners drop the transferred ranges — and not immediately; keep them for a grace period so a client with a stale ring still gets an answer (7.2).

If the joiner dies halfway: nothing has been lost, because the old owners still hold everything and are still authoritative. This is why step 7 is last and why "delete after transfer" is a separate, delayed decision. The cost of getting the order wrong is permanent data loss; the cost of being conservative is disk.

If a write arrives mid-transfer: step 4 is the answer, and the ordering matters — start double-writing before you start copying, not after, or you have a window whose writes land only on a machine that is about to stop being the owner.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 — The placement layer

200 nodes, 3 DCs, 4 PB, r=3, 400 KB objects. That is ~10 billion objects and ~13 TB of primary data per node before replication.

  • Strategy: the ring. At 200 nodes rendezvous costs 3.5 µs per lookup (measured) and an object store makes placement decisions constantly. Fixed slots would also work well here and are worth considering if a coordinator already exists — see 8.5.
  • Positions: ~100–200 per node. 200 × 200 = 40,000 entries, which is nothing to hold and fast to rebuild. Do not go to 1,000: 4.1 shows the balance gain is inside the noise and the rebuild cost is not.
  • Replicas: one per datacentre, using the rack-aware walk from 5.2. With r=3 and 3 DCs this is the natural mapping and it survives losing an entire DC. Do not just take the next three distinct nodes — they can easily be three nodes in one DC.
  • Membership: gossip, with an explicit version number on the list, because 7.2's write-side fix depends on being able to name a ring version.
  • Alerts: (1) per-node capacity as a ratio to the mean, above 1.3×; (2) any node receiving zero traffic — the loudest and only symptom of F6 and F7; (3) streaming backlog during membership changes; (4) how many distinct ring versions are live at once, which makes F4's window visible rather than inferred.

At 20 nodes instead of 200: switch to rendezvous. At that size its lookup cost is irrelevant (0.384 µs) and you delete the position count, the sorted array, the rebuild path and an entire class of tuning bug. Smaller clusters should use the simpler algorithm, and the reason they usually do not is that everyone reaches for the famous one.

8.2 — Adding 50 nodes to a 200-node cluster in a week

What actually happens if you do it at once. 50/250 = 20% of the entire dataset must move — 800 TB at 4 PB. That streaming competes with live traffic for disk and network on both ends (F8), so latency rises for as long as it runs, which at any sane throttle is days. Meanwhile every cache in front of it takes a 20% cold hit at the moment membership flips (F3), and the membership change itself propagates over a longer window than usual because the list changed by a quarter (F4).

What to do instead: add them in batches of five, with the cluster fully settled between batches. Each batch moves ~2% instead of 20%.

The quantified argument, from 4.2: incremental costs ~2 percentage points more total data movement, because some objects move twice. In exchange you get ten small, individually recoverable events instead of one large irreversible one — and critically, you can stop after batch three if latency degrades, which you cannot do halfway through a single 20% rebalance.

The sentence to say out loud to whoever asked for a week: the constraint is not the ring and not the machines, it is disk and network bandwidth against live traffic, and that does not care how many people want it done by Friday.

8.3 — The reply about the hot key

I don't think consistent hashing will help here, and I want to be clear about why before we spend a sprint on it. Every scheme we might switch to — the ring, rendezvous, jump hash — agrees that one key has exactly one owner. That agreement is the feature. So a single product ID that is 30% of traffic will still be 30% of traffic on one machine afterwards, and adding machines still will not help, because sharding distributes keys and we have a problem with one key.

What will actually help, cheapest first. Put a small in-process cache in front of it with a one-second TTL — for a key this hot, one second of staleness collapses ~30% of our cache traffic to roughly one fetch per app server per second, and it is a few lines. If we need more, split the key into product:X:0 through product:X:9, write to all ten and read from a random one; that spreads it across ten machines at the cost of ten times the write amplification for that key, which for a read-heavy product page is a good trade.

Consistent hashing is still worth doing, for the reason we found last month — the miss storm when we scale the fleet. It is just a fix for a different problem, and I'd rather we adopted it for the one it actually solves. Shall I do the local cache this week and we look at the rest after?

8.4 — Migrating from modulo without a storm

The trap: switching 20 servers from % 20 to a ring in one deploy remaps ~95% of keys (measured: 20→ring is essentially a total reshuffle) — the exact storm you are adopting consistent hashing to prevent, suffered once on the way in.

The approach: read from both, write to the new, for one TTL.

  1. Ship a client that computes both placements. Writes go to the ring's owner only. Reads try the ring's owner, and on a miss fall back to the modulo owner, and on a hit there, populate the ring's owner.
  2. Deploy that to the whole fleet. The dual-read makes the hit ratio hold flat — every key is found either where it is going or where it was.
  3. Wait one full cache TTL (plus margin). By then every live key has been read at least once and re-populated at its new home, or expired.
  4. Ship a second client that drops the fallback.

Cost: reads are slightly more expensive during the window, and memory usage roughly doubles for one TTL because keys exist in both places. Both are temporary and cheap.

Do not try to migrate the data directly. These are caches; the source of truth can regenerate them, and a read-through migration is self-healing where a copy is a distributed transaction you do not want. And pin the ring's three parameters (hash, position naming, position count) in the first deploy — if the two client versions disagree on any of them, you have shipped F7 into the middle of your migration.

8.5 — Consistent hashing vs fixed slots, with etcd already running

For consistent hashing: no new dependency on the data path. Placement is a pure function, so a client with the machine list is never blocked, never waits, and etcd being slow or unavailable cannot stop a single request. Adding a node is one list entry.

For fixed slots + an assignment table: you already run etcd, so the coordinator is not a new cost — it is a thing you already operate, monitor and back up. In exchange you get exact placement rather than statistical: you can move one slot off a struggling node by hand, drain a node deliberately, place slots to satisfy a rule the hash cannot express (no two replicas in one rack, this tenant on those nodes), and you get no disagreement window because there is one authoritative table version (F4 disappears). Rebalancing stops being an emergent property and becomes an operation with a plan and a progress bar.

I would pick fixed slots, for 40 nodes with etcd already in place. The decisive argument is not performance — it is that during an incident you can look at the table and see exactly which node owns what, and change it. Consistent hashing's answer to "why is node 17 hot?" is "the hash function decided that", which is true and useless at 2 a.m.

The single fact that would change my mind: if the clients are not all ours — a polyglot fleet, third-party consumers, or anything embedded that we cannot redeploy on demand. Then the coordinator becomes a distribution problem across code we do not control, and the pure function that every client can compute independently wins on the spot.
