Lab 63 · Running It In Production

Terraform — the ledger that knows what you own

Tell a friend: Asha built her company's cloud by clicking through screens, and now nobody can say exactly what she built, copy it, or rebuild it after a bad day. Writing the clicks down as a script does not rescue her, because a script has no memory of what it already made, so running it again gives her a second copy of everything. Terraform's answer is a written ledger that maps every name Asha chose to the object the cloud actually created. Nothing in the cloud knows that mapping, so the ledger is the only thing in the world that knows what she owns. Every hard thing in this lab comes from that one ledger being shared, going stale, or going missing.

The simulator is visualization.html, beside this file; section 9 says what to do with it. What this lab is made of, and where its numbers come from, is in section 17 at the end.

1. Why must the description of a system live outside the system?

In one breath: Asha built her company's cloud by clicking. The only complete description of what she built is the running thing itself. You cannot copy a thing, review a thing, or rebuild a thing from memory.

You built something on a cloud by clicking. It works. Now you need it again.

You need it in a second environment for testing. Or in a second region, because the first one had a bad day. Or simply back the way it was before somebody changed it.

And nobody can tell you what "it" is. The only complete description of what you built is the thing itself, sitting in a console, spread over a hundred screens you visited once, in an order you no longer remember.

Infrastructure as code is the idea that the description should exist somewhere other than inside the running system. Terraform is one way to do that. Why it is harder than it sounds is the subject of this lab.

What does it cost to leave the description inside the system?

Four bills arrive, and none of them looks like an infrastructure problem when it lands.

Your staging environment quietly stops resembling production, so tests stop meaning anything. A change made at 2am to end an incident is never made anywhere else, and is forgotten by Friday. The one person who remembers why a setting is the way it is leaves the company. And the disaster recovery plan says "rebuild in another region", which is a sentence, not a plan.

What promise is this lab paying off?

The amazon-web-services lab made a claim in its §13.2 and did not defend it:

Your infrastructure code is your disaster recovery plan. If everything but state and identity is a template, a region loss is "apply the template elsewhere and restore the data". If it is 400 console clicks that one person half-remembers, a region loss is a rewrite under pressure.

That was an assertion. This lab is the mechanism behind it. The mechanism turns out to be far more interesting than "write your infrastructure down", and section 13.2 comes back and qualifies the claim properly.

Here is the thesis. Everything else in this document is a consequence of it.

Terraform is not a tool for creating infrastructure. It is a tool for maintaining a mapping — between names you chose and objects a cloud created. Nothing in the cloud knows that mapping, so Terraform writes it down. Every difficult thing about Terraform is a consequence of that written mapping being shared, going stale, and being the only thing in the world that knows what you own.

2. Why does Asha have to build Marigold Pay twice?

In one breath: Asha built a payments company's infrastructure by clicking for three weeks. Then four different people needed a copy of it, and there was nothing to copy — only a running system nobody could read.

No jargon in this section. A ten-year-old could follow it.

Asha runs the engineering at Marigold Pay, the small payments company from the last lab. Over three weeks she has built their system on AWS by clicking. A private network. Subnets in three zones. A load balancer. Servers that replace themselves. A database with a standby. Storage for receipts. And about forty permission rules.

It works. Then four things happen, in this order, and each one hurts more than the last.

One: the second environment

The team wants a staging copy to test against, so Asha starts clicking again. Three days later staging exists and is almost the same. The load balancer timeout is different, because she forgot. One permission is broader, because it was easier that afternoon.

Six weeks later a change works in staging and breaks in production. It takes two days to find out why. The difference was a setting nobody knew was different, because nowhere in the world is there a list of the settings.

Two: the change nobody can see

At 2am during an incident, Priya is on call. She widens a rule to let traffic through, and the incident ends. The change is never written down, never reviewed, and never removed.

It is now permanent, invisible and load-bearing. Hold on to Priya's 2am fix. It comes back four more times in this lab, and each time it costs more.

Three: the person who left

The engineer who set up the network leaves. Nobody knows why one subnet has a strange address range. Nobody dares change it. It becomes a thing the team works around rather than understands.

Four: the plan that is a sentence

The disaster recovery document says: if we lose the region, we rebuild in another one. Omar, who runs the quarterly drill, reads that and does the arithmetic. "Rebuild" means three weeks of clicking, from memory, at the worst possible moment, by whoever is awake.

3. Why doesn't writing the clicks down as a script fix it?

In one breath: Every click has a command-line twin, so Asha writes the whole build as a script. It is a real fix for two of her four pains. Then she runs it a second time.

This is the obvious repair, and it is not a strawman. It is how everyone did this for years.

Every click Asha made has a command-line equivalent. So she writes a script: create the network, then the subnets, then the load balancer, then the servers. Now the description lives in a file, in version control, where it can be read and reviewed and run again.

It fixes pains three and four partially. There is now a written description, and "rebuild" means "run the script". Ben, the newest engineer on the team, reads it on his first day and understands the shape of production in an hour. That is real value.

Before you read on, commit to a guess.

🔮 Predict: Asha runs her script a second time — same file, same account, nothing changed. What happens?

Reveal

She gets a second copy of everything. A second network, six more subnets, another load balancer, another database.

The script says "create a load balancer". It does not say "there should be one load balancer". Those are different sentences, and only the second one is safe to say twice.

This is the same distinction as idempotency: an operation is safe to repeat only if repeating it lands you in the same state. "Create" is not that operation. And she cannot simply avoid running it twice, because running it again is the entire point — she wants to change one setting and have everything else stay as it is.

4. What three things can a script never do?

In one breath: Asha patches the script to check before it creates. Each patch works until it meets the next problem, and the third one is the problem the rest of the lab is about.

Asha does the obvious repair. Before creating anything, check whether it is already there. In her words: if it does not exist, create it, which she writes in the script as if not exists, create.

Then Ben asks three questions, and each one opens a hole underneath the last.

Ben's first question: how do you know it is already there?

Check by name? Most cloud objects do not have a unique, immutable, queryable name. An AWS load balancer has a name. A subnet has an identifier that AWS chose, a string like subnet-0a3f.... At best it also carries a Name tag, which is neither unique nor enforced.

Two different teams can tag two different subnets marigold-app-a. A tag can be edited by anyone.

So "does marigold-app-a exist?" has no reliable answer. And worse: if something does come back, Asha has no way to know whether it is the one her script made, or somebody else's that happens to share a name.

Ben's second question: how does the script delete anything?

Asha removes the "create a cache cluster" block from her script, because Marigold stopped using it. She runs the script. Nothing happens. The cache cluster is still there, still running, still billed.

Of course it is. The script is a list of things to create, and she deleted a line from the list.

A file of instructions has no memory of the instructions it used to contain. To delete something, you must compare what the file says now with what the file caused before. A file cannot compare itself to its own past.

Ben's third question: what will it do before it does it?

Asha wants to change the load balancer's timeout from 30 seconds to 60. She edits the script and stands with her finger over the enter key.

The only way to find out what the script will do is to let it do it. On a payments system. On a Tuesday afternoon.

🔮 Predict: All three problems have one shared root cause. Before reading section 5, try to say it in one sentence.

Reveal

The script describes actions, and Asha wants to describe an outcome. To turn an outcome into the right actions, you must know which real objects in the cloud your previous outcomes produced.

Not "what exists" — the cloud can tell you that. Which of what exists is yours, and which line of your file it came from. That mapping is not recoverable from the cloud, because the cloud never knew about your file.

Every one of the three problems is that missing mapping:

  • can't tell if it exists → can't map a config line to a real object;
  • can't delete → can't tell which real object a removed line used to own;
  • can't preview → can't compare against something you never recorded.

If you guessed "the script isn't idempotent", that is the symptom and section 3 already named it. The cause is one level down: idempotence needs identity, and she has no identity to work with.

5. What does Asha actually need? Five sentences, and one of them is the hard one

In one breath: Asha writes down what a tool would have to do for her. Five sentences. The third one forces the entire design of Terraform, and the fifth one causes most of its pain.

Everything from here pursues five sentences. Asha needs a tool where:

  1. She describes the end state, not the steps. "There should be one load balancer with a 60-second timeout", not "create a load balancer".
  2. Running it when everything already matches does nothing. Not "does it again harmlessly" — does nothing, and says so.
  3. Removing a description removes the thing. The tool must remember what its own past descriptions produced.
  4. It shows her exactly what will change, before anything changes, in enough detail that Meera can review it in a pull request.
  5. It works for a team, not for one laptop — several people, several changes, no lost work.

Requirement 3 is the one that forces the whole design. It is the only one that cannot be satisfied by looking at the cloud, because the cloud has no idea what your file used to say.

Requirement 5 is where most of the pain in sections 11 and 12 lives. Meera reviews Marigold's infrastructure pull requests, and Arjun looks after the shared plumbing the team runs on. Both of them exist because of requirement 5, and both of them appear again shortly.

6. What if you simply write the mapping down?

In one breath: Keep a written note of which real cloud object each line of your description produced. That one note turns an impossible question into an arithmetic problem, and it is the whole invention.

Here is the idea, stated once, plainly:

Keep a written record of which real cloud object each line of your description produced. Then "what should change?" becomes a comparison between three things instead of two: what you want, what you made last time, and what is actually out there now.

That record is the whole invention. Four things fall out of it: the plan output, the locking, the strange refactoring rules, and the famous ways to destroy production by accident. All of them exist because this record exists, is shared, and can be wrong.

There are two halves here, and separating them is worth doing carefully.

The description is a set of named blocks. Each says what should exist. And here is the crucial part. Each carries a name you chose, which is stable even though the cloud's own identifier is not.

The record maps each of your names to the cloud's identifier for the object it produced. On the left, my name "app_load_balancer". On the right, the thing AWS calls arn:aws:elasticloadbalancing:...:loadbalancer/app/marigold/6d0ecf831eec9f09.

With those two, all five of Asha's requirements become achievable. And one thing becomes newly dangerous.

That record is now the only object in the universe that knows the connection between your file and your production infrastructure. Lose it and you have not lost your configuration. You have lost your claim on everything you built.

7. Which nine words carry the weight, and what did each one cost?

7.1 What is a resource, and what is the name you call it by?

Asha writes one block that says "there should be one load balancer, with a 60-second timeout". One block, one thing that should exist. Engineers call that block a resource, and call the block itself a declaration of one object managed by Terraform.

Now she needs to refer to it later, and here is the trouble. The cloud's own identifier for that load balancer is a long string AWS chose after creating it. Asha cannot write it in a file she has not run yet. So she needs a name that exists before the object does, stays stable while the object changes, and can be read by Meera in a pull request.

That name is built from two parts she controls: the kind of thing it is, and a local label she invents. Engineers call it the resource address, and it is written aws_lb.app. Another one is aws_subnet.private_a. Once resources are grouped into reusable bundles, which section 7.8 gets to, addresses nest: module.network.aws_vpc.main.

The address is the key of the whole system. The record from section 6 is filed by it. Section 10.7 is entirely about what happens when addresses move.

Here is a concrete pair. The block Asha named aws_lb.app produces a load balancer whose AWS identifier is arn:...loadbalancer/app/marigold/6d0ecf83. Tomorrow she changes the timeout. The AWS identifier stays. The address stays. Terraform knows they are the same object, because the record says so.

That is an identity problem, and identity problems are the spine of consistent-hashing. Hold that thought until 10.7.

Here is the shape of one resource block, printed to be read:

resource "aws_lb" "app" {                 # address: aws_lb.app
  name               = "marigold-app"
  load_balancer_type = "application"
  subnets            = [aws_subnet.public_a.id,   # <- a reference
                        aws_subnet.public_b.id,
                        aws_subnet.public_c.id]
  idle_timeout       = 60
}

(That is HCL, HashiCorp Configuration Language. You are not learning the syntax in this lab — you are learning what the tool does with it. The one thing to notice is the references to aws_subnet.public_a.id. Section 7.5 explains why they matter far more than they look.)

7.2 Who actually knows how to talk to AWS?

Not Terraform. This surprises people.

Terraform knows nothing about load balancers, subnets or databases. It is a general engine for reconciling a description against a record against reality. The thing that turns "there should be a load balancer" into actual API calls is a separate plugin, and engineers call it a provider.

Said precisely: a provider is a separately-versioned binary. For every resource type it supports, it implements four operations: create, read, update and delete. It also carries a schema, describing that type's arguments and which of them can be changed in place.

That separation is why the same tool manages Cloudflare DNS, GitHub repos, Kubernetes objects and PostgreSQL roles. Learn the engine once; the providers are catalogue lookups.

And it has a consequence worth writing on the wall: idempotence is the provider's job, not Terraform's. When section 12 tells you that Terraform guarantees less than you assumed, most of the missing guarantees are missing right here.

7.3 Why does Asha need three states and not two?

Asha now has three different pictures of Marigold Pay, and confusing them is the single most common way to misread a plan. Naming them separately is the most useful thing you can do for your own understanding, and Terraform's own documentation does not always do it.

The first picture is what her files say should exist. Engineers call it the desired state.

The second is what Terraform believes it created, including a full copy of every object's attributes as of the last time it looked. That is the record from section 6. Engineers call it the recorded state, and it lives in a file called the state file.

The third is what is really in the cloud right now, discovered by asking the provider. Engineers call it the actual state.

Why three, and not just "what I want" versus "what is there"? Because desired-versus-actual cannot tell you what to delete — nothing in the cloud says "this used to be in your config". It also cannot tell you what is yours. Desired-versus-recorded tells you what you meant to change. Recorded-versus- actual tells you what changed behind your back. Asha needs both comparisons, which means she needs all three states.

Watch it decide something. Desired says the timeout should be 60. Recorded says 30. Actual says 30. Conclusion: you asked for a change, nobody has interfered, do the update.

Now change only the third number. Actual says 120, because someone edited it by hand. The conclusion is now different, and the plan will tell you so.

One connection to carry forward: the recorded state is a cached replica of the cloud, and it goes stale exactly the way caching said replicas do. The refresh in section 10.4 is cache revalidation, wearing a hat.

7.4 What is a plan, and which of its four symbols ends careers?

Asha's fourth requirement was "show me what will change before it changes". The two halves of that are a plan and an apply, and in plain words they are "tell me what you would do" and then "do it".

A plan is the computed difference between the three states, expressed as one action per resource address. There are four possible actions: create, update in place, destroy, or replace — which means destroy and then create. An apply executes a plan.

The separation exists so that Meera can read the change before it happens. This is the single most valuable thing Terraform gives you over Asha's script, and it is why a Terraform plan belongs in a pull request the way a code diff does.

Here are the four action symbols, printed as you will read them for the rest of your career:

  + create              a new object will be made
  ~ update in-place     an attribute changes; the object survives
  - destroy             the object goes away
-/+ replace             DESTROY and then CREATE  <-- read this one twice

🔮 Predict: Meera is reviewing a 40-line plan. One line reads -/+ aws_db_instance.main. Everything else is a ~. Which line should stop the review, and what is about to happen if it does not?

Reveal

The -/+ line. It says the production database will be destroyed, and a new empty one created in its place. Terraform does not copy the data across. There is no undo.

It happens because Asha changed an attribute the provider cannot change in place, and the provider's only remaining option is to make a new object. Failure F6, in section 11.6, is this exact line and the three controls that stop it.

If you said "the ~ lines are the risky ones because they change things", that is the natural guess and it is backwards. A ~ keeps the object and edits it. A -/+ throws the object away. The dangerous symbol is the one that looks like two small ones.

7.5 Who decides what gets built first?

Nobody writes the order down. That is the elegant part, and it takes a moment to believe.

Look again at Asha's block in 7.1. The load balancer's list of subnets is not a list of names she typed. It reads subnets = [aws_subnet.public_a.id, ...], and each entry is a reference to another block. Terraform cannot even work out what to send to AWS for the load balancer until those subnets exist and have identifiers.

So the reference is the ordering. Terraform reads every reference in the configuration and builds a graph out of them: one box per resource, one arrow per reference. Engineers call it the dependency graph, and it is a directed acyclic graph — a set of boxes and arrows with no loops.

Terraform then walks that graph. Independent work runs in parallel, ten concurrent operations by default, and no box is ever started before the boxes it points at have finished.

The ordering is a consequence of the data flow. That means it cannot drift out of sync with the code, the way a hand-written ordering would.

Here is the shape of Marigold's graph. Read it downwards; every arrow exists because the lower box mentions the upper one.

flowchart TD
  VPC["aws_vpc.main"] --> SA["aws_subnet.public_a"]
  VPC --> SB["aws_subnet.public_b"]
  VPC --> SC["aws_subnet.public_c"]
  SA --> LB["aws_lb.app"]
  SB --> LB
  SC --> LB
  VPC --> SG["aws_security_group.app"]
  SG --> ASG["aws_autoscaling_group.app"]
  LB --> ASG
  VPC --> DB["aws_db_instance.main"]

What to look at: the three subnets have no arrows between them, so Terraform creates all three at the same time. And destroy runs the graph backwards, which is why terraform destroy removes the load balancer before the subnets it sits in.

There is an escape hatch, and it is a smell. depends_on lets you declare an arrow by hand. It exists for dependencies that are real but invisible to the data flow. The classic case is an IAM policy that must exist before a service can assume a role, where nothing in the second resource references the first. Every depends_on in a repository is a place the graph could not infer the truth.

7.6 Where does the ledger live, and who is allowed to hold it?

Arjun is the engineer who looks after Marigold's shared plumbing, and this is his section.

The record has to live somewhere. Engineers call that somewhere the backend. By default it is an ordinary file sitting next to your configuration, named terraform.tfstate. That is fine for one person on one laptop, and it is not a team's record.

So Arjun moves it to shared storage — an S3 bucket, Terraform Cloud, or a database. That is a remote backend, and it satisfies Asha's fifth requirement. The moment he does it, he creates a new problem: two people can now write the same file at once, which is a lost update on a shared mutable object.

His fix is a state lock: a mutual-exclusion mechanism, taken at the start of an apply and held until the record is written.

The classic AWS setup is an S3 bucket holding the state file, with versioning switched on, plus a lock. Historically that lock was a DynamoDB table using the state's path as its partition key, claimed with a conditional write — which is precisely the conditional-write pattern from optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking. Recent Terraform versions can hold the lock in S3 itself using a lock file, removing the separate table. (Version-specific; check the current backend documentation rather than trusting a blog post, including this one.)

Everything distributed-locking established applies here without modification, including its limits. Section 11.2 is what happens when the lock is off.

A naming collision, cleared up before it confuses you. Terraform has two unrelated things called a lock. The state lock above is mutual exclusion during an apply. The dependency lock file is .terraform.lock.hcl, committed to git. It pins exact provider versions and their checksums, like a package-lock.json. They share a word and nothing else.

7.7 What is drift, and why is it worse than a config change?

Come back to Priya's 2am fix from section 2.

She widened a rule by hand, in the console, to end an incident. Nothing in Terraform's record knows she did it. Reality quietly stopped matching the description. Engineers call that drift: a difference between the actual state and the recorded state, caused by something other than Terraform.

Drift has many parents. A console click. Another tool. An AWS-side default applied without asking. An autoscaling event that changed a server count.

The word exists because drift is a genuinely different failure from "someone changed the config". A config change is intentional and reviewable. Drift is neither, and it is invisible until the next plan — or worse, until the next plan silently reverts it.

Here is the whole shape in one run. Six weeks after Priya's night, Ben applies an unrelated change. Terraform, doing exactly its job, narrows the rule back, because the description says it should be narrow. The incident returns, apparently caused by a change that had nothing to do with it. That is failure F1 in section 11.1, and its operational answer is in 13.5 and 13.6.

7.8 What is a module, and why is the root one your blast radius?

Asha's first pain was building staging by hand. The repair is to write "a network with three subnets and the routing" once, and then ask for it twice with different inputs. Engineers call that reusable bundle a module.

Precisely: a module is a directory of configuration with declared inputs, called variables, and declared outputs. The configuration you actually run is the root module. Everything it calls is a child module. Addresses nest to match, so a subnet inside the network module is module.network.aws_subnet.private_a.

Now the sentence that matters more than the definition:

The root module is the unit of state, and therefore the unit of blast radius. One root module means one state file, which means one lock, one plan and one apply. Put everything your company owns in one root module, and every change to anything locks and risks everything. Section 13.3 is how Arjun draws those boundaries, and 11.10 is what it costs when he does not.

7.9 What are the two ways to say "make several of these"?

Marigold needs five private subnets. Asha does not want five nearly-identical blocks, so Terraform offers two ways to say "make several of these". They are called count and for_each.

Both create multiple instances of one resource block. They differ in how the instances are named in the record, and that is the only difference that matters.

With count = 3, the instances are addressed by their position in a list: aws_subnet.private[0], [1], [2].

With for_each = { a = ..., b = ..., c = ... }, they are addressed by a string you chose: aws_subnet.private["a"], ["b"], ["c"].

It does not look like it should matter. It is the single most destructive detail in the tool, and section 10.7 is the whole story. For now, hold this shape: one of them keys identity by where a thing sits in a list, the other keys it by what the thing is.

If that framing rings a bell, it should. consistent-hashing opened with the same bug. There, a key's location was a function of a global property, the machine count, rather than of the key itself. That is hash(key) % n, and count is the same idea with subnets instead of cache entries.

8. What two pictures should you keep in your head?

In one breath: Two mental models. A shopkeeper with a list, a ledger and a stock-take is the whole of Terraform. Then we strip the shop away and say the same machine precisely, and then we find the four places the shop lies to you.

8.1 What does the shopkeeper's ledger tell you?

Imagine Asha keeping a shop, and stocking it from one supplier.

She keeps a shopping list pinned to the wall: what the shop should stock. Three shelves of rice. One freezer. A sign outside. That is the configuration.

She keeps a ledger of what she has actually bought, with the supplier's order number written next to each line — "one freezer, supplier reference FRZ-88213". That is the state file. The reference numbers matter, because the supplier only understands its own numbers, not her descriptions.

Before ordering anything she does a stock-take. She walks the shop and checks that each ledger line's item is still there, and still as recorded. That is the refresh.

Then she compares three things: the list, the ledger and the shelves. Out of that comparison she writes a to-do list: buy this, return that, one shelf needs restocking. She reads it before she does any of it. That is the plan.

Only then does she act, ticking the ledger as each thing is done.

The three-way comparison is what makes the picture earn its keep, so here it is made concrete. Read the last row twice.

Shopping list says Ledger says Shelves say What Asha concludes
one freezer one freezer, FRZ-88213 freezer there nothing to do
one freezer (nothing) no freezer buy one
(nothing) one freezer, FRZ-88213 freezer there return FRZ-88213
one freezer one freezer, FRZ-88213 no freezer someone took it — buy again
big freezer small freezer, FRZ-88213 small freezer upgrade FRZ-88213
one freezer one freezer, FRZ-88213 two freezers the second one is not hers, and she will not touch it

That last row explains the entire existence of the ledger. Without it she could not tell her freezer from somebody else's.

8.2 What is the same machine, stated precisely?

Now strip the shop away. Six sentences, and they are the engineering model: a reconciliation loop with a written key.

Terraform is a reconciler. It computes a plan as a function of three inputs: what you want, what it recorded, and what is out there. Then it executes that plan. Written the way an engineer would write it, plan = f(desired, recorded, actual). It is not a script runner, and it has no concept of steps you wrote.

The address is the primary key. Structurally, the state file is a keyed table. Each row is address → (provider, cloud identifier, full attribute snapshot, dependencies). Everything Terraform can do rests on that key being stable.

Ordering is derived, not declared. The graph comes from references, and the walk runs in parallel wherever the graph permits.

The state file is a replica of the cloud. It is refreshed at plan time and written at apply time. Between those two moments it is stale by definition.

An apply is not a transaction. It is a graph walk of independent operations, against many separate systems that share no commit protocol. There is no rollback. Section 10.6 is what that feels like.

The unit of concurrency, blast radius and locking is the root module, not the resource.

8.4 Where does the shop story lie to you?

Four places, and all four are expensive.

Lie one: the ledger is a passive record. It is not. It has authority.

If Asha's ledger says she owns a freezer and the freezer is gone, she buys another, which is fine. But suppose the ledger says she owns nothing, while a freezer she paid for stands in the shop. She will not claim it. She will not maintain it. She will happily buy a second one beside it.

Terraform manages exactly what is in the state file, and is blind to everything else. Objects that fall out of state do not become "unmanaged but fine". They become invisible, unowned, un-deleted, and billed. That is the failure behind bringing an existing object under management, in section 11.5, and behind the sinking feeling of a lost state file, in 11.3.

Lie two: the shelves hold still while she walks them. A stock-take assumes a still shop. A cloud is a live multi-tenant system with other actors, autoscaling, and its own eventual consistency. An API that returns success before the object is fully visible is normal, not exceptional. You saw exactly this shape in eventual-consistency.

So the actual state Terraform reads is a snapshot with a timestamp, and the plan built from it is a prediction that was true a moment ago. Sections 11.7 and 11.8 are the two ways that bites.

Lie three: a half-finished stock-take is a bug. It is not. It is a real outcome.

If Asha is interrupted after buying the freezer but before buying the shelves, the shop is genuinely half-stocked, and the ledger genuinely records one purchase. There is no undo. She cannot un-buy a freezer by closing her eyes.

Terraform has no rollback, and does not pretend to. Recovery is forward: fix the problem and run it again. This is saga without compensations, which means forward recovery only. It is a deliberate choice rather than an omission. Section 10.6 builds it.

Lie four: the ledger lists items. A shop ledger lists items. Terraform's record stores every attribute of every object, including the sensitive ones. Generated passwords. Private keys. Certificate bodies. Connection strings.

Marking a variable sensitive hides it from console output. It does not remove it from the file. Anyone who can read your state file can read your database password. That is section 11.9, and it is the most commonly missed fact in the entire tool. Nothing in the shop picture warns you about it.

A good answer sounds like "The freezer is still there and still working. But nothing now connects it to her shopping list. She cannot prove she bought it, cannot look up its supplier number to return it, and if she reads her list tomorrow she will conclude she needs a freezer and buy a second one. The goods survived. The claim on them did not."

10. How would you build the reconciler yourself, one failure at a time?

In one breath: We build Terraform the way you would build it. Simplest thing that could work, then let it break, then let the breakage force the next repair. Every strange feature of the real tool arrives here as the scar of a failure you just watched.

10.1 What does one plan look like, worked by hand?

Marigold's configuration has four resources. Here are the three states from section 7.3, with real values in them.

Four attribute names appear below, so read them as English first. A network's address range is written cidr_block. How long a load balancer waits before hanging up is idle_timeout. The size of machine a database runs on is instance_class. And whether that database keeps a standby in a second zone is multi_az.

What Asha's files say should exist — the desired state:

resource "aws_vpc" "main"        { cidr_block   = "10.0.0.0/16" }
resource "aws_subnet" "public_a" { vpc_id = aws_vpc.main.id, cidr_block = "10.0.1.0/24" }
resource "aws_lb" "app"          { subnets = [aws_subnet.public_a.id], idle_timeout = 60 }
resource "aws_db_instance" "main"{ instance_class = "db.r6g.large", multi_az = true }

The three things to take from that trace. First, drift did not produce a warning. It produced a plan action, silently, and the fix someone made in the console is about to be destroyed. Second, -/+ appeared because of a provider decision, since the address range of a subnet is immutable in AWS. That is section 7.2's sentence about the provider owning the semantics, made concrete. Third, the plan says 1 to add, 1 to destroy for what is one resource, and that arithmetic surprises people in exactly the situations where it matters most.

Also notice what is not in the plan. The load balancer references the subnet that is about to be replaced. In a real run, replacing the subnet also disturbs everything attached to it. Whether Terraform destroys the old subnet before or after creating the new one decides whether there is an outage. Section 10.6 gives that behaviour its name.

10.2 Version 1 — why isn't comparing config to cloud enough?

Start with the obvious reconciler, and no record at all. Read the config, read the cloud, and diff them.

For creation it works. The config says a load balancer named marigold-app should exist. Ask AWS for load balancers. None named that, so create it. Run again, find it, do nothing. Asha's requirement 2 looks satisfied.

🔮 Predict: Two problems kill version 1 immediately, and one of them is the reason the state file exists at all. What are they?

Reveal

Problem A — it cannot delete. You remove the cache cluster block from your config. Version 1 compares config to cloud: the config does not mention a cache cluster, and the cloud has one. Should it be deleted? Terraform cannot tell. "Not in the config" is true of the cache cluster it created and of every other object in the account: the database another team owns, the bucket the CI system made, the VPC that came with the account. Without a record of what it made, "delete everything not in my config" is the most destructive command ever written.

Problem B — matching by name is a lie. Version 1 has to find "the load balancer for aws_lb.app" by some attribute of the config, and every candidate is wrong. Names are not unique across resource types or accounts. Most resource types have no name at all. Tags are mutable and unenforced. And if you change the name in your config, name-matching finds nothing and creates a second one rather than renaming the first.

Both problems are the same missing thing: a record of which cloud object each address produced. That is the state file, and this is why it cannot be optimised away.

If you predicted "it would be too slow", that is true of very large estates and it is not fatal. These two are fatal.

10.3 Version 2 — what changes once you write down what you made?

Add the record. After creating an object, store the address, the cloud identifier, and the attributes. Now four rules cover everything.

  • Create: the address is in the config and not in the record, so make it, then write it down.
  • Address in both, attributes differ → update it.
  • Address in the record and not in the config → destroy it, then remove the line. This is safe now, because the record only ever contained things Terraform made.
  • In the cloud but not in the record → not yours; never touched.

That last rule is what makes Terraform usable in a real account alongside other people, and it is the source of section 8.4's first lie.

Version 2 satisfies Asha's requirements 1, 2 and 3. It is a genuinely working tool, and plenty of teams have run something like it for years.

🔮 Predict: Version 2 compares config against the record and never looks at the cloud. Priya widens a security group rule by hand at 2am to end an incident. A week later Ben runs version 2 to change an unrelated load balancer timeout. What does the plan say, and what happens to Priya's fix?

Reveal

The plan says 1 to change — just the timeout. Priya's fix is not mentioned, and it is not reverted either. Version 2 has no idea the rule was widened, because it never looked.

That sounds like a lucky escape and it is the opposite. The record now says the rule is narrow; reality says it is wide; and nobody will ever find out until something makes them look. The record is the one artefact the team trusts to describe production, and it is quietly lying. Every plan computed from it is computed from a false premise.

This is the stale-replica problem from caching exactly: a cached copy whose source changed underneath it, serving confident wrong answers. And the fix is the same one that lab prescribed — revalidate before you use it.

10.4 Version 3 — why must a plan look at reality first?

The repair is one step, inserted before the diff. Ask the provider to read the current state of every recorded object, and update the record to match. Then diff the config against the refreshed record. Engineers call that step the refresh, and it is what real Terraform does at the start of every plan.

Now Priya's 2am fix appears in the plan, as a change back to the narrow rule. That is the correct behaviour, and it still surprises everyone. Ben wanted to change a timeout, and he is now also reverting a security fix he knows nothing about. That is failure F1 in section 11.1, and the operational answer is in 13.5.

Three consequences of the refresh are worth carrying with you.

A plan costs API calls in proportion to your resource count. A root module with 800 resources makes roughly 800 read calls per plan. That is why very large root modules become slow enough that people start skipping the refresh, and why section 13.3's advice to split them is a performance argument as well as a blast-radius one.

There is a mode that only looks. In words: update the record from reality and tell me what changed, without proposing to change anything back. The flag is -refresh-only. It is the honest way to detect drift, and it is the basis of 13.5's drift report.

There is also a mode that does not look. The flag is -refresh=false, and skipping the refresh puts you straight back into version 2, with version 2's lie. Use it knowing that.

10.5 Version 4 — what happens when Asha and Ravi apply at once?

Marigold now has four engineers, and the record lives in a shared bucket that Arjun set up. Version 3 was safe on one laptop. Watch it meet a second person.

Asha and Ravi both plan at 14:32. Both read the same record. Asha's plan says "change the timeout". Ravi's says "add a subnet". Both apply.

The record carries a counter that goes up by one on every write. Engineers call it the serial, and here it starts at 41.

14:32:00  Asha  reads record  (serial 41)
14:32:01  Ravi  reads record  (serial 41)
14:32:10  Asha  applies: timeout changed in AWS
14:32:14  Asha  writes record (serial 42, containing the new timeout)
14:32:20  Ravi  applies: subnet created in AWS
14:32:25  Ravi  writes record — built from serial 41, plus his subnet
                    -> the record now describes the OLD timeout and the new subnet

The load balancer's timeout really is 60 in AWS. The record says 30. Asha's work has been erased from the record, not from the cloud. Next time anyone plans, Terraform will helpfully offer to change the timeout back.

🔮 Predict: You have seen this exact failure before, twice, in two different labs. Name the failure, and name the two mechanisms that fix it — one that Terraform actually uses for the file, and one it uses for saved plans.

Reveal

It is the lost update — read-modify-write on a shared mutable object with no concurrency control, from optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking. Ravi's write was computed from a version that was no longer current, and SQL's version of this was that lab's central result.

The pessimistic fix, which is what Terraform actually does for the state file: take a lock before the refresh and hold it until the record is written. Ravi's apply waits, or fails with Error acquiring the state lock naming Asha and when she started. Everything from distributed-locking applies, including its limits. The lock is a lease with an owner recorded in it. A crashed apply leaves it held. And force-unlock is the manual override that exists precisely because slow and dead are indistinguishable from outside.

The optimistic fix, which Terraform uses for saved plans: the state file carries a serial number that increments on every write, and a lineage identifying the state's history. A plan saved with -out=tfplan records the serial it was built against, and applying that saved plan when the serial has moved is refused as stale. That is WHERE version = ?, the first-committer-wins rule, carrying proof-of-what-you-read exactly as your locking lab described.

Both mechanisms exist because they answer different questions: the lock stops two applies overlapping, and the serial check stops one apply executing a decision made against a world that has since moved.

If you named only the lock, you have the common answer. The serial is the half most people never notice, and section 11.7 is what it does not protect.

10.6 Version 5 — what is left behind when an apply stops halfway?

Version 4 is safe against two people. Now watch one person fail in the middle.

Marigold applies a change of 7 resources. The graph walk reaches resource 4, an RDS database instance, and AWS returns an error, because the chosen instance class is not available in that availability zone.

14:40:00  aws_vpc.main               no change
14:40:00  aws_subnet.public_a        CREATED   subnet-0f1e2d3c   -> recorded
14:40:02  aws_subnet.public_b        CREATED   subnet-0c4b5a69   -> recorded
14:40:03  aws_security_group.app     CREATED   sg-07d8e9f0       -> recorded
14:40:31  aws_db_instance.main       ERROR: InvalidParameterValue
14:40:31  apply halts. aws_lb.app, aws_autoscaling_group.app: never attempted.

🔮 Predict: What is in the cloud, and what is in the record, one second after that error?

Reveal

Three new objects exist in the cloud, and the record correctly lists all three. Nothing is rolled back. Nothing is orphaned either — the record was written as each resource completed, which is the important half.

Terraform has no rollback, and this is a design choice rather than a gap. Rolling back would mean destroying resources it just created, in order to recover from a partial success. For a database that is data loss. For a load balancer it is an outage. And a rollback can itself fail, leaving you somewhere worse than either end state.

So the recovery model is forward-only: fix the configuration, run again, and the reconciler converges from wherever it actually is. This works only because of the property the whole tool is built on — the desired state is absolute, not relative. A script that says "create three subnets" cannot be re-run from the middle. A description that says "there should be three subnets" can be re-run from anywhere.

You have met this trade before: saga is a sequence of local transactions with compensations — backward recovery. Terraform is the same shape with the compensations deliberately removed and forward recovery in their place. And the reason it can get away with that is the reason sagas cannot: infrastructure operations are re-runnable to convergence, whereas "charge the customer" is not.

Two refinements come out of this failure, and both are settings you will meet in real code. Terraform groups them under the word lifecycle.

The first says: for a replacement, make the new object before destroying the old one. It is written create_before_destroy. The default is destroy-first, which for anything serving traffic is an outage window. Destroy-first is the default because create-first is impossible when a unique name or address is involved.

The second says: refuse to plan any destroy of this resource at all. It is written prevent_destroy. A blunt instrument, and exactly right on a production database. It is section 7.4's -/+ catastrophe, stopped by a fence.

There is a third, which tells Terraform to stop caring about particular attributes. It is written ignore_changes. It is the correct answer when something outside Terraform legitimately owns a value. Two examples: the server count of an autoscaling group, written desired_capacity, and a tag applied by a compliance system. Used well it is essential. Used to silence a confusing diff, it hides drift permanently.

10.7 Version 6 — how does deleting one subnet destroy four?

Everything so far has treated the address as fixed. Here is what happens when it is not. This is the most destructive detail in the tool, and it is where consistent-hashing comes back.

Marigold has five private subnets. Asha wrote them the compact way, with a list of address ranges and one block that repeats itself once per entry.

resource "aws_subnet" "private" {
  count      = length(var.subnet_cidrs)
  cidr_block = var.subnet_cidrs[count.index]
}

Reveal

Here is the arithmetic, and it is worth reading slowly:

| Address | Record says | Config now says | Action | |---|---|---|---| | private[0] | 10.0.1.0/24 | 10.0.1.0/24 | none | | private[1] | 10.0.2.0/24 | 10.0.3.0/24 | -/+ replace | | private[2] | 10.0.3.0/24 | 10.0.4.0/24 | -/+ replace | | private[3] | 10.0.4.0/24 | 10.0.5.0/24 | -/+ replace | | private[4] | 10.0.5.0/24 | (nothing) | - destroy |

Nothing is wrong with Terraform's reasoning. It is doing precisely what the record and the config say. The bug is in the identity scheme: with count, an object's address is its position in a list, so removing an element renumbers everything after it, and every renumbered address now points at an object whose attributes no longer match.

If you predicted "one destroy", you were reasoning about what Ben meant. Terraform reasons about keys, and Ben changed four of them.

Now name the bug, because you have already met it. From consistent-hashing:

The bug: hash(key) % n makes every key's location a function of a global property of the cluster, so changing the machine count changes almost every answer at once — measured at 100→101 machines, modulo moves 99.0% of keys where only 1.0% had to move.

count is % n. The subnet's identity is a function of a global property: its position in a list. So changing the list changes almost every identity at once. Here the numbers are smaller and the objects are bigger.

Case Things disturbed Things that had to move Churn
Modulo hashing, 100→101 machines 99.0% of keys 1.0% of keys 99× more than needed
count, 5 subnets minus one 4 of 5 objects 1 of 5 objects 4× more than needed

Same bug, same shape. Read the right-hand column of that table as 80% churn to do 20% of the work. And this time the things being destroyed and recreated are not cache entries. They are subnets, and in the general case databases.

The fix is the same fix, too: make identity a function of the thing, not of where it sits. Terraform's version of that is for_each, which takes a map of names to values instead of a list.

variable "subnets" {
  default = {
    app_a = "10.0.1.0/24"
    app_b = "10.0.2.0/24"     # <- delete this line
    app_c = "10.0.3.0/24"
    app_d = "10.0.4.0/24"
    app_e = "10.0.5.0/24"
  }
}

resource "aws_subnet" "private" {
  for_each   = var.subnets
  cidr_block = each.value
}

Now the addresses are aws_subnet.private["app_a"], ["app_b"] and so on. Ben deletes app_b, and the plan is:

Plan: 0 to add, 0 to change, 1 to destroy.
  - aws_subnet.private["app_b"]

One destroy. Nothing else moves. That is the same guarantee consistent hashing bought — the ring's monotonicity, where adding or removing a node moves only the keys that must move and never shuffles the others sideways. for_each is monotone under insertion and deletion. count is not.

The rule, stated so it survives without the analogy: use count only when the instances are genuinely interchangeable and the number is the only thing that ever changes. Three identical NAT gateways qualify. So does a resource that exists zero or one times, written count = var.enabled ? 1 : 0. Use for_each whenever the instances have identities — which is nearly always, because "the subnet in zone b" is an identity even when it looks like item 2 of a list.

And when you get it wrong, the repair is a moved block. It tells Terraform that an address changed without the object changing.

moved {
  from = aws_subnet.private[2]
  to   = aws_subnet.private["app_c"]
}

That is a record edit, not an infrastructure change. It rewrites the key in the ledger, so the same cloud object is now filed under a new name. The older, imperative form is terraform state mv, run by hand. A moved block is better, because it is reviewable by Meera, is applied by everyone automatically, and cannot be forgotten by the colleague who pulls your branch.

10.8 Why must Terraform know every address before it does anything?

One rule ties versions 1 to 6 together. Once you see it, several of Terraform's most confusing error messages become obvious.

The set of addresses must be fully known at plan time. Not the attribute values; those may be unknown. The addresses. Because addresses are the keys of the record, and a plan is a statement about keys.

That is why asking for_each to loop over a value that depends on another resource's not-yet-created attribute fails with this well-known message:

Error: Invalid for_each argument
The "for_each" value depends on resource attributes that cannot be determined
until apply.

People read that as a limitation. It is a safety property. If the set of addresses were only discoverable during the apply, then a partially-failed apply from 10.6 would leave Terraform unable to say what it owns. The whole forward-recovery model depends on the record being trustworthy after a crash, so it would collapse.

Its sibling is the marker Terraform prints for a value the cloud has not generated yet:

  + aws_lb.app
      + arn         = (known after apply)
      + dns_name    = (known after apply)
      + idle_timeout = 60

A plan is a prediction, and any value the cloud generates cannot be predicted. Everything downstream of an unknown is also unknown. So a plan containing a new resource is systematically less informative than a plan that only changes existing ones.

This matters for review. Plan: 12 to add tells Meera much less than Plan: 0 to add, 1 to change does, and reviewers should be told so.

CHECKPOINT — you have the intermediate model. You can read a plan, predict what an apply will do, and explain why the state file is unavoidable. You can also say exactly why count is dangerous and when it is not, which is better than most people using Terraform daily. Next: the failures, each tied to the guarantee it bends.

Say it back. Without using the words count, index or key, explain to Omar why deleting one line from a list of five destroyed four subnets.

A good answer sounds like "Terraform did not file those subnets under their names. It filed them under their place in the queue: first, second, third. Delete the second one and everybody behind it shuffles forward a place. Now the thing filed as 'third' is a different subnet from the one that was filed as 'third' yesterday, so Terraform throws the old one away and builds the new one. Nothing shuffles if you file things under their names instead."

11. What does it look like when it goes wrong?

In one breath: Ten failures, each one a night in somebody's life. Four of them arrive with an error message. The other six arrive as silence, and those are the ones to read twice.

They are ordered by how often you will actually meet them, which is not the order tutorials use. Every one of them happens to somebody you already know: Asha, Ravi, Priya, Meera, Arjun, Ben, Dev and Omar all get a turn.

Reveal

Six of the ten are silent, and they are the expensive ones: F1, F3, F5, F7, F9 and F10 all complete without an error message. F2 is silent without a lock and loud with one, which is the whole argument for the lock.

A red error stops you before the damage. A green apply that quietly reverted a security fix, or quietly left a database unmanaged, hands you the damage and a clean exit code. If you guessed that the loud ones are worse, that is the natural instinct, and it is exactly backwards.

11.1 F1 — why does a timeout change undo Priya's 2am fix?

In the simulator, choose "The 2am fix". You can also build it by hand: click Someone edits it in the console on the security group, then press Plan.

Priya widened the rule six weeks ago and told nobody. Ben opens a pull request that changes a load balancer timeout, and nothing else.

The refresh from section 10.4 reads the widened rule into the record. The diff against the config then proposes narrowing it back. That proposal appears mixed in with the thing Ben actually wanted, often as a one-line ~ among twenty lines, and it is applied without comment.

Terraform is doing its job perfectly here. The configuration is the desired state, and reality no longer matches it. There is no notion of "this difference is deliberate" unless somebody encodes one.

Convergence survives: after the apply, reality matches the config, which is the whole promise. What is lost is an assumption everybody held who was not in the 2am incident — that a change to a load balancer timeout only changes a load balancer timeout.

Three habits fix it, in order of value. Read the whole plan, and make that a review rule: a plan touching resources unrelated to the pull request's stated purpose is a blocked review, not a curiosity. Run -refresh-only on a schedule, so drift arrives as a daily report rather than as a surprise inside someone else's change. And close the loop on emergency changes: the incident is not resolved until the fix is in the configuration, because otherwise the next apply is a scheduled re-incident.

11.2 F2 — what breaks when two applies overlap?

In the simulator, set State lock: off, then press Second engineer applies too. Run it again with the lock on and watch the difference.

Without a lock you get section 10.5's timeline exactly: a lost update on the record. Reality holds both changes. The record holds one. Every future plan is then computed from a false premise, and the first symptom is usually a plan proposing to "fix" something that was never broken.

There is a worse version. The two writes interleave inside the file itself, and the state is corrupt rather than merely wrong.

The mechanism is read-modify-write on a shared mutable object with nothing guarding it, which is the exact shape from optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking.

With Arjun's lock in place, Ravi's apply fails cleanly: Error acquiring the state lock, naming who holds it and since when. Notice the shape of that outcome. It is a fast, loud failure, which is the good kind.

So the lock buys the record's correctness. Without it, what you lose is the trustworthiness of the only artefact that knows what you own.

A remote backend with locking is not optional for a team; it is the first thing Arjun set up. Turn on versioning on the bucket holding the state, so a corrupt write is recoverable rather than terminal. Know that force-unlock exists for the crashed-apply case, and know why it is dangerous: distributed-locking established that slow and dead are indistinguishable from outside, so force-unlocking a lock whose holder is merely slow gives you two concurrent applies with your blessing.

11.3 F3 — what is left when the state file is gone?

In the simulator, press Delete the state file, then Plan.

Arjun's heart stops, because the plan proposes to create everything, from scratch, alongside the infrastructure that already exists and is still serving production traffic:

Plan: 47 to add, 0 to change, 0 to destroy.

The cause is section 8.4's first lie. Terraform manages what is in the record and is blind to everything else. With an empty record it owns nothing, so every config block is a thing that does not exist yet.

Say the good news out loud, because the panic suggests otherwise. Your infrastructure is untouched. Nothing has been damaged. Your configuration is also intact. What is lost is the mapping. You still own everything; you can no longer prove it, and Terraform can no longer manage it.

Prevention is cheap and total: remote state, bucket versioning, and never letting the state file live only on one laptop because someone added it to .gitignore and stopped there. Recovery is import, which is section 11.5, and for a large estate it is counted in days. This is the failure that turns "we'll set up remote state later" into a decision with a number attached.

11.4 F4 — what does a half-finished apply leave behind?

In the simulator, use Make apply fail at step N, then step through it.

You get section 10.6's timeline. Some resources created and recorded. The failing one absent. Everything after it never attempted. terraform apply exits non-zero with the provider's error, and Ben has to decide what to do next.

No transaction spans several independent cloud APIs, and Terraform does not pretend otherwise. Forward recovery instead of rollback.

What survives is the record's accuracy: it lists exactly what was created, which is precisely what makes re-running safe. What you lose is atomicity, which was never offered. And a partially-built environment can be worse than either endpoint. A security group that exists without the rules that were going to constrain it is briefly more open than both the before and after states.

Re-run after fixing, and expect that to be normal rather than alarming. Two sharp edges are worth knowing. A resource whose creation succeeded but whose provisioner failed is marked for replacement on the next run; modern Terraform expresses that as -replace=ADDRESS rather than the older taint command. And an apply interrupted by a lost network connection or a killed CI job may not have written its final state, which is F2's corruption case arriving by a different road. Bucket versioning again. In CI, make applies non-cancellable, or accept that cancelling one is an incident.

11.5 F5 — what happens to something Terraform never knew about?

In the simulator, press Someone creates it in the console. A resource appears in the Cloud column with no row in State.

Then nothing happens. Terraform ignores it forever. Omar keeps paying for it forever.

And if the configuration later describes something with the same name, the apply fails with a provider error about a duplicate. That is the lucky outcome, because the alternative for resource types with no name is a silent second copy.

The cause is section 10.3's fourth rule: in the cloud, not in the record, not yours.

Safety survives, and it is the reason the rule exists at all: Terraform will never destroy something it did not create. What is lost is completeness. Your configuration is no longer a description of your infrastructure, only of the part Terraform happens to manage, and the gap between those two is invisible.

Dev brings it under management with an import block, which is declarative and reviewable:

import {
  to = aws_subnet.private["app_f"]
  id = "subnet-0f9e8d7c"
}

He then runs a plan. Terraform reads the real object, writes it into the record at that address, and shows him the difference between it and the configuration. That difference is usually enlightening, because hand-made resources rarely match what anyone would have written. (The import block form is available in recent Terraform; the older imperative terraform import command does the same job one resource at a time.)

The strategic answer is to prevent the gap rather than close it repeatedly: deny console write access in production and make Terraform the only path. That is section 13.6.

11.6 F6 — how does one character delete the production database?

In the simulator, edit an immutable attribute on the database row, press Plan, and read the -/+.

Meera is reviewing. One line says:

-/+ aws_db_instance.main (forces replacement)

If that is applied, the production database is destroyed and a new, empty one is created. Terraform does not restore the data. There is no undo.

The cause sits in the provider, not in Terraform. The provider declares each attribute as updatable in place or not, and some attributes are genuinely immutable in the cloud API — a subnet's CIDR, an instance's availability zone, many name fields. Changing one leaves the provider no option but replacement, and the plan says so, in a line that looks much like the other lines.

Honesty survives here, completely. Terraform tells you, every time, in the plan, before doing it. This failure is always preventable by reading. What is lost, if you do not read, is everything in the resource.

Three controls, each of which stops it alone. Put lifecycle { prevent_destroy = true } on every stateful production resource; it is the fence that turns a catastrophe into a failed plan. Make forces replacement a searched-for string in code review and in CI, so a plan containing it on a stateful resource requires a second approver. And when a replacement is genuinely intended, do it deliberately: snapshot first, and consider create_before_destroy so there is a working object at every moment.

11.7 F7 — why isn't a reviewed plan a contract?

This one is hard to stage cleanly. The simulator approximates it: press Someone edits it in the console between a Plan and an Apply.

Meera reviewed a plan showing one change and approved it. Between the plan and the apply, reality moved. That gap might be thirty seconds, or a lunch break, or a CI queue. The apply does something the reviewed plan did not describe.

A plan is a prediction computed from a snapshot, which is section 8.4's second lie. Terraform's protection is the optimistic check from section 10.5: a saved plan records the state serial it was built against, and is refused if that serial has moved. But that protects against the state changing, not against the cloud changing beneath an unchanged state.

So the record's consistency survives, and with a saved plan you also keep the guarantee that you are not applying a decision made against a superseded record. What is lost is the idea that a reviewed plan is a contract. It is a well-informed forecast.

In CI, always run plan -out=tfplan and apply that artefact, never re-planning at apply time. Otherwise the thing a human approved and the thing that runs are two different computations. Keep the gap between plan and apply short. And treat a large gap, such as an approval that sat for two days, as grounds to re-plan and re-review.

11.8 F8 — why does the same apply fail once and then succeed?

Not simulated; this one is a provider-level behaviour.

Arjun's apply creates a permission role and then, immediately, something that references it. The second step fails, saying the role does not exist. He runs it again, changing nothing, and it succeeds.

Cloud control planes are distributed systems with their own propagation delay. An API returns success when the write is accepted, not when it is visible everywhere. IAM, the AWS identity service, is the classic offender. You met this precise shape in eventual-consistency; here it appears as a flaky apply.

Correctness on retry survives — the reconciler converges. What is lost is the assumption that an apply either works or is broken. Some applies simply need to be run twice, and telling that apart from a real failure is a judgement call.

Good providers implement retry-with-backoff internally, so this is less common than it was. When it persists, the honest fix is a depends_on that makes the ordering explicit, rather than a sleep. And be suspicious of any runbook step that says "if it fails, just run it again" without saying why. That sentence hides both this failure and several worse ones.

11.9 F9 — who else can read your database password?

Not simulated. Click the database row in the simulator and read the note.

Nothing happens, until someone reads the file. The state file contains every attribute of every managed object. Generated passwords. Private keys. Certificate bodies. All in plain text.

That is not an oversight. The record's job is to hold a full attribute snapshot so the next plan can diff against it, and it cannot diff against something it did not store. Writing sensitive = true on a variable suppresses the value in console output. It does not remove it from the file.

The diff's correctness survives. What is lost is any assumption that the state file is merely metadata. It is a credential store that nobody labelled as one, and Omar's auditors will eventually ask about it.

Treat the state backend as a production secret store: encryption at rest, a bucket policy tighter than the rest of your account, access logging, and no state file in git, ever. Better still, avoid putting secrets in Terraform at all. Have Terraform create the container, such as a Secrets Manager entry or a KMS key. Let something else populate it, so the value never enters the record.

11.10 F10 — what does one root module for the whole company cost?

In the simulator, set the root module to include every resource, then watch the lock and the plan time.

Every change to anything locks everything. A plan takes eight minutes, because it refreshes 900 resources. Two teams cannot ship at once. And the blast radius of one bad apply is the entire company. So is the blast radius of one terraform destroy run in the wrong directory.

The cause is section 7.8's sentence: the root module is the unit of state, lock, plan and blast radius. Nothing else in Terraform is.

Correctness survives. A giant root module is not wrong. What is lost is team throughput, plan speed, and any containment of mistakes.

Arjun splits it by rate of change and by blast radius. Networking, which changes rarely and breaks everything, goes in one place. Applications, which change hourly and break one thing, go in another. Data stores go in a third. Cross-module references become explicit inputs: pass values in as variables from a pipeline, rather than reaching into another module's state, which recreates the coupling you split to avoid. The cost of splitting is real and is paid in plumbing. Pay it anyway.

12. What does Terraform actually promise?

In one breath: One sentence, with four conditions attached, and every one of the four conditions is a failure you just read. What Terraform sells is convergence. It has never sold atomicity.

12.1 What is the one real guarantee?

Here it is, in full:

If the record is accurate and the apply completes without error, then reality matches your configuration for every resource Terraform manages.

Read the conditions, because all four do real work.

"If the record is accurate" — sections 11.3 and 11.5 are the two ways it is not.

"If the apply completes" — section 11.4 is what a partial apply leaves you.

"reality matches your configuration" — as understood by the provider. If the provider's schema does not model an attribute, Terraform cannot manage it and will not notice it changing.

"for every resource Terraform manages" — which is exactly the set in the record, and nothing else in your account.

That is the whole promise, and it is worth a great deal. It is convergence, not atomicity.

12.2 What is explicitly not guaranteed?

Four things about the run itself:

Not guaranteed Consequence Where you saw it
Atomicity A failed apply leaves a partial world; there is no rollback 10.6, 11.4
That the plan is what happens A plan is a prediction from a snapshot 10.8, 11.7
Ordering beyond the graph Independent resources run in parallel, in an unspecified order 7.5
Idempotence of provider operations That is the provider's responsibility, and providers have bugs 7.2, 11.8

And four things about the record and the world around it:

Not guaranteed Consequence Where you saw it
That the record matches reality Only true immediately after a successful refresh 10.4, 11.1
Protection from your own destroy terraform destroy is a supported command 11.6, 11.10
Secrecy of state contents Everything is stored in plain text 11.9
That two runs of the same config produce identical infrastructure Provider defaults, cloud-generated values and AMI lookups drift over time 13.4

That last row deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the most frequently over-claimed property of infrastructure as code.

Terraform gives you reproducible descriptions, not reproducible infrastructure. Pin your provider versions, which is what .terraform.lock.hcl is for. Pin your inputs too, and you narrow the gap a great deal. But a configuration that says "the latest Ubuntu AMI" means something different this month than last, and that is a property of the configuration, not a failure of the tool.

12.3 What was traded away to get it?

Four trades, and the third one is the deep one.

Speed, for reviewability. Plan-then-apply is slower than clicking. That is the point. The slowness is where the review happens.

Expressiveness, for predictability. HCL is deliberately not a general programming language. You cannot loop arbitrarily, cannot call out to anything, and must know your resource addresses before you run. Section 10.8 explains why the last one is a feature rather than a wart. When people find this constraining they reach for CDK or Pulumi, which trade it back — and inherit a different class of problem, where a plan depends on the behaviour of a program.

A single source of truth, for a second source of truth. This is easy to miss, so slow down. Before Terraform, the cloud was the only place the truth lived, and you could not review it. After Terraform there are two places, the config and the record, plus the cloud.

Keeping three things in agreement is now a permanent job. Every failure in section 11 is a disagreement among those three. You did not remove a problem. You exchanged "I cannot see the truth" for "I must maintain the truth in three places", and that is a very good trade made with open eyes.

Cloud-native abstractions, for portability of workflow. Terraform's model is the same for AWS, Cloudflare, GitHub and Datadog, which is enormous. It is not the same as being cloud-agnostic. A configuration full of aws_* resources does not become an Azure configuration, and anyone promising you otherwise is selling something.

12.4 Which three sentences should you memorise?

These three are the lab's invariants. Everything else is a consequence of them.

  1. Terraform manages exactly what is in the record. Not what is in your config; not what is in your account. Everything in 11.3 and 11.5 follows.
  2. The address is the identity, so anything that moves an address moves an object. count renumbering, module renames, and refactors all destroy and recreate unless you tell Terraform the address moved.
  3. A plan is a prediction and an apply is not a transaction. Read the whole plan; expect to re-run.

CHECKPOINT — you have the engineering model. You can state what Terraform guarantees, in one sentence with four qualifications, and defend each qualification with a failure. What remains is how a team runs this without hurting itself.

Say it back. A colleague says: "Terraform is idempotent, so applying twice is always safe." Answer them in two sentences, without using the word idempotent.

A good answer sounds like "Running it twice is safe only if the picture it holds of your infrastructure is still true, and if it managed to finish last time. Either of those can be false. Someone edited the console, or an earlier run died halfway. Then the second run is not a repeat of the first; it is a new decision made from different facts."

In one breath: Move the record somewhere shared and locked, split it into pieces small enough to reason about, and put the plan in front of a human before it runs. Everything else in this section is detail on those three.

13.2 What does "your IaC is your disaster recovery plan" actually mean?

Now the AWS lab's assertion from section 1 can be defended precisely, and also qualified, which the original claim was not. Omar runs the quarterly drill, so this is his section.

What the configuration genuinely gives him: every resource whose creation is a pure function of your inputs can be recreated elsewhere by changing a region variable and applying. Networks, subnets, security groups, load balancers, scaling groups, roles, policies, DNS records. In the AWS lab's terms, that is almost the whole diagram.

What it does not give him, and this is the part the assertion skipped, is four separate things.

Data. The configuration creates an empty database. Recovery is configuration plus a restored snapshot, and the restore is the long pole.

State. Omar's Terraform record describes objects in the region that just died. A second region needs its own state, which means the disaster recovery configuration must have been structured for two regions before the disaster — not adapted during it.

Quotas. Straight from the AWS lab's F4: quotas are per account, per region. An apply in the recovery region will get partway through and start failing on limits nobody raised. A configuration that has never been applied in the recovery region is a hypothesis.

Provider and module versions. A configuration that has not been applied for a year may not apply at all, because the provider has moved. The dependency lock file is what makes this survivable.

So the honest version of the claim: infrastructure as code converts disaster recovery from an unbounded task into a bounded one, and the bound is only real if you have measured it. Which is the same conclusion the AWS lab reached about its recovery time objective, its RTO, by a different road: an untested recovery is a wish.

13.3 Where should the state boundaries go?

The one decision that matters is where the state boundaries go, because that is where locks, plans and blast radii go. Sections 7.8 and 11.10 are why. Here is a layout that works for most teams:

live/
  prod/
    network/      <- own state: VPC, subnets, routing. Changes monthly.
    data/         <- own state: RDS, S3. Changes rarely. prevent_destroy everywhere.
    services/     <- own state: ASGs, load balancers, ECS. Changes hourly.
  staging/
    network/  data/  services/
modules/
  network/        <- reusable, versioned, no provider or backend blocks inside
  service/

Three rules come out of this lab rather than out of taste.

Split by rate of change, so a fast-moving thing does not hold a lock against a slow-moving one, and a routine deploy cannot propose to touch your VPC.

Modules do not configure providers or backends. A module is a function; the root module is the program. Mixing them makes a module unusable in any context but the one it was written for.

Pass values between layers explicitly, as pipeline variables or via a parameter store, rather than reading another layer's state. Reaching into another state file recreates the coupling you split to remove, and it adds a read dependency on a file that layer's owners believe is private.

There is a tempting alternative, and it is usually wrong. Terraform's workspace feature gives one configuration several states. For environments it is normally the wrong tool, because production and staging then share a configuration, a provider, a backend and often an account. A mistake reaches both, and environments cannot legitimately differ in shape. Workspaces suit short-lived parallel copies of the same thing — per-developer sandboxes, per-branch preview stacks. Directories suit environments.

13.4 What does the pipeline look like, and why each step?

The workflow most teams converge on, with the reason for each step attached.

  1. Pull request opens. CI runs fmt -check, validate, a linter (tflint) and a security scanner (tfsec/checkov).
  2. CI runs terraform plan -out=tfplan with read-only credentials, and posts the plan as a comment. The plan is the diff being reviewed; the HCL change is only the cause of it.
  3. Meera reads it. Rules worth encoding: any -/+ forces replacement on a stateful resource needs a second approver; any resource touched that is unrelated to the pull request's purpose is a blocking question, because that is F1 arriving.
  4. On merge, CI applies the saved tfplan — the artefact, not a fresh plan, because of F7. Credentials for apply live only in the pipeline.
  5. Nightly, a -refresh-only run reports drift, which is F1 again, this time caught early.

The security scanners are worth more than they look. Most of the AWS lab's F7, over-broad IAM, is catchable by a static rule before it ever reaches an account.

13.6 Which habits prevent whole classes of failure?

Nobody has write access to production in the console. This is the single most effective control, because it makes F1 and F5 structurally impossible rather than culturally discouraged. Break-glass access exists, is audited, and its use ends with a configuration change.

prevent_destroy on everything stateful, from day one, not after the incident.

Bucket versioning on state, so F2's corruption and F4's interrupted write are recoverable.

Pin everything: provider versions in the lock file, module versions by tag, and AMIs by identifier rather than "latest" where an identical rebuild matters.

moved blocks for every refactor, so a rename never reaches an apply as a destroy.

Small, frequent applies. A plan with four changes is reviewable. A plan with ninety is rubber-stamped, and every failure in section 11 hides more easily in ninety.

13.7 What about the neighbours, honestly?

CloudFormation, and the CDK which generates it, is AWS's own. Its state is managed by AWS, inside the service, which removes F2 and F3 entirely: no file to lose, no lock to run. In exchange it is AWS-only, its rollback behaviour can leave stacks in states that need manual rescue, and its iteration loop is slower. If you are AWS-only and value never handling a state file, this is a real argument and worth weighing rather than dismissing.

Pulumi and CDK for Terraform use the same reconciler model, with a general programming language instead of HCL. You gain expressiveness and lose section 12.3's predictability: your plan now depends on the behaviour of a program.

Kubernetes operators and Crossplane are a continuous reconciler running in a cluster, rather than one you invoke. The mechanism you learned here is the same three-state comparison. The difference is that it runs every few seconds forever, which makes F1 self-correcting, and makes the "silently undoes the 2am fix" problem constant rather than occasional.

Ansible and friends are configuration management, not infrastructure provisioning. Ansible is mostly stateless: it re-derives the current state from the machine each run instead of keeping a record. That works because a machine can be inspected in a way that "which of these 900 cloud objects are mine" cannot.

And a licensing note you need, because it affects tool choice. In August 2023 HashiCorp changed Terraform's licence from the open-source MPL to the Business Source License. The community forked the last MPL version as OpenTofu, now a Linux Foundation project, which remains largely a drop-in alternative. IBM subsequently acquired HashiCorp. For most teams this is a procurement question rather than a technical one, and the two tools' mechanisms are the ones taught in this lab either way.

Which pairs get confused, and what separates them?

  • Terraform vs Ansible — Terraform provisions infrastructure and keeps a record of what it owns; Ansible configures machines and re-derives their state each run. The record is the difference, and it exists because you can inspect a machine but you cannot inspect "which of these cloud objects are mine".
  • Terraform vs CloudFormation — same reconciler idea; CloudFormation's state is held by AWS inside the service, so there is no file to lose and no lock to run, at the cost of being AWS-only.
  • Declarative vs idempotent — declarative means you describe the end state; idempotent means running twice is the same as running once. Terraform is declarative, which is how it achieves idempotence; a script can be made idempotent without being declarative, and it is much harder.
  • State lock vs .terraform.lock.hcl — mutual exclusion during an apply versus pinned provider versions. Same word, unrelated things.
  • count vs for_each — identity by position in a list versus identity by a stable key. The second one is almost always what you mean.
  • Drift vs a config change — reality moved versus intent moved. A plan shows both in the same output, and telling them apart is a review skill.
  • terraform destroy vs removing a resource block — the first destroys everything in the state; the second destroys one thing. Both are real destroys, and neither asks twice.
  • Terraform vs OpenTofu — a licence fork of the same mechanism; everything in this lab applies to both.

Solutions — Terraform

Attempt first. Rungs 7–8 are worked positions with the reasoning visible, not single right answers — but there are wrong answers, and they are named.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Why a state file is unavoidable

Two independent reasons, and it is worth giving both because people usually only know one.

Reason one: without a record, Terraform cannot delete anything. You remove the cache-cluster block from your config. Comparing config to cloud, the cache cluster is "in the cloud, not in the config" — but so is the database another team owns, the bucket your CI system made, and the default VPC that came with the account. "Delete everything not in my config" is the most destructive command anyone has ever written. The record is what makes deletion safe, because it contains only things Terraform created, so "in the record, not in the config" is a precise and small set.

Reason two: matching by name is a lie. To compare config to cloud, Terraform would have to find "the load balancer belonging to aws_lb.app" by some attribute. Every candidate fails: most resource types have no unique name; tags are mutable and unenforced, so two teams can tag two objects identically; and if you rename something in your config, name-matching finds nothing and creates a second copy rather than recognising the first.

Both are the same missing thing — a mapping from your stable name to the cloud's identifier, which the cloud never knew about because it has never seen your file.

1.2 The three states

  • Desired — what the configuration files say should exist.
  • Recorded — what Terraform believes it created, with a full attribute snapshot: the state file.
  • Actual — what is really in the cloud right now.

The question only recorded-vs-actual can answer: "did something change behind my back?" That is drift. Desired-vs-recorded tells you what you changed; only recorded-vs-actual tells you what someone else changed. This is why a two-way tool is blind to the 2am fix and a three-way tool is not.

1.3

  • (a) A plan is the computed difference between the three states, expressed as an action per resource address, printed before anything happens.
  • (b) +, ~ and - each do one thing. -/+ does two — it destroys and then creates — so it is the only symbol under which an object with data in it can silently become a different, empty object. It is also caused by the provider, not by anything you asked for, which is why it appears in plans people did not expect it in.
  • (c) Drift is reality moving away from the record without Terraform doing it. A config change is intent moving. They appear in the same plan output in the same notation, and telling them apart is a review skill, not a tool feature.

1.4

First error: "idempotent" describes the outcome of applying, not the safety of applying. An apply converges reality to your configuration — which is exactly the problem when reality contains a deliberate emergency change that the configuration does not know about. F1: running apply is how the 2am fix gets reverted. Idempotence guarantees you land on the config; it says nothing about whether the config is currently what you want.

Second error: the guarantee has four conditions (§12.1) and "safe to run whenever" assumes all of them hold. In particular the apply is not atomic (§10.6) and the plan is a prediction (§10.8), so "run it and see" can leave you in a partial state that is worse than either endpoint — for instance a security group created before the rules that were going to constrain it.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1

Left is desired (config), middle is recorded (state file), right is actual (cloud).

Terraform writes only the middle one. It writes to the cloud too, of course — but it writes the cloud by making API calls that it hopes succeed, and it writes the record as its own authoritative bookkeeping. It never writes the config: you do. That asymmetry is why config lives in git and state does not, and why editing state by hand is a last resort.

2.2

aws_lb.app shows idle_timeout in all three columns. Green means all three agree; amber means desired differs from recorded (you asked for a change); red means recorded differs from actual (drift — someone else changed it). The distinction between amber and red is the whole point of the three columns.

2.3

The hand-made security group appears in Cloud with no State row. Terraform does nothing with it, ever. That protects you from the reason-one catastrophe in 1.1: a tool that acted on everything it could see, in a shared account, would delete other teams' infrastructure. The cost of the protection is F5 — the resource is invisible, unmanaged and billed, and your configuration is no longer a description of your infrastructure.

2.4

The address is aws_db_instance.main; the AWS identifier is something like marigold-prod-db / an ARN. You chose the address; AWS chose the identifier. Only the address appears in git — which is precisely why it must be stable, and precisely why §10.7's renumbering is so destructive.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1

No changes. Your infrastructure matches the configuration. — and it still makes one read API call per managed resource, because the refresh happens first (§10.4). This is why a plan on an 800-resource root module is slow even when the answer is "nothing", and it is one of the two arguments for splitting root modules (F10).

3.2

No, the plans are different, and this is the most useful thing in rung 3.

  • You edit the config to 90: desired 90, recorded 60, actual 60. Plan: ~ idle_timeout: 60 -> 90. One change, which you asked for.
  • Someone edits the console to 90: desired 60, recorded 60, actual 90. The refresh pulls 90 into the record, then desired (60) differs from it. Plan: ~ idle_timeout: 90 -> 60 — Terraform proposes to change it back.

Same end value in reality, opposite plans. The lesson: the plan's direction is determined by the config, always. Terraform has no concept of "the newest change wins"; the configuration is the definition of correct, and everything else is something to be corrected.

3.3

  • Deleted in the console: recorded says it exists, actual says it does not, desired says it should. The refresh removes it from the record; the diff then finds a config block with no record entry. Plan: + create. Terraform rebuilds it.
  • Deleted from your config: recorded says it exists, actual says it exists, desired does not mention it. Plan: - destroy.

Opposite actions from what looks like the same event, because the source of the disappearance is what determines the meaning. The record is what lets Terraform tell those two situations apart at all.

3.4 — the centrepiece

With count: 4 destroyed, 3 created. One deletion you asked for, and three resources destroyed and recreated for nothing. The reason is in the address table in §10.7 — removing element 1 renumbers 2→1, 3→2, 4→3, so three addresses now point at objects whose attributes no longer match, and cidr_block is immutable, so each is a replacement.

With for_each: 1 destroyed, 0 created.

If you predicted "1 destroyed" for the count case, you have just experienced the exact failure this section exists for — and so has everyone else. The number is not intuitive because nothing in the configuration looks positional; the positionality is in the record, which you do not normally read.

The connection to name: this is consistent-hashing's opening bug. Identity as a function of a global property (position in a list) versus identity as a function of the thing itself (a key). That lab measured 99% of keys moving on a 100→101 machine change where 1% had to; here it is 80% of resources disturbed where 20% had to. Same shape, and here the objects being destroyed are not cache entries.

3.5

The cloud has both changes — the timeout is 90 and the subnet exists. The record has only one of them, because the second writer computed its update from a copy of the record read before the first writer saved. The config is fine; both changes are in git.

So: reality is right, intent is right, and the bookkeeping is wrong. The next plan will propose to "fix" whichever change got lost — reverting a timeout nobody touched, or creating a subnet that already exists (which will fail with already exists from the provider, and is the lucky version).

This is the lost update from optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking, and that lab's central trap applies here too: nothing errors. Both applies said "Apply complete!".

3.6

With a saved plan (plan -out=tfplan then apply tfplan), Terraform checks that the state's serial has not moved and refuses a stale plan. But a console edit does not move the state serial — it moves the cloud. So the saved plan is still considered valid, and the apply proceeds against a world that has changed. Depending on what was edited, the apply may succeed and quietly overwrite the console change, or fail with a provider error about an unexpected current value.

Without a saved plan (terraform apply typed at a prompt), Terraform re-plans from scratch and shows you the new plan for confirmation — which is safer in this specific respect and much worse in the respect that matters in CI, because the thing a human approved and the thing that runs are then two different computations (F7).

The production rule follows: in CI, always apply a saved plan, and keep the gap between plan and apply short.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1

Lost: the trustworthiness of the record. Not damaged: the cloud. Both changes really happened and both are correct in reality. Nothing is broken in production at the moment of the failure — which is exactly why it goes unnoticed. The damage surfaces later, when someone applies a plan computed from the false record and it undoes real work.

Worth noticing which property survived: convergence, in the sense that each individual apply did converge its own resource. What was lost is the record's role as a shared description, which is requirement 5 from stage 5.

4.2

At the moment of failure: resources 1–3 exist in the cloud and are in the record; resource 4 does not exist; 5–7 were never attempted.

The second plan is shorter because the record is accurate — Terraform knows resources 1–3 already exist and are correct, so it proposes only 4–7. This is the property that makes forward recovery work, and it is why the record being written incrementally during an apply (rather than only at the end) is a deliberate and important design choice. If the record were written only on success, a failed apply would leave three orphans (F5) every time.

4.3

Plan: 47 to add, 0 to change, 0 to destroy.

Lost: the mapping — your ability to manage what you own. Not lost: the infrastructure, which is untouched and still serving traffic, and the configuration, which is still in git. Say both halves out loud, because the panic of this moment suggests the first sentence without the second, and people have made it much worse by acting on the panic — the truly catastrophic version of F3 is applying that plan and building a second copy of everything alongside the first.

4.4

The apply is refused, and — the important part — it is refused at plan time, before anything happens: Error: Instance cannot be destroyed … Resource has lifecycle.prevent_destroy set.

So the control fires at the earliest possible moment, in CI, on the pull request, without a human needing to notice a -/+ in 340 lines. That placement is what makes it valuable: it converts F6 from "a reviewer must be careful" into "the pipeline fails".

4.5

The lock is now held for every change to anything, so two teams cannot ship concurrently; and the plan refreshes all 900 resources, so it takes minutes.

Degraded: throughput and blast-radius containment. Not degraded: correctness — a giant root module produces correct plans and correct applies. That is what makes F10 insidious: there is no bug to find, no error to search for, and no moment at which anyone decides the architecture is wrong. It just gets slower and scarier until someone names it.

5.3 — reordering with no additions or removals

[0] a -> a   no change
[1] b -> c   -/+ replace
[2] c -> b   -/+ replace
[3] d -> d   no change

Two resources destroyed and recreated to change nothing at all. The set of values is identical; only the order changed.

Same phenomenon as 3.4 in one sentence: with count, identity is position, so any operation that changes positions changes identities — and insertion, deletion and reordering all change positions. for_each is immune to all three because a key does not care where it sits.

This is also why sorting a list, or switching to a data source that returns AZs in a different order, can produce a destructive plan out of a change that touched nothing.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 — the tidy repository

The flaw: one root module for all of production (F10). Everything else listed is genuinely good practice.

The symptom they will report, in about six months: "Terraform is slow" and "we keep waiting for each other". A plan refreshes every resource in the state (§10.4), so plan time grows linearly with the estate; and the state lock is held per root module, so a one-line tag change on a service blocks a networking change. Neither presents as a correctness problem, which is why it is never prioritised — it presents as the tool being annoying, and teams respond by running plans less often, which is the actual damage.

The second-order harm, which is worse: a plan that takes eight minutes and touches 900 resources will not be read. F1 and F6 both depend on someone reading the plan, and this design guarantees nobody does.

The fix is §13.3: split by rate of change and blast radius, pass values between layers explicitly.

6.2 — the Tuesday afternoon

What the plan contained: a -/+ aws_db_instance.main (forces replacement) line among 340 lines of ~ tags.

What caused it: almost certainly not the tag. The tag change is what brought the resource into the plan; the replacement came from something else in that resource's configuration that had drifted from, or never matched, reality — or from a provider version upgrade that changed how an attribute is handled. A resource that has not been in a plan for a year can carry a latent replacement that only surfaces the next time anything touches it. This is why "it's only a tag change" is not a safe sentence.

Three controls, each sufficient alone:

  1. lifecycle { prevent_destroy = true } on the database. The plan fails in CI with an explicit error. Cheapest, most effective, and should have been there from day one.
  2. A CI rule that fails the build when the plan contains forces replacement on any resource in a protected list, requiring an explicit override label on the PR. Mechanical, and it makes intent visible.
  3. Splitting data/ into its own root module (§13.3). A tagging PR against services/ then cannot produce a plan that touches the database, because the database is not in that state.

Note what is not on the list: "review more carefully". The reviewer behaved reasonably; 340-line plans are not reviewable, and a control that depends on human vigilance at the end of a long day is not a control.

6.3 — the clean-up

What it achieved: the plans are clean. What it destroyed: the configuration is no longer a description of the infrastructure.

ignore_changes = all tells Terraform to stop comparing desired to actual for those resources. The record still exists, so Terraform still owns them and will still destroy them if you remove the blocks — but it will never correct them, and it will never report that they differ. The desired state is now unmanaged: what is in git and what is in production have no enforced relationship at all.

The next disaster recovery attempt produces the infrastructure as written, not as it actually ran. Every console change made over two years — every widened rule, every raised timeout, every added subnet — is absent, silently, and nobody knows which ones mattered. The team believed they had a tested recovery path; what they have is a configuration that has diverged from production by an unknown amount in an unknown direction.

The legitimate use of ignore_changes is narrow and specific: name the exact attributes that something else legitimately owns (desired_capacity on an autoscaling group, a tag applied by a compliance system). = all is the difference between a scalpel and switching off the lights.

6.4 — the refactor

14 real resources are destroyed and 14 new ones created. Subnets, security groups and anything with data in it are all replaced. If the module contained a database, the data is gone.

The engineer's reasoning — "the resources are identical either way" — is true about the configuration and irrelevant. The record is keyed by address (§12.4's second invariant), the addresses all changed, and Terraform therefore sees 14 records with no config and 14 configs with no record.

What they should have written: moved blocks, one per resource, telling Terraform the address changed without the object changing:

moved {
  from = aws_subnet.private["app_a"]
  to   = module.network.aws_subnet.private["app_a"]
}

The plan then reads Plan: 0 to add, 0 to change, 0 to destroy with a note that 14 resources have moved. moved blocks are reviewable, are applied automatically by everyone who pulls the branch, and cannot be forgotten — which is why they are better than the older terraform state mv run by hand.

The generalisable rule: every refactor that changes an address is a data migration until proven otherwise.

6.5 — the disaster recovery drill

Reason one, from this lab: applying a configuration successfully creates empty infrastructure. The drill proves the configuration is valid and the quotas are sufficient — genuinely valuable — and proves nothing about the thing that actually takes the time, which is restoring the data. The 45-minute figure almost certainly measures terraform apply and excludes a database restore that is measured in hours for anything large.

Reason two, from the amazon-web-services lab (F4): if the drill has been succeeding every quarter, the resources it creates are presumably destroyed afterwards — so the quota headroom exists. But if the drill runs at a smaller scale than production (a common shortcut, since nobody wants to pay for a full second copy quarterly), then the quotas that the real recovery needs have never been exercised, and quotas are per account per region. The drill would then pass forever and the real event would fail on limits.

A third, if you found it: nothing in the drill tests the state for the recovery region — where it lives, whether it survived the event, and whether the team can acquire a lock on it. If the DR state file lives in the region that just died, the recovery plan begins with a problem nobody rehearsed.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 — the deleted state file

First, and before anything else: stop all pipelines and tell everyone not to run Terraform. The single worst outcome available right now is someone applying the 47 to add plan and building a duplicate of production alongside it. That is a bigger incident than the one you have.

Then, in order:

  1. Check for a copy before assuming there is none. Bucket versioning (not enabled here, but check), the CI job artefacts of the last successful apply, a terraform.tfstate.backup next to a local run, or a developer's laptop that ran a plan recently and has a cached copy. Recovering a state file that is a week old is enormously better than starting from zero — you re-import only the difference.
  2. Confirm the infrastructure is untouched. It is; say so out loud to the people who are panicking, because the recovery is a bookkeeping exercise, not an outage.
  3. Rebuild the record by importing, layer by layer, starting with the things nothing depends on (the VPC, then subnets, then security groups, then the rest). Use import blocks so the work is reviewable and repeatable rather than 47 shell commands typed by one person.
  4. After each import batch, run a plan and expect it to be noisy. Hand-made and imported resources rarely match what the configuration says, and each difference is a decision: change the config to match reality, or change reality to match the config. Do not blanket-apply. Resolve them individually.
  5. Only after the plan is empty is the recovery complete.

Realistic cost: days for 47 resources, and it is fiddly rather than hard.

The ten-minute prevention: remote state in S3, with versioning and a bucket policy that denies deletion, plus MFA-delete on anything you truly cannot lose. Versioning alone would have turned this entire incident into restoring the previous object version. This is the cheapest insurance in the tool.

7.2 — mechanical prevention of the replaced database

The control: in CI, after terraform plan -out=tfplan, convert it to JSON (terraform show -json tfplan) and fail the build if any resource whose type or address is on a protected list has an action of ["delete", "create"] or ["delete"].

Why this shape:

  • Mechanical — it reads the plan's structured output, not its prose, so a 340-line plan is exactly as safe as a 4-line one.
  • Does not block ordinary changes — a plan with only ~ and + actions on protected resources passes untouched, which is the overwhelming majority.
  • Allows intentional replacement — the override is a labelled pull request (allow-replace: aws_db_instance.main) requiring a second approver. Making the override explicit, named and reviewed is the point; making it impossible would just push people to bypass the pipeline.

Belt and braces: prevent_destroy = true on the resource as well. The two controls fail differently — prevent_destroy fails at plan time in Terraform itself and cannot be bypassed by a CI misconfiguration; the CI rule catches resources nobody remembered to annotate. Neither subsumes the other.

What not to do: requiring two approvers on all infrastructure PRs. It slows every change to catch a rare one, and approval fatigue makes the second reviewer less careful than the first, not more.

7.3 — emergency changes without permanent drift

The constraint is the interesting part: they must be able to act now, and the guarantee must come from a mechanism rather than a promise.

The process:

  1. Break-glass access exists and is easy. A role that grants console write access in production, assumable by on-call, with no approval step. If it is hard to use, people will find another way and you will lose the audit trail entirely — that is the failure mode to design against first.
  2. Assuming that role automatically opens a ticket and posts to the incident channel. Not a request for permission; a record that it happened. The mechanism, not the person, creates the record.
  3. A scheduled -refresh-only run detects the drift and comments on that ticket with the exact diff — the specific attributes that differ from the configuration. Now the change is described precisely, by a machine, rather than from someone's memory of a 4am hour.
  4. The ticket cannot be closed until the drift is gone, and there are exactly two ways to make it gone: adopt the change into the configuration, or revert it. Both are pull requests. This is the guarantee, and it is mechanical because the drift report keeps reappearing until it is true.
  5. Escalate on age. Drift older than, say, 72 hours pages the team that owns the resource. Without this the ticket rots and you are back where you started with extra process.

The insight to name: you cannot prevent emergency changes and should not try. What you can do is make the record of them automatic and make the drift self-reporting, so the only human judgement required is the one that actually needs judgement — adopt or revert.

7.4 — the cancelled apply

Possible states, and they are genuinely different:

  1. Terraform caught the signal, finished the in-flight operation, wrote the state and released the lock. Best case, and reasonably likely — Terraform handles interrupts gracefully when it can.
  2. Terraform was killed hard. Resources may have been created in the cloud whose IDs never reached the record → orphans (F5). The lock may still be held.
  3. The state write itself was interrupted → a truncated or corrupt state file (F2's worst case).
  4. In-flight cloud operations continued after the process died. An RDS instance being created does not stop because your CI job did; it will finish, exist, and be unrecorded.

How to determine which:

  • Check the lock first. If it is still held, nothing else is running, and you have time. force-unlock only after you have confirmed the process is dead — distributed-locking established that slow and dead are indistinguishable from outside, and force-unlocking a slow holder gives you two concurrent applies with your blessing.
  • Read the CI log to see the last resource Terraform reported completing. That is your boundary between "recorded" and "maybe orphaned".
  • Validate the state file parses and its serial looks sane. Compare against the previous object version in the bucket.
  • Run a plan. A plan proposing to create something you saw succeed in the log is the signature of an orphan.

What to do in each case: (1) re-run. (2) find the orphans against the cloud console or a cost report, then either import them or delete them by hand; then re-run. (3) restore the previous state version from the bucket, then treat as (2). (4) wait for the operation to finish before doing anything, then treat as (2).

The prevention: make applies non-cancellable in CI, or accept that cancelling one is an incident and write that in the runbook. And enable bucket versioning, which is the answer to case 3 and appears in almost every solution in this file.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 — the state architecture

The split, on two axes.

Primary axis — environment and account. prod, staging, dev, each in a separate AWS account (straight from the AWS lab's §7.8), each with its own backend bucket in its own account. This contains F2, F3 and F10 across the largest boundary, and it makes a catastrophic mistake in staging structurally incapable of reaching production.

Secondary axis — rate of change. Within each environment:

Root module Contains Changes Contains which failure
network/ VPC, subnets, routing, transit monthly F10 — a deploy cannot lock or touch the network
data/ RDS, S3, DynamoDB. prevent_destroy on everything rarely F6 — a tagging PR cannot reach a database
platform/ IAM baseline, KMS, logging, shared roles rarely F7 — security changes reviewed separately
services/<name>/ ASG, ALB, ECS per service hourly F2 — teams do not queue behind each other

Regional axis. Each of the above exists per region, with its own state. Not a workspace: a directory, so the two regions can legitimately differ (and they will — see the AWS lab's §10.7 on multi-region being a different kind of problem). The DR region's state lives in the DR region, which is the oversight in 6.5.

That is roughly 3 environments × 2 regions × (3 + N services) root modules. It sounds like a lot; each is small, fast to plan, and independently safe.

How values cross: outputs published to Parameter Store / Terraform Cloud outputs, and consumed as explicit inputs by the layer below, injected by the pipeline. Not terraform_remote_state data sources reaching into another layer's file — that recreates the coupling you split to remove and adds a read dependency on a file the owning team believes is private.

The boundary I refuse to draw: per-resource or per-team state inside a single service. Splitting until each root module holds three resources sounds like maximum containment and is actually the worst outcome: the plumbing between modules becomes larger than the modules, a single logical change becomes four coordinated applies in a required order — which is a distributed transaction you now have to run by hand, with no coordinator and no rollback (your two-phase-commit lab, arriving somewhere it was not invited). The right granularity is the largest unit that one team can deploy atomically, and no smaller.

8.2 — adopting an existing estate

First principle: a half-managed estate is genuinely worse than an unmanaged one, because nobody can tell which half is which. So the adoption strategy is organised around never being ambiguous about ownership.

Order of adoption, and the reasoning:

  1. New things first. From day one, everything new is created by Terraform, in a new account or a clearly-labelled boundary. This is the highest-value, lowest-risk step and it stops the problem growing while you fix it.
  2. Then the stable foundation — networking and IAM. These change rarely, so an import is unlikely to be invalidated while you are doing it, and almost everything else depends on them.
  3. Then stateless compute, service by service, with each team importing its own. Stateless resources are the safest to get wrong: a bad plan destroys and recreates something that recreates cleanly.
  4. Data stores last, and with prevent_destroy written before the import. These are the ones where a mistaken plan is unrecoverable, and by this point the team has practised.

Avoiding ambiguity: a mandatory tag (managed_by = terraform) applied by every module, and a scheduled report listing every resource in the account without that tag. That report is the adoption backlog, it is generated rather than maintained, and it goes to zero visibly.

What I would deliberately never import: anything created and owned by another tool that has its own reconciler — resources managed by a Kubernetes operator, by a vendor's control plane, by CloudFormation stacks somebody else owns. Two reconcilers pointed at one object is a fight neither wins, and each will report drift caused by the other, forever.

"Done" and the timeline: done is when the untagged report is empty and a plan against every root module is clean. Honestly: 6–18 months of part-time work for three years of estate, and the schedule risk is not the importing — it is the negotiation about which team owns each shared resource, which is exactly the finding from the AWS lab's account-structure exercise.

8.3 — Terraform or CloudFormation

My choice: Terraform, for a specific reason rather than popularity — the team will not stay AWS-only. Cloudflare DNS, GitHub repositories, Datadog monitors, PagerDuty schedules and an identity provider are all things a six-person team acquires within two years, and having one reconciler model for all of them is worth more than anything CloudFormation offers. The plan/apply loop is also materially faster to work with, which compounds over thousands of iterations.

The strongest case against my own choice, and it is a real case:

  • CloudFormation removes F2 and F3 completely. There is no state file, so it cannot be lost, corrupted, or written by two people at once; AWS holds it inside the service. For a six-person team with no platform engineer, deleting two entire failure classes is a serious offer, and every solution in this file that ends in "enable bucket versioning" is a problem they simply do not have.
  • It removes the secrets-in-state problem (F9) in the same stroke.
  • It is free, has no licensing question hanging over it (§13.7), and needs no external backend infrastructure to exist before you can start.
  • Against that: CloudFormation's rollback behaviour can leave stacks in states requiring manual rescue — a different failure from Terraform's forward-only model, not obviously a better one — and drift detection is weaker.

The honest summary: Terraform trades two removed failure classes for portability and iteration speed. At six people and AWS-only today, that is a closer call than the industry's enthusiasm suggests, and a team that chose CloudFormation for those reasons has not made a mistake.

8.4 — applying automatically on merge

What must be true for this to be safe:

  1. The plan that was reviewed is the plan that runs — saved artefact, never a re-plan (F7). Without this, auto-apply means nobody has reviewed anything.
  2. Mechanical destroy protection — 7.2's control, plus prevent_destroy on everything stateful. With no human at the apply step, the last line of defence is gone.
  3. Small blast radius per root module (§13.3), so an unattended mistake is bounded by construction.
  4. Fast, reliable rollback-by-roll-forward — revert the PR, which produces a plan that returns the previous state. This works for stateless resources and does not work at all for anything that was destroyed.
  5. Alerting on apply failures that reaches a human quickly, because F4's partial state is now nobody's problem by default.

Which failures get worse: F1 (drift is silently reverted with no human in the loop at all), F4 (a partial apply happens unattended), F6 (no last-second human catch). Which get better: F2 — the pipeline is the only actor, so concurrent applies stop existing; and F5 — nobody is clicking in consoles when merging is easier.

Would I do it? Per layer, differently, and that is the interesting part:

  • services/ — yes. Changes are frequent, resources are stateless, roll-forward works, and the friction of manual applies is a real tax paid hourly. This is where auto-apply earns its keep.
  • network/ — no. Changes are rare, so the friction costs almost nothing; and a bad network change is a total outage that a revert does not fix quickly.
  • data/ — absolutely not. The failure mode is unrecoverable, the change rate is near zero, and "a human definitely looked at this" is worth far more than the convenience it costs.

The generalisable rule: automate the apply where the change rate is high and the failure is recoverable; keep a human where the change rate is low and the failure is not. Notice this is the same axis as §13.3's state split, which is not a coincidence — both are asking "what is the blast radius here?".

8.5 — reading a real plan

The point of this exercise is the last question, so here is the guidance rather than an answer.

A plan for the AWS lab's version 4 architecture, from nothing, would be roughly Plan: 40-60 to add, with the great majority of interesting attributes reading (known after apply) — because everything downstream of a not-yet-created resource is unknowable (§10.8). That plan is nearly unreviewable and that is not a fixable property of the plan. A first-creation plan can only be reviewed by reading the configuration.

The reviewable plans are the incremental ones: Plan: 0 to add, 1 to change. Which produces the actual answer to "what would you change about the pull request, not the code":

  • Make it smaller. One logical change per PR. A plan with four lines gets read; a plan with ninety gets approved.
  • Say what the plan should say, in the PR description, before CI runs it. If the author writes "this should show exactly one ~ on the load balancer" and the plan shows eleven changes, the discrepancy is the review — and it catches drift (F1) and latent replacements (6.2) automatically, with no tooling at all.
  • Separate refactors from behaviour changes. A PR containing moved blocks should contain nothing else, so its plan can be checked against the only acceptable outcome: 0 to add, 0 to change, 0 to destroy.
