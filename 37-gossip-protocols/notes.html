Gossip protocols

Tell a friend: Five hundred people are at the school fête, and the buses have just been moved to a different gate. Nobody is in charge of telling anybody. So Asha turns to the two people nearest her and says it. Each of them turns to two more, and a minute later the whole field knows. Arjun was on the phone under the mango tree, Dev had gone home early, and half the shouts were lost in the noise, and it still got everywhere. That is the whole protocol: once a second, tell two random peers everything you know. The number of people who know then multiplies instead of creeping up by one.

BEGINNER MODEL

1. Why does a machine need to know who else is alive?

In one breath: A machine that has not heard its neighbour died keeps posting work into a hole. Two machines that disagree about the settings send the same request to two different places. And there is nobody to ring five hundred machines and tell them.

You have five hundred machines. Every one of them has to keep two kinds of fact straight.

The first kind is who else is currently working. Send a request to a machine that is gone, and the request simply vanishes.

The second kind is the current shared settings. Which machine owns which data. Which version is live. Who the write leader is.

Nobody can be told these things by a person. Machines join. Machines crash. Machines freeze for eight seconds in the middle of garbage collection and then come back as if nothing happened.

If a machine never finds out that another one has died, it keeps sending it work that disappears. If two machines disagree about the settings, they send the same request to different places, and your data ends up in two homes.

So here is the problem in one line. Five hundred machines, all of which must keep agreeing about a changing set of facts, with no one in charge, on a network that loses messages.

By the end of this lab you will know how a thousand machines agree on who is alive and what the current config is — with no coordinator, no broadcast, no acknowledgements and no retries.

2. What goes wrong when one person has to ring five hundred families?

In one breath: The school office can telephone every family, and it works right up to the evening the office phone is out. Then nobody hears anything at all, and nobody finds out that nobody heard.

Meet a school with 500 families. Ben runs the office. Something changes. Tomorrow's trip is cancelled, and every family has to hear about it tonight.

The obvious plan is Ben's office. He telephones all 500 families. It works, right up until the evening it doesn't.

  • One person makes 500 calls. If each takes a minute, the last family hears eight hours later.
  • If the office line is out, nobody hears anything. Not "some people hear late" — nobody, ever, because Ben was the only one making calls.
  • Every family also has to ring Ben when they move house. So his line is busy all evening with 500 families calling in.

The other obvious plan is that everybody rings everybody. No single point of failure, and it is fast. It also means each family makes 499 calls, and the town makes 249,500 calls. Every evening. Whether or not anything has changed.

🔮 Predict: A third plan. Five families volunteer as relays. Ben rings the five, and each relay rings a hundred families. What breaks?

Reveal

You have not removed the single point of failure. You have made five of them, and now the failure is partial and silent.

If relay number three is out, a hundred families never hear, and nobody notices. From Ben's point of view the call to relay three went through fine.

If you guessed "nothing much, it is five times faster", you are right about the speed and wrong about the risk. Speed was never the thing that hurt.

This is the shape of every hierarchical scheme. It converts a total, obvious failure into a partial, invisible one. That is usually worse, because you find out from the families who missed the trip.

3. Is "one machine in charge" really such a naive answer?

In one breath: Ben's office, translated to servers, is a coordinator. It is not a strawman. Several systems you use every day are built exactly that way.

Translated to servers, Ben's office is a coordinator: one process that holds the real membership list and the real settings. Everyone reports to it, everyone reads from it, and it tells everyone about changes.

This is a completely reasonable design. Kubernetes has etcd. Kafka had ZooKeeper and now has a controller quorum. Redis Cluster has a slot table that a coordinator publishes.

The consistent-hashing lab called this the "fixed slots plus a coordinator" answer, and it found real advantages: exact control, no disagreement window, placement you can move by hand.

So the question is not "is a coordinator bad?". The question is: what does it cost, and what happens when you cannot afford it?

4. What are the three costs, and which one is fatal?

In one breath: A coordinator has to be kept alive, has to be talked to by everybody, and has to be reachable. The third one is the killer, because the network splits exactly when you most need an answer.

The first one: who keeps Ben's office alive?

A coordinator that matters cannot be one process. It becomes three or five, running a consensus protocol (paxos, Raft) to agree on every change. You now operate a distributed database in order to answer "who is alive?".

The second one: what happens when everybody rings Ben?

Every machine reports in. Every machine reads the list. The load grows with the cluster, and it lands on the one component whose failure is total.

The third one: what if you cannot reach Ben at all?

This is the fatal one. When the network splits, the machines on the wrong side have no membership list at all. They are up. Their peers are up. And they cannot see each other's health, because the thing that told them is on the other side.

Asha, Meera and Priya are all standing on the same field, perfectly able to talk to each other. The one thing that could tell them who is present is on the far side of the marquee.

That is cap-theorem arriving exactly where you least want it. A consistent coordinator must refuse to answer during a partition, and "who is alive?" is a question you need answered especially during a partition.

5. What would a good answer actually have to do?

In one breath: Everybody learns everything, nobody is special, each machine's bill stays the same as the cluster grows, and none of it depends on the network behaving.

Five requirements. Each one is a person on the field. The fifth is the one people forget.

  1. Every machine eventually learns every fact — memberships and settings. Priya, at the far end by the tug-of-war rope, must end up knowing everything Asha knows.
  2. No machine is special. Any machine may die at any moment without stopping the flow of information. When Dev goes home, or Ben's office closes, the news has to keep moving anyway.
  3. Each machine's cost is fixed. It must not grow as the cluster grows. Meera cannot be asked to make more calls just because more families turned up.
  4. It survives a lossy network with no acknowledgements and no retry bookkeeping. Half of what Asha says is lost in the noise, and nobody is keeping a list of what to repeat to whom.
  5. Machines discover failures themselves. The thing that would otherwise have told them is exactly the thing that might be unreachable. Nobody is going to announce that Arjun has stepped away, or that Omar cannot be heard.

Look at who requirement 5 is really about. Arjun is not gone. Dev is. Omar is right there and inaudible. From the outside, all three look identical, and no machine on the field can tell them apart.

Now look hard at the word eventually in the first requirement. It is doing enormous work, and giving it up is the price of everything else on the list.

Asha's crowd is not going to know things at the same instant. It is going to know them shortly.

6. What if everybody just told two people?

In one breath: Once a second, every machine picks two others at random and tells them everything it knows. Anyone who hears something new starts telling it too. So the number who know multiplies instead of creeping up by one.

Here is the key idea, and it is small enough to say out loud.

Every machine, once a second, picks two other machines at random and tells them everything it knows. That is the entire protocol.

Asha, at the tea stall, hears that the buses have moved to Gate 7. She does not look for a microphone. She does not fetch a list. She turns to the two people nearest her, one of whom is Meera, and says it.

A minute later Meera turns to two more. So does the other one. So does Asha. Nobody keeps a record of who has been told. Some people are told six times.

Why does multiplying beat adding?

Because a person who has just heard the news starts telling it too. So the group that knows does not grow by two each minute. It multiplies.

What is the second half of the idea?

There is one more piece, and it arrives properly in section 10 once you have felt the need for it.

When two machines exchange what they know, the way they combine their two versions must not depend on who spoke first.

Hold on to that sentence. It is the difference between a protocol and a game of telephone.

7. What do engineers call the things you have just watched?

In one breath: Ten words, and you have already felt the distinction behind every one of them. Here is the terminology, each word attached to the moment at the fête where you met the thing it names.

The full one-line definitions live in the glossary at the end. What follows is the map back to the story.

What is this family of protocols called, and what are its two dials?

What Asha's crowd is doing has a name. A gossip protocol, also called an epidemic protocol, is a family of protocols in which each node periodically exchanges state with randomly chosen peers. Information therefore spreads probabilistically, rather than along a planned route.

The word exists to separate this from two neighbours. Broadcast is a planned delivery to everyone. Consensus is an agreement reached before anyone proceeds. Gossip promises neither delivery nor agreement. It promises that if you wait, everyone ends up knowing. Cassandra nodes gossip once per second with up to three peers, which is this exact idea in production.

You already met one line about "periodic sync/gossip" in a table in distributed-rate-limiting. This lab is that line, opened up.

Two dials control the spread, and section 6 introduced both.

At the fête The word Where you met it
one minute, when everyone makes their calls round, also called the gossip interval section 6, "each second"
how many people you turn to fanout section 6, the two people nearest Asha
the moment everybody on the field has the same answer convergence section 6, minute 6

A round is the period between one node's gossip exchanges. In this lab every node ticks together, which makes the traces legible. In production each node has its own timer and nothing is synchronised. The word matters because it is the unit in which the time to reach everybody is stated. "Five rounds" is a portable answer. "Five seconds" depends entirely on how you set the timer.

Fanout is the one dial that trades bandwidth for speed, and its effect is lopsided: cost is linear in fanout, benefit is only logarithmic. Fanout 3 in a 500-node cluster reaches everyone in about 5 rounds. Fanout 1 takes about 8.3 rounds (experiment 3).

Convergence is the state in which every node's copy of the shared state is identical. Gossip guarantees it eventually and with high probability, never at any fixed time. The word exists to name the goal precisely enough to put a number on it, and to force the honest qualifier. Convergence is a destination, not a promise about when you arrive. It is the eventual consistency of cap-theorem and the consistency window of cqrs, applied to the membership list rather than to your data.

Which two numbers does every machine carry about itself?

Arjun goes under the mango tree to take a call. He is not gone. He is just not saying anything. Two small numbers exist entirely because of people like Arjun.

At the fête The word Where you meet it
tapping the table every minute so the people near you know you are still there heartbeat counter section 10, version 3
coming back and saying "I am here, and that was before — ignore it" incarnation number section 10, version 5

A heartbeat counter is a per-node number that only ever increases. It is gossiped with the node's entry. Peers keep the highest value they have seen, and time how long since it last moved.

Why a counter and not a clock? Because a counter answers "is this fresher than what I have?" without comparing clocks. Two machines' clocks may differ by seconds. A number that only goes up needs no agreement about time at all. So n5: heartbeat 412 merged with n5: heartbeat 419 keeps 419, on every machine, in any order.

An incarnation number is a per-node counter that only that node may increase. It exists because bad news spreads and cannot be un-spread. Once the crowd believes Arjun has gone home, no third party can talk it out of that belief. You need one authority whose word outranks the rumour, and the only sensible authority about Arjun is Arjun.

So Arjun is suspected at incarnation 1. He comes back, sets incarnation 2, and says he is fine. Everyone prefers the higher incarnation, so his correction overtakes the rumour wherever it goes. That is the same shape as the fencing token in redis and the expected-version check in event-sourcing: a number that decides whose claim is current.

How does a crowd decide that somebody has gone home?

Dev leaves the fête without telling anybody. Nobody announces it. All anyone has is an absence.

At the fête The word Where you meet it
the rule you use to decide Dev has gone failure detector section 10, versions 3 and 4
saying "I think Dev has gone" out loud, so he can contradict you suspicion section 10, version 5

A failure detector is the component that turns silence into a verdict. Typically: no evidence of life for some time, then suspect, then dead. The word exists to insist that this is a separate, fallible component, not a property of the network. It can be wrong in two directions, and the two errors trade against each other.

That is the timeout-versus-retry judgement from networking-for-developers, arriving again. You cannot tell a dropped packet from a slow server. You pick a number and you accept both kinds of error.

Suspicion is an intermediate state between alive and dead, gossiped like any other fact, which gives the accused a chance to answer before being written off. It exists because going straight from alive to dead on a timeout makes a garbage collection pause indistinguishable from a crash, and evicts healthy machines. Suspicion turns an instant verdict into an appealable one.

What are the two ways to talk, and the two layers of the system?

Priya is at the far end of the field. Nobody has walked over. She could wait, or she could ask.

At the fête The word Where you meet it
I tell you; I ask you; we swap push, pull, push-pull section 10, version 6
walking the whole field at the end, comparing notes properly anti-entropy section 10, version 7

Push sends your state to a peer. Pull requests a peer's state. Push-pull does both in one exchange. The words exist because the three styles have opposite weaknesses, and the difference only shows up at the end of a spread. Push is quick to start and slow to finish. Pull is the reverse. Experiment 4 puts numbers on both.

Anti-entropy is a background reconciliation: two machines compare everything they hold, in full or as a digest, and fix whatever differs. Its opposite number is rumour mongering, which spreads individual new facts eagerly and then stops.

The pair of names exists to label a safety net. Rumour spreading is fast and can miss nodes. Anti-entropy is slow and misses nobody. Experiment 5 shows what happens without the net: an eager stop rule left 9 nodes in 1,000 without the news, for ever. Real systems run both layers, and the names tell you which one you are talking about.

8. Where does the crowd picture stop being true?

In one breath: The crowd is a good picture of how news spreads and a bad picture of what machines are like. Real people garble rumours, remember who they told, and never declare each other dead. Machines do the opposite of all three.

The simple model: a rumour in a crowded room

Two hundred people at a party. One of them learns that the band has cancelled. Every minute, each person who knows tells whoever is nearest. Nobody organises it. Nobody keeps a list of who has been told. Some people are told six times.

Within a few minutes the whole room knows. And if a few people had left the party halfway through, everyone else would still have found out.

The engineering model: one row per machine, and one rule

Each node holds one row per node, and a rule for combining rows.

stateDiagram-v2

    ALIVE --> SUSPECT : no evidence of life for `suspectAfter` rounds
    SUSPECT --> ALIVE : the node itself refutes, with a higher incarnation
    SUSPECT --> DEAD : still nothing after `deadAfter` more rounds
    DEAD --> [*] : no longer gossiped to
    note right of SUSPECT
      A verdict that can be appealed.
      Only the accused node can appeal it.

Where the story lies to you — read this before you trust it

Four lies. Each is a place where a real cluster is not like a party.

Rumours mutate; gossip state must not. The party version gets garbled. Ours is defined by a merge rule that is deterministic, commutative and idempotent. Without those three properties, two nodes given the same facts in different orders end up in different states permanently. That is the difference between a protocol and a game of telephone, and it is why section 10 spends so long on one comparison.

People know who they told; nodes do not. A node keeps no record of who it has informed. That record is exactly the bookkeeping that grows with the cluster, and avoiding it is the whole point. The node just keeps saying it.

A person can ask "are you okay?" and get an answer. A node cannot. Silence is all it ever observes, and silence has three causes it cannot tell apart: crashed, busy, unreachable.

Party guests do not declare each other dead. Ours do, and it is the most dangerous thing they do. Experiment 10 shows a cluster of 100 perfectly healthy machines declaring every one of its members dead.

INTERMEDIATE MODEL

10. How would you actually build this, and where would you go wrong?

In one breath: Eight versions. Each one is what a careful engineer writes next, and each breaks in a way that forces the following one. Every odd-looking detail in the finished protocol is the scar of one of these breaks.

This is the actual mechanism, built the way it actually gets built.

Throughout, keep five machines in your head, n1 to n5, and one shared fact. The fact is the identity of the current write leader — the machine that accepts writes right now.

At the fête, the same fact is which gate the buses leave from. Asha (machine n1) is at the tea stall. Meera, Priya, Arjun (machine n4) and Omar (machine n5) are spread across the field. When you read "the leader is n7", hear "the buses go from Gate 7".

v1 — what if you just say what you know?

Each node picks a random peer each second and sends its copy of the settings. The receiver overwrites its copy with whatever arrived.

🔮 Predict: Asha learns leader=n7 at 09:00:01 — the buses go from Gate 7. Priya learns leader=n9 at 09:00:04, a genuinely newer decision. Both start telling people. Where does the field end up?

Reveal

Nowhere in particular, and different people end up in different places permanently.

Someone who hears Asha last believes Gate 7. Someone who hears Priya last believes Gate 9. There is no "last" in a network, only "last as observed by me". So the crowd settles into two camps and stays there.

If you guessed "the newer one wins because it is newer", ask who is holding the clock. Nobody is. Nothing in v1 carries the information that Gate 9 came later.

Nothing detects this. Every node is behaving correctly by its own rule.

v2 — what if every fact carries a version?

Every fact carries a version number, and on receipt you keep the higher version. Asha's announcement is the fourth of the day. Priya's is the fifth.

The two versions Written as What the merge keeps
the fourth announcement, Gate 7 leader=n7 (v4) —
the fifth announcement, Gate 9 leader=n9 (v5) leader=n9 (v5)

That result holds on every node, in any order, however many times each message arrives. Three properties now hold, and they are the ones that matter for the rest of this lab.

Property In words Why gossip needs it
commutative combining a and b gives the same answer as combining b and a order does not exist in a network, so it must not matter
idempotent the same message twice is the same as once duplicates are free, so nothing has to be de-duplicated
monotonic the result is never older than either input information never goes backwards

Together they make "highest version wins" a join: the merge always moves the node's state upward in an ordering both sides agree on.

That is the whole correctness argument for the dissemination half of this protocol. It is why gossip needs no ordering, no delivery guarantees and no de-duplication.

v3 — how do you find out who is alive?

The settings were the easy half. Now: who is alive?

Each node increases its own heartbeat counter every round and gossips it. Everyone keeps the highest value they have seen for each peer, plus the local time at which it last increased.

Why a counter rather than a timestamp? A timestamp requires two machines to agree about the time, and they do not. A counter compared with max needs no agreement at all. It is the same trick as v2's version numbers, applied to liveness.

Detection is then local arithmetic. If a peer's heartbeat has not moved for suspectAfter rounds, something is wrong with that peer. At the fête, suspectAfter is how many minutes of silence you give somebody before you start telling people they have gone home.

So we do not go straight from alive to dead. We add SUSPECT: a public statement that a node looks gone, gossiped like any other fact, which the accused gets a chance to answer.

v5 — why can the accused not simply say "I am here"?

Arjun comes back. Every other node is saying SUSPECT about him. He says "I am alive" — and his message is just one more opinion about Arjun, merged against nineteen opinions saying otherwise.

It is worse than a stalemate. With a naive "worse status wins" rule, the SUSPECT rumour keeps circulating and re-infecting every node Arjun corrects. Arjun is dead for ever, while standing right there.

The fix is to give exactly one node the last word about each node. That is the incarnation number, which only that node may raise. Arjun sets incarnation 2 and says ALIVE. The merge rule prefers the higher incarnation over any status, so the refutation beats the rumour everywhere it goes, including at nodes the rumour reaches first.

The complete rule is a comparison of three fields in a fixed order.

// PeerState.mergeFrom -- keep whichever state is LARGER, comparing
// (incarnation, then status, then heartbeat), exactly like version 2.7.1.
int cmp = Long.compare(in.incarnation, this.incarnation);
if (cmp == 0) cmp = Integer.compare(in.status.ordinal(), this.status.ordinal());  // ALIVE<SUSPECT<DEAD
if (cmp == 0) cmp = Long.compare(in.heartbeat, this.heartbeat);
if (cmp <= 0) return false;      // mine is already at least as new

Two things about that snippet are worth more than they look.

Why does bad news win at the same incarnation?

Within one incarnation, SUSPECT beats ALIVE. Otherwise a suspicion would be cancelled by any node that had not heard it yet, and a real failure would never be agreed on. Dev really has gone home, and that news has to travel.

So bad news must travel. Only the accused, carrying a fresh incarnation, may stop it.

Why are the fields compared in a fixed order, and never mixed?

Writing this lab I first implemented "take the higher heartbeat, and separately take the worse status". It reads perfectly sensibly out loud. It is wrong.

The test merging_in_either_order_gives_the_same_answer caught it immediately. Forty orderings of the same five facts produced two different final states.

Mixing the fields destroys commutativity, and a cluster whose merge is not commutative never converges. Nodes that saw the same news in different orders stay different for ever. That test is in Tests.java because of this bug, and exercise 6 plants it back for you to find.

ENGINEERING MODEL

11. What are the failure scenarios, and which of them survive?

In one breath: Eight ways this goes wrong. Seven of them the protocol handles, and the eighth takes down a cluster in which not a single process has failed.

Eight short stories first, then two tables that compare all eight at once. The first table is what you would see. The second is what it costs you.

F1 — what happens when Dev simply goes home?

Dev leaves the fête without telling anybody. Nobody is notified, because there is nobody to notify.

For suspectAfter rounds, nothing happens at all. Then peers begin suspecting him, one at a time, as each one's own timer runs out. The suspicion is itself a fact, so it spreads — which means the last few machines learn it from gossip rather than from their own timers. In the 8-node simulation, everyone believes the crashed node is dead within 13 rounds of the crash.

The protocol's answer is the three-state walk: ALIVE, then SUSPECT, then DEAD. Dead nodes are dropped from the gossip partner list.

Convergence holds — every node reaches the same verdict. What bends is the gap before it: for several rounds, some machines are still routing work to a machine that is gone.

In a real cluster that gap is the detection latency you tune. It is why a service that must fail over fast, such as a database primary, uses a shorter timeout than one that merely needs an accurate roster.

F2 — what happens when Arjun is only on the phone?

Arjun has not left. He is under the mango tree, or in a garbage collection pause, or waiting on a slow disk, or in the middle of a long fsync.

His peers observe exactly what they observed for Dev: silence. So they suspect him. With a three-round timeout and a four-round pause, 12% of the cluster wrongly suspects him. With a one-round timeout, 98% do.

No failure detector can tell a paused process from a dead one. That is not a gap in this implementation; it is the shape of the problem.

The protocol's answer is suspicion rather than death, plus refutation. Arjun raises his incarnation on return and the correction overtakes the rumour. He keeps his identity, his data and his place in the ring.

What bends is the middle: work routed to him stalls during the pause, and if the pause runs long enough he is evicted and has to rejoin.

This is why Cassandra uses a phi-accrual detector. Instead of a fixed threshold it learns each peer's normal reply distribution and emits a suspicion level. A node on a jittery link is given more rope than one that is normally instant.

F3 — what happens when a machine that was declared dead comes back?

Arjun again, but this time the verdict has already landed. Without incarnation numbers he is re-killed for ever: his "I'm alive" is one vote against many, and the old rumour keeps re-infecting the nodes he corrects.

Bad news has to be monotonic, because F1 needs it to be. So only a distinguished authority can reverse it, and the incarnation number is that authority.

Convergence holds, and so does the rule that no node ever believes itself dead. Nothing bends at all — this one is free, once the merge rule is right.

The same mechanism appears under the same name in SWIM, Serf, memberlist and Ringpop.

F4 — what happens when most of the shouting is lost?

Set message loss to 60% on the page, or run java src/Experiments.java 6. Convergence slows and nothing breaks.

The reason is that every node retries implicitly every round, with different partners. There is no retry logic anywhere in this lab, because none is needed.

Here is the slowdown at 500 nodes and fanout 3. The right-hand column is the one to read: it says how much worse than a perfect network each row is.

Everything is intact. What bends is latency, and it bends gracefully. Losing four messages in five costs you a factor of four, not a broken cluster.

This is why gossip runs over UDP, and why it is the layer that keeps working when TCP-based control planes are timing out.

F5 — can a network fault kill a cluster in which nothing has failed?

This is the honest limit of the design, and it deserves your full attention.

🔮 Predict: 100 machines. Not one of them crashes. The network drops 90% of packets and the suspicion timeout is three rounds. After 40 rounds, what fraction of all the beliefs in the cluster say DEAD?

Reveal

All of them. Every machine has written off every other machine.

| Packet loss | Processes actually running | Beliefs saying DEAD | Nodes that got the news | |---|---|---|---| | 60% | 100 of 100 | 15% | 100 of 100 | | 80% | 100 of 100 | 64% | 93 of 100 | | 90% | 100 of 100 | 100% | 10 of 100 |

If you guessed "it degrades like F4 did", you were reasoning about the spreading half, which really is that robust. This is the judging half, and it has no such property.

At 90% loss every node has written off every other node, stopped gossiping to all of them, and the cluster has gone silent. It cannot recover on its own, because recovery would mean talking to nodes it has already buried.

Silence is the detector's only symptom, and a bad network manufactures unlimited silence. Every process is running the whole time.

There is no response to this in the lab, and that is the point. Nothing useful survives, and everything bends, from a network problem that broke no process.

Real mitigations exist, and every one of them is partial. All four are about the same thing: refusing to be certain.

  • Adaptive timeouts, such as phi-accrual.
  • Lifeguard's trick of extending your own timeouts when you look unhealthy to others.
  • Never garbage-collecting a "dead" node without an explicit administrative removal.
  • Alerting on the rate of state changes, rather than on any single node's state.

F6 — what happens when the field splits in two?

The marquee comes down across the middle of the field. Asha's half cannot hear Priya's half at all.

Each half converges internally and is unreachable from the other. Each half sees the other half's nodes fall silent and starts suspecting them. From inside, a partition looks exactly like "half the cluster died". That is cap-theorem, unchanged.

Healing needs no repair step. The first ordinary message across the mended link carries higher versions, and the merge rule does the rest — 1.8 rounds to full reconvergence in a 100-node cluster.

Convergence holds once connectivity returns, and there is no split-brain in the data sense, because there is no election and nothing to win. What bends is serious. During the split each side has a wrong membership list. Anything built on top of it, such as a hash ring or a router, is making decisions from it.

This is why membership gossip is AP. Any decision that must not be made twice, such as a leader, a lock or an owner, needs a CP mechanism on top. Gossip tells you who is probably there. It never tells you who is in charge.

F7 — what happens to the person the rumour never reached?

Run experiment 5 with an eager stop rule. Nine nodes in 1,000 never learn the fact, permanently, and nothing in the system notices.

The stop rule is local. "I have heard this back twice" cannot know about a node that nobody happened to pick.

The answer is the anti-entropy layer underneath: a full-state exchange that eventually compares everything with somebody. Eventual convergence holds because of that second layer. With rumour mongering alone the fact would be lost entirely.

Cassandra's gossip carries full digests for exactly this reason, and the read-repair and hinted-handoff machinery is the same anti-entropy idea applied to data rather than to membership.

F8 — what happens when the message itself grows with the cluster?

This one is not run in the lab. It is stated, because the arithmetic is plain.

Each message carries one row per known node. So a 10,000-node cluster is carrying a 10,000-row table in every message, several times a second. The payload is the whole membership table.

Real systems answer it three ways. They send a digest, which is ids and versions only, and let the peer ask for just the rows it lacks. They cap the number of updates piggybacked per message, preferring the freshest and least-disseminated ones. And they keep packets under the network's MTU: see networking-for-developers, where a message above the path MTU is a black hole waiting to happen.

Cluster.java has a maxEntriesPerMessage knob that implements the cap, so exercise 5 can put a number on what bounding it costs. What bends is convergence speed, because each message now carries less news.

12. What is actually guaranteed, and what did the guarantee cost?

In one breath: Gossip guarantees that everyone ends up agreeing, and guarantees nothing whatsoever about when. Almost every hard question in this lab is a question about when.

Which invariants hold, and what enforces each one?

Seven promises, each with a named piece of code behind it and a test that fails if the code stops keeping it.

# Invariant Enforced by Test
I1 Merging is order-independent the fixed-order comparison in PeerState.mergeFrom merging_in_either_order_gives_the_same_answer
I2 Merging the same message twice changes nothing the same comparison returning false on a tie merging_the_same_news_twice_changes_nothing
I3 Information never moves backwards the merge only ever replaces a smaller tuple with a larger one a_higher_incarnation_always_wins
I4 A suspicion may only be overturned by its subject incarnation is raised only in the "somebody is talking about me" branch of Node.merge a_refuted_node_is_not_re_suspected_by_stale_gossip
I5 Within one incarnation, bad news wins status.ordinal() comparison, ALIVE < SUSPECT < DEAD bad_news_beats_good_news_at_the_same_incarnation
I6 No running node believes itself dead a node always rewrites its own row each round no_node_ever_believes_itself_dead
I7 Dead nodes stop receiving traffic Node.randomTargets skips DEAD peers nobody_keeps_gossiping_to_a_node_declared_dead

Invariant I4 is Arjun's protection. Invariant I5 is what makes Dev's departure stick. Invariant I7 is why nobody keeps walking over to where Dev was standing.

What is guaranteed?

Eventual convergence, with high probability. If the network delivers some messages, and the membership stops changing, every node reaches the same state. This is a probabilistic statement, not a deadline. The probability that a given node has not heard after r rounds falls exponentially in r, and it is never exactly zero.

Order independence. Nodes may receive the same facts in any order, twice, or not at all, and still agree.

Constant cost per node. The bill is fanout × message size, and it does not depend on how big the cluster is.

Self-healing. No repair step exists because none is needed. After a partition, a crash or a storm of loss, the ordinary mechanism is the recovery mechanism.

What is explicitly not guaranteed?

No agreement at any instant. There is no moment at which the cluster "has" a membership list. Experiment 9 shows a single crash leaving the cluster with a peak of 45% of nodes disagreeing, for about 10 rounds.

No delivery guarantee for any individual message, and no way to know whether one arrived.

No total order. Two facts published concurrently at different nodes have no defined relative order. The version numbers only order updates to the same fact. Genuinely concurrent updates need version vectors or a CRDT, which is beyond this lab.

No accuracy from the failure detector. It is wrong in both directions, by design, and F5 shows it can be catastrophically wrong.

Nothing about leadership. Gossip cannot elect anything. It reports beliefs; it does not create facts.

No security. Any node that can talk to the cluster can inject any belief, including "everyone is dead".

Why is the safety argument so short, and the liveness argument so long?

The unusual thing about gossip is that its safety property is trivial. Nothing bad can happen from merging states, because the merge is a join over a partial order, and every node only ever moves upward in it. There is no equivalent of two Raft leaders or a lost committed write.

So almost everything interesting is a liveness question: when does everyone know?

And the one true safety-flavoured failure is F5, the cluster declaring itself dead. That one does not come from the gossip at all. It comes from the judgement layered on top of it.

That division is worth carrying away. Spreading is robust. Deciding is fragile.

In one breath: Eight small files, no build tool, no dependencies. Four short passages do nearly all the work, and each one is a thing you have already watched happen on the field.

The four lines that decide every disagreement

src/PeerState.java — the merge rule. The whole protocol's correctness lives here.

int cmp = Long.compare(in.incarnation, this.incarnation);
if (cmp == 0) cmp = Integer.compare(in.status.ordinal(), this.status.ordinal());

Fixed order, no mixing. Commutative, idempotent and monotonic — the three properties that make ordering, de-duplication and acknowledgement unnecessary.

The one place a node writes a number about itself

src/Node.java — refutation. This is Arjun coming back from the mango tree, in code. It is the only number a node writes about itself that every other node must accept.

if (in.status != Status.ALIVE && in.incarnation >= incarnation) {
    incarnation = in.incarnation + 1;     // only I may raise my own incarnation
    ...
}

long silence = round - p.lastChangedRound;

Which real systems should you go and read?

Cassandra gossips once per second with up to three peers. Its per-node state is (generation, version), where generation is the boot count and therefore plays the role of the incarnation number. Failure detection is phi-accrual. Every idea in this lab appears there, under these names.

Consul, Serf and HashiCorp memberlist are a direct SWIM implementation, plus Lifeguard. Lifeguard's core insight is that a node that is itself overloaded should be more forgiving of others, because its own observations are suspect. That is the honest answer to F5 that this lab does not implement.

Redis Cluster runs its cluster bus as gossip. A node's private suspicion is called PFAIL; it is promoted to a cluster-wide FAIL verdict only once a majority of primaries agree. Note the difference from this lab: Redis requires a quorum before a verdict becomes binding, precisely to avoid one node burying a healthy peer. Worth pairing with redis.

Ringpop, from Uber, is SWIM plus consistent hashing — exactly the pairing the uber-system-design lab needed to answer "which node owns which driver".

Amazon Dynamo, the 2007 paper, is the origin of gossiped membership plus consistent hashing in one design, and still the clearest short read.

Which trade-offs will you actually be asked about?

  1. Fanout — cost is linear, benefit is logarithmic. Real systems sit near 3.
  2. Gossip interval — detection latency against constant background traffic.
  3. Suspicion timeout — false positives against slow failover. There is no good setting; adaptive detectors choose per peer, continuously.
  4. Rumour mongering against anti-entropy — speed and cheapness against completeness. Run both.
  5. Hearsay against direct evidence — a cheap, forgiving view of liveness against an accurate view of reachability that needs second opinions to be safe.
  6. Gossip against a coordinator — no single point of failure and constant per-node cost, bought with permanent uncertainty about the present moment. That is the consistent-hashing lab's "coordination for control" trade, seen from the other side.

What is this commonly confused with?

Six pairs, and the one sentence that separates each.

Gossip against broadcast. Broadcast plans a delivery to everyone; gossip makes no plan and relies on repetition. That is why broadcast fails completely when a link fails, and gossip merely slows down.

Gossip against consensus (Raft, Paxos). Consensus decides one value and can tell you it is decided. Gossip spreads values somebody else decided, and can never tell you it is finished. Use consensus to pick a leader; use gossip to tell everyone who it is.

Gossip against a heartbeat to a monitor. A monitor is one observer with one opinion; gossip has many observers whose opinions merge. That is why gossip degrades into "half the cluster disagrees" rather than "the monitor is down and nobody knows anything".

Anti-entropy against rumour mongering. Same protocol family, opposite jobs. Rumours are fast and can miss. Anti-entropy is slow and misses nobody.

Failure detector against failure. The detector is a component with its own error modes. Experiment 10 is entirely a detector failure with zero process failures — a distinction that matters enormously at 3am.

Eventually consistent against eventually convergent. Convergence is about replicas of the same state agreeing. Consistency is about what a reader is promised. Gossip gives you the first; what a client sees is a separate design question.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Because that machine becomes the thing everything else depends on. Every one of the 500 has to call it to report in and to find out about the others, so it carries load that grows with the cluster. If it stops, nobody learns anything new. And worst of all: if the network splits, the machines on the far side cannot reach it, so they are left with no idea who is alive — at exactly the moment when knowing that matters most. To make it reliable you end up running several copies that must agree with each other, which is a harder problem than the one you started with.

1.2 Because every machine that hears the news starts telling it too, so the number who know is multiplied each round rather than increased by one: 1, 3, 9, 27. Reaching 500 by multiplication takes about five steps; reaching it by addition takes 500.

1.3 Gossip can spread the news of who the leader is, but it cannot pick one. It has no notion of agreement at an instant — two halves of a partition will each happily gossip a different leader, and both are internally consistent. Electing requires a mechanism that can refuse to proceed without a majority (paxos, Raft). The usual architecture is both: consensus decides, gossip distributes.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 From a fresh page with fanout 2: 1 → 3 → 7 → 15-ish, then all 20. The ratio is roughly 3 (each knower tells 2 more and keeps knowing), until it runs out of uninformed nodes to reach.

2.2 The inspector shows whether it is running, whether it has the news, its heartbeat and incarnation, and how many peers it believes alive / suspect / dead. While a machine is selected the ring shows its opinion — the page says so under the table, and it is the fastest way to see that different machines hold different views at the same moment.

2.3 A mixed column means the nodes disagree about that machine — some say alive, some suspect, some dead. The nodes disagreeing stat box puts a percentage on it, and it is the same quantity the consistent-hashing lab called the membership propagation window.

2.4 The merge:

int cmp = Long.compare(in.incarnation, this.incarnation);
if (cmp == 0) cmp = Integer.compare(in.status.ordinal(), this.status.ordinal());
if (cmp == 0) cmp = Long.compare(in.heartbeat, this.heartbeat);
if (cmp <= 0) return false;

The refutation, in Node.merge: incarnation = in.incarnation + 1; inside the branch that handles somebody gossiping about me.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 6 rounds. 10 nodes take 2. The rule is log(n)/log(fanout+1) = log₄(5000) ≈ 6.1. Five hundred times the cluster, three times the rounds.

3.2 Nine of 1,000 never learn it, and they never will. A stopped rumour cannot restart: no node knows anybody is missing it. That is exactly why an anti-entropy layer runs underneath the rumour layer. The stop rule is still worth having — it used 4,696 messages against 29,933.

4.1 Measured, push-pull:

4.2 With suspectAfter = 1, scene 4's paused node is suspected almost immediately and by nearly everyone (experiment 7: 98% of the cluster). A cluster whose nodes pause for 200 ms every few seconds would flap constantly: nodes endlessly suspected, endlessly refuting, membership changing many times a minute. Every consumer of that list — a hash ring, a router — then reshuffles on each change, which is far more damaging than the pauses were. Liveness lost, and avoidably.

4.3 Measured, 100 nodes, one crash, rounds until every node agrees again:

   maxEntriesPerMessage = 0 (unlimited) : 10.0 rounds
   maxEntriesPerMessage = 3             : did not reconverge within 200 rounds

A 3-row cap in a 100-node cluster starves the news: each message carries almost nothing, so a change cannot outrun the noise. The real constraint that makes a cap non-negotiable is packet size — a UDP datagram above the path MTU is fragmented or silently dropped (see networking-for-developers), so real systems cap the payload and compensate by sending digests and letting peers ask for the rows they lack, which is exercise 5.1.

4.4 Removing SUSPECT (straight ALIVE → DEAD) fails a_node_that_was_only_slow_refutes_its_own_death_and_stays_alive — measured, 11 passed, 1 failed. What the cluster lost is the appeal window: there is no longer a public statement of doubt that the accused can answer before the verdict is final.

Whether the node ever recovers now depends on something that ought to be unrelated — the exchange mode. Under push-pull it still recovers, because it keeps initiating exchanges and the replies carry the verdict back to it, which triggers refutation. Under push-only it is buried permanently: nobody gossips to a dead node, so it never hears the verdict and never refutes (verified in 7.2 below). A protocol whose recovery depends on that accident is a protocol with a missing state.

Rung 5 — Implement

5.1 — digest exchange. The shape: send {id: (incarnation, heartbeat)}, have the receiver diff it against its own table and reply with (a) the ids where it is behind, requesting full rows, and (b) full rows where it is ahead. Two observations worth having before you start:

  • The digest is O(n) too — just with a much smaller constant (two numbers per node instead of a full row). To get a genuinely bounded message you must also cap it and rotate which ids you include, which is what real implementations do.
  • Convergence should cost roughly one extra round-trip per exchange, not more rounds of spread: the same information moves, in two messages instead of one.

5.2 — adaptive timeout. Track a per-peer mean gap and suspect at ~4× it. You should find rows that look better in both columns of experiment 7, because a node with a fast reliable link gets a tight threshold while a jittery one gets a loose one — that is precisely the argument for phi-accrual.

The new failure you have introduced: a peer with no history (just booted, or just became reachable) has no mean, so whatever default you pick is applied to exactly the nodes you know least about. Real detectors handle this with a conservative default and a warm-up period, and it is worth noticing that the warm-up window is itself an attack surface for flapping.

Rung 6 — Debug

6.1 Three tests fail:

FAIL  merging_in_either_order_gives_the_same_answer   2 distinct results from 40 orderings
FAIL  bad_news_beats_good_news_at_the_same_incarnation
FAIL  a_refuted_node_is_not_re_suspected_by_stale_gossip

The single wrong idea: "whoever has the newer heartbeat has the newer information" — deciding on the heartbeat first instead of on the incarnation and status.

6.2 Said out loud it sounds obviously right: a bigger heartbeat is more recent, so prefer it. It is wrong because heartbeat is only meaningful within one incarnation and one status. A stale message can carry a large heartbeat with an old status, and under this rule that stale status wins — so a node that refuted its death gets re-buried by an old rumour still circulating behind it. And because the ordering of the three fields is now inconsistent, the merge stops being commutative: 40 orderings of the same five facts produce two different final states, so two nodes given the same news in different orders never agree again.

6.3 It violates I1 (order independence) and I4 (only the subject may overturn a suspicion). The user-visible symptom: nodes that pause — a long garbage collection, a slow disk — get marked dead, refute, and get marked dead again by the rumour that is still going round. The membership list flaps for those nodes indefinitely, and everything downstream (a hash ring, a router, a shard map) reshuffles on every flip.

6.4 Two merges and one assertion:

PeerState p = new PeerState(2, 11, Status.ALIVE, 0);        // a fresh refutation
p.mergeFrom(new PeerState(1, 99, Status.SUSPECT, 0), 1);    // an old rumour, big heartbeat

Note the shape: it asserts that something did not change. A test that only checks the happy merge direction passes against the bug.

Rung 7 — Handle a failure

7.1 — the detector meltdown. Three mitigations, and the one thing that separates them:

  • A cap on simultaneous suspicion ("never suspect more than X% of the cluster at once"). Purely local, trivial to implement, and blunt: it delays the meltdown rather than preventing it, and picks an arbitrary victim set.
  • A quorum before a verdict binds. This is Redis Cluster's PFAIL → FAIL: one node's private suspicion is only promoted to a cluster-wide verdict when a majority of primaries agree. Strong, and it costs you a majority — during the very partition where you cannot get one, no node can be marked dead at all. That is a deliberate CP-flavoured choice inside an AP protocol.
  • Self-doubt (Lifeguard). A node that is failing to hear from many peers concludes that the problem is probably itself, and lengthens its own timeouts and lowers its confidence. This is the one that directly addresses F5, because in a meltdown every node is in exactly that situation.

The distinguishing question the exercise asks: which can a node decide with purely local information? The cap and self-doubt can — a node knows how many of its own peers have gone quiet. The quorum cannot: it needs to talk to a majority, which is precisely what a bad network denies. That is why the local, humble mechanism is the one that saves you in the worst case.

7.2 — a rebuilt node with a reset incarnation. Measured on a 20-node cluster where n7 was buried by all 19 peers, then rebuilt with incarnation 1:

  push-pull : every peer says ALIVE again; n7's incarnation is 2
  push-only : 0 peers say ALIVE; n7 stays buried, incarnation still 1

The step-by-step: nobody gossips to a dead node, so n7 hears nothing. Under push-pull, n7 still initiates exchanges; the peer ignores its stale ALIVE(inc 1) (the peer holds DEAD(inc 9), which is larger) but its reply carries that DEAD(inc 9) back to n7, whose refutation branch fires and jumps to incarnation 10. Under push-only there is no reply, so the verdict never reaches n7 and its protests never win. A rebuilt node is invisible for ever.

The fix is to make the incarnation strictly increasing across restarts, which means it cannot live only in memory. Options and their real costs: a boot timestamp (Cassandra's generation — free, but it trusts the clock, and a machine whose clock jumps backwards after a rebuild is silently unrecoverable); a counter persisted to disk before joining (correct, but now joining requires a durable write and a rebuilt-from-blank machine has lost it); or a fresh unique id per process (correct and free, but the node is now a different member, so anything keyed by node identity — a hash ring position, stored shards — must be rebuilt, which is the very churn you were avoiding).

7.3 — a malicious or buggy node. One node gossiping everyone: DEAD, incarnation 999 is catastrophic in this design: the merge rule is built to prefer higher incarnations, so the lie beats every truthful record, and gossip's own efficiency spreads it to the whole cluster in log(n) rounds. Every real node would refute itself on hearing its own death — but only for its own row, and only if it hears; meanwhile every node's belief about every other node is now DEAD, and dead nodes are not gossiped to.

What limits it: the self-refutation path, and (in Redis Cluster's design) a quorum requirement before a verdict binds. What amplifies it: the merge rule's preference for higher incarnations, the fanout, and the fact that no node questions the provenance of what it is told.

This is a Byzantine fault — a node that lies rather than simply stops. Nothing in this lab, or in Cassandra or SWIM, defends against it; they assume crash-stop failures. Surviving it requires signed statements about a node's own state (so nobody can forge a claim about you) plus a Byzantine-fault-tolerant protocol for anything that must be agreed. In practice, production systems buy this far more cheaply with authentication at the edge: you make sure only your own nodes can join the gossip at all.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 — three datacentres. Keep the protocol, change the topology.

  • Within a DC: normal gossip, fanout ~3, sub-second interval, tight-ish timeouts. Local links are fast and reliable, so detection can be quick.
  • Across DCs: far fewer messages. Elect (by a deterministic rule, e.g. lowest id per DC per round) a small number of nodes that gossip cross-DC, so the WAN carries O(DCs) streams rather than O(nodes). Cassandra does exactly this.
  • Timeouts must be per-link-class, not global. An 80 ms RTT with jitter needs a much looser threshold than a local link; a single global suspectAfter will either flap the remote DCs or fail to detect local crashes. This is the same reasoning as phi-accrual, applied by topology.
  • A DC cut off entirely must NOT result in the other two marking 100 nodes dead and reshuffling everything. Guard it with the "never suspect more than X% at once" rule from 7.1, and make cross-DC failover an operator decision rather than an automatic one. The important sentence: from inside, a partition and a dead datacentre look identical, and the cost of being wrong is very different in the two cases.
  • Survives from this lab: the merge rule, incarnations, suspicion, anti-entropy. Must change: flat uniform peer selection, one global timeout, and the assumption that all links are equal.

8.2 — 10,000 nodes. In order of what breaks first:

  1. Message size (F8). 10,000 rows in every message, several times a second. Symptom: gossip traffic dominates the network, then packets exceed the MTU and are fragmented or dropped. Fix: digests plus a capped, rotating set of piggybacked updates.
  2. Membership churn. With 10,000 nodes, something is always joining, dying or pausing, so the list never stops changing. Symptom: the disagreement window never closes. Fix: make consumers of the list tolerant of churn (a hash ring already is) and damp the flapping with adaptive timeouts.
  3. Convergence time. log₄(10,000) ≈ 6.6 rounds — the good news, and the reason gossip is used at this scale at all.
  4. The detector. More nodes means more simultaneous pauses, so the false positive rate scales with the cluster while your tolerance for it does not. Fix: phi-accrual, suspicion, and a cap on simultaneous suspicion.

8.3 — the alert. Page on the rate of membership state changes across the cluster — say, more than N transitions per minute sustained for five minutes — not on any node's state.

  • Not "node X is dead": that is routine and self-healing, and it will page you every night.
  • Not "cluster size below N": it fires only after the damage, and it cannot tell a rolling deploy from an outage.
  • Not convergence time alone: it is the best health metric but it stays fine right up until the meltdown, then goes to infinity with nothing in between.

What the rate metric misses: a quiet wrong state — a cluster that has settled on an incorrect but stable view, such as everyone agreeing a healthy node is dead. Nothing is changing, so nothing alerts. Cover that with a separate, cheap check from outside the gossip system: can a prober reach the nodes the cluster says are dead?

8.4 — 2,000 proxies: gossip or a coordinator?

For a coordinator (the real service meshes' choice): routing config is authored centrally and must be exact, versioned and auditable; operators need to say "roll out config v37" and know what is live. Gossip cannot give you "this version is now live everywhere", which is a requirement here rather than a nicety.

For gossip: 2,000 proxies phoning home is real load and a real dependency; a control-plane outage must not stop the data plane from knowing which peers exist.

Commit: a coordinator for configuration (it is authored, versioned and must be exact), and gossip for liveness (it is observed, local, and needed exactly when the control plane is unreachable). The two questions have opposite requirements, and the standard mistake is answering both with one mechanism.

The failure that would make me regret it: the split brain between the two layers — gossip says a proxy is alive while the coordinator has not sent it the current config, so traffic is routed to a reachable proxy running stale rules. The mitigation is to make config version part of the gossiped state, so peers can see who is behind, and to have a proxy refuse traffic for rules it knows are outdated — which is the redis lab's min-replicas-to-write idea in a different costume.
