Clock Synchronisation — the one number you can never check

Tell a friend: Meera's dashboard says a message crossed a single rack in 4.3 seconds, and Arjun's packet capture says the same hop takes 5 milliseconds. Nothing is broken. The two servers, Alba and Bruno, each keep time by counting the wobbles of a small piece of quartz, and no two pieces of quartz wobble at quite the same rate. You cannot repair that by asking a better machine what the time is, because the answer arrives late and nobody can say how late. So the honest output of a clock is not an instant. It is a bracket: the time is somewhere between here and here. Half the width of that bracket is the number your whole design is quietly built on.

1. What question can a logical clock never answer?

In one breath: You already own a clock that says which event caused which. It cannot tell you what time it is, how long something took, or whether a certificate has run out. Those questions need a different kind of clock, and that kind is what this lab builds.

Omar, who answers the regulator's letters, stops at Dev's desk with one request. Show me everything the payments service did between 09:00 and 09:05 last Tuesday.

Dev has a good clock. causal-consistency gave him happens-before. hybrid-logical-clocks gave him a stamp that never puts an effect in front of its cause. He can tell Omar which of any two events came first, as long as one of them caused the other.

He cannot answer Omar's question at all. "Between counter 4100 and counter 4900" is not a window in the morning.

So why is there a second family of clocks? Because some questions about time have no causal answer, and they are the most ordinary questions in the building.

  • Did this certificate expire? Nobody sent Dev a message when it expired. Expiry is not an event at some node. It is a property of wall-clock time.
  • Has this lease run out? The lock holder did not message Priya to say it had stopped holding the lock. That is precisely the case where it cannot.
  • How long did this request take? A duration is the difference of two physical times. A Lamport counter difference of 4 could mean 4 microseconds or 4 hours. hybrid-logical-clocks found one counter value spanning 34 ticks of real time.
  • Show me everything between 09:00 and 09:05. Omar asks it. So does the billing system. So does every auditor Omar has ever met.
  • Which of these two log lines, from two machines, happened first? Ask it when there is no causal path between them at all. hybrid-logical-clocks counted this exactly: fast-clock writer, slow-clock reader, no message between them, 86 of 86 reads stale.

Logical clocks are silent on that last one by construction, not by accident.

🔮 Predict: Dev suggests stamping every log line with an HLC value as well as a wall-clock time, so Omar can use whichever is better. Does that give Omar his 09:00–09:05 window?

Reveal

No, and the reason is worth being precise about. The HLC half of that stamp carries a wall-clock reading inside it, so Omar would be reading the wall clock again, with all of this lab's problems intact. The logical half tells him about causal order and nothing else.

If you guessed yes, you were treating an HLC as two independent clocks in one field. It is not. It is a physical clock that has been taught never to go backwards relative to a message it has seen. The physical reading it carries is exactly as wrong as the machine's own clock.

Physical time is not a legacy inconvenience that logical clocks replaced. The two families answer different questions. Every real system needs both. HLC exists because it needs both at once.

Here is the split, and it is worth memorising.

Answers "did A cause B?" "was it 09:03?", "how long?", "has it expired?"
Source of truth messages actually sent a vibrating rock (§2)
Fails by saying "concurrent" when you wanted an order being wrong, silently, by an unknown amount
Fixed by more bookkeeping nothing. It can only be bounded

That last cell is this entire lab. Logical time is now put down and not picked up again. Lamport clocks, vector clocks, happens-before and HLC are already yours from causal-consistency and hybrid-logical-clocks. The boundary between the two families is the table above.

2. What is a clock, and why do two of them drift apart?

In one breath: A clock is a small rock that vibrates, plus something that counts the vibrations. No two rocks vibrate at exactly the same rate, so two healthy machines set to the same time on Monday disagree by seconds by Tuesday.

Strip away the software. Asha opens a server and looks for the clock. What she finds is a chip containing a sliver of quartz.

Put a voltage across that quartz and it vibrates at a nominal frequency. Commonly 32,768 times a second. For a CPU reference, tens of megahertz. A counter circuit counts those vibrations. The operating system makes one agreement with the counter: when it advances by 32,768, one second has passed.

That is the whole mechanism. A clock is a rock that vibrates, plus a counter, plus an agreement about how many vibrations make a second.

Now the problem. No two rocks vibrate at exactly the same rate. The cut of the crystal moves the frequency. So does the temperature of the room, the age of the part, and the supply voltage. A crystal specified at "±20 ppm" is promised to stay within 20 parts per million of nominal.

"Parts per million" sounds negligible. Put a number on it and it stops sounding that way. 20 ppm means the clock gains or loses 20 microseconds every second.

Time elapsed Error a 20 ppm crystal has accumulated Why that matters
1 minute 1.2 ms already larger than a datacenter round trip
1 hour 72 ms larger than a London–New York round trip
1 day 1.73 seconds larger than most lease durations
1 month about 52 seconds larger than most people's imagination of "drift"

Meet the two machines this lab follows all the way through.

Alba and Bruno are two servers in the same rack, in Asha's Mumbai datacenter. Alba's crystal runs fast by 20 ppm. Bruno's runs slow by 30 ppm. Both are well inside the spec printed on the part. Neither is faulty. Neither will ever be replaced.

Asha set them both to the correct time at the same instant, on Monday at midnight. Read the fourth column below: it is the gap Meera's dashboard will eventually have to explain.

🔮 Predict, before reading on: It is Tuesday. Bruno sends Alba a request. Arjun has measured the hop between them at 5 milliseconds; they are in the same rack. Bruno logs the send, Alba logs the receive. What will Meera see when she stitches the two log lines together?

Reveal

Work outward from the truth. Say Bruno really sends at true time 10:00:00.000, and Alba really receives at true time 10:00:00.005.

  • Bruno's clock is 2.59 s slow. At that instant it reads 09:59:57.410. That is what goes in his log.
  • Alba's clock is 1.73 s fast. At her instant it reads 10:00:01.735. That is what goes in hers.

Meera's tracing tool stitches them together:

09:59:57.410  bruno   sent request req-8814
10:00:01.735  alba    received request req-8814

A 5-millisecond hop is reported as 4.325 seconds. Meera's latency dashboard is now wrong by three orders of magnitude, and it is wrong consistently, so it looks like a real finding. Somebody is about to spend a week optimising that network path.

Now send it the other way. Alba to Bruno, same 5 ms hop:

10:00:01.730  alba    sent response  req-8814
09:59:57.415  bruno   received response req-8814

The message is received 4.315 seconds before it is sent. You have seen this in a real trace. Everyone has. The instinct is to blame the tracing library, or the log shipper, or a clock "glitch". Nothing glitched. Two healthy machines, two in-spec crystals, one day of uptime.

If you guessed "the timestamps will be a few milliseconds apart, in the right order", you assumed the two clocks agree because the two machines are close. Distance has nothing to do with it. Uptime does.

This is what makes clocks dangerous rather than merely imprecise. A wrong clock does not throw an exception. It answers. Its answer has the same type, the same format and the same confident appearance as a right one.

Alba and Bruno now carry the whole lab. Everything after this is either an attempt to close that 4.32-second gap, or an admission of how much of it can never be closed.

3. Offset, drift, skew, jitter — which one is your dashboard showing?

In one breath: Four words that get used as if they mean the same thing. One is how wrong the clock is. One is how fast it is getting wronger. One is the gap between two clocks. One is not about the clock at all.

Ben is building the first clock dashboard the team has ever had. He asks Asha what to plot. Asha makes him learn four words first, because conflating them is the single most common source of confused clock debugging.

Offset is how wrong the clock is right now. One number, in seconds: the clock's reading minus true time. Alba's offset after one day is +1.73 s. Offset is a position error.

Drift, also called frequency error or skew rate, is how fast the wrongness is growing. It is measured in ppm, or in seconds per day. Alba's drift is +20 ppm. Drift is a velocity error.

That distinction matters more than the other three put together, and here is why in one sentence: correcting the offset does not correct the drift. Set Alba's clock right and change nothing else. She is perfect for an instant. Then she immediately starts gaining 20 µs every second again. §9.5 is built entirely on this sentence.

Skew is how far apart two clocks are from each other. Alba minus Bruno is 4.32 s after a day. Skew says nothing about whether either one is correct. Two machines can be perfectly synchronised to each other and both be an hour wrong. Skew is usually the quantity your distributed system actually cares about; hybrid-logical-clocks counted its inversions against exactly this axis.

(A warning about the literature. Some sources, including parts of the NTP documentation, use "skew" for the frequency error, which is what Asha calls drift. Both usages are common. When Ben reads a dashboard labelled "skew", he checks the units: seconds means offset-between-clocks, ppm means frequency.)

Jitter is how much the measurement bounces around. It is not a property of the clock. It is a property of your ability to observe it. Ask Bruno the time ten times and get ten answers scattered over 3 ms, and your jitter is about 3 ms.

One more pair, borrowed from metrology, settles a lot of arguments:

  • Accuracy is closeness to UTC. It needs a link to the outside world.
  • Precision is closeness to each other. It needs only that the machines talk.

Omar's trading timestamps must match a regulator's clock, so Omar needs accuracy. Priya's database must order its own transactions, so Priya needs precision. They are bought differently and they cost differently. Spanner needs both, which is why it is expensive.

4. Why can't Alba just ask a machine that knows?

In one breath: Put one good clock in the datacenter and let everyone ask it. The answer is always stale by the time it arrives, and always in the same direction, so the error never averages away.

The fix looks trivial, and Ben proposes it in the first ten seconds. Put one machine in the datacenter with a good clock. Call it the time server. Everyone else asks it what time it is and sets their clock to the answer.

It is a genuinely reasonable first design. It is also, in outline, what NTP does. So let us find exactly where it cracks, because everything else in this lab is a repair to one specific crack.

Alba asks the time server, which today is Bruno.

Alba's clock reads 10:00:00.000  →  "what time is it?"  ────────►
                                                        (network)
                                    ◄──────  "it is 10:00:00.105"
Alba's clock reads 10:00:00.040

Alba receives the answer "10:00:00.105" and sets her clock to 10:00:00.105.

🔮 Predict: By how much is Alba's clock wrong immediately after she sets it, and in which direction?

Reveal

Wrong by roughly 20 ms, and always in the same direction: behind.

Bruno's answer was true at the moment Bruno wrote it. By the time Alba applies it, that moment is in the past, because the reply had to cross the network. Alba has set her clock to a stale value, and she has done it systematically. The error never averages out, because the reply is never early.

If you guessed "sometimes ahead, sometimes behind, averaging to zero", you were thinking of random noise. This is not noise. It is a bias with a sign. Run this in a loop and Alba does not converge on the right time. She converges on the right time minus the one-way network delay, forever.

The bug is not subtle once you see it. Neither is the fix.

5. What does the round trip give back? Cristian's algorithm, 1989

In one breath: Alba knows how long the whole exchange took on her own clock. If she assumes the two legs were equal, she can add half of it back and remove almost all of the bias.

Alba threw away something in §4 that she already had: how long the whole exchange took, measured on her own clock. She sent at 10:00:00.000 and received at 10:00:00.040. Forty milliseconds elapsed on her clock.

If the reply took some of that 40 ms to come back, Bruno's answer is stale by that much. Alba does not know how much of the 40 ms was the outbound leg and how much was the return. So she assumes it split evenly, 20 ms each way, and adds 20 ms to Bruno's answer.

Alba sets her clock to 10:00:00.105 + 20 ms = 10:00:00.125

This is Cristian's algorithm, published by Flaviu Cristian in 1989. It is the ancestor of every physical clock synchronisation protocol running today. NTP's version uses four timestamps instead of three, so that the server's own processing time can be removed.

Think of those four timestamps as four times written on a postcard as it travels. Alba writes one when she posts it. Bruno writes one when it lands on his desk, and one when he posts the reply. Alba writes the last one when the reply lands on hers. Bruno copies his two into the reply, so Alba ends up holding all four.

Postcard moment Recorded by On whose clock Engineers call it Value
posted Alba, on send Alba's T1 10:00:00.000
landed on his desk Bruno, on receive Bruno's T2 10:00:00.105
reply posted Bruno, on send Bruno's T3 10:00:00.106
reply landed Alba, on receive Alba's T4 10:00:00.040

Two quantities come out of those four numbers. Say them in words before you meet their letters.

The quantity, in words Its letter How it is worked out
the total time the two messages spent in the network, with Bruno's own pause taken out δ, said "delta", the round-trip delay (T4 − T1) − (T3 − T2)
the best single guess at how far Bruno's clock is ahead of Alba's θ, said "theta", the offset estimate ((T2 − T1) + (T3 − T4)) / 2

Those two lines are the arithmetic core of NTP (RFC 5905 §8). Here they are with our numbers in them:

round-trip delay   δ = (T4 − T1) − (T3 − T2)     "total time, minus the server's own pause"
                     = (40 − 0)  − (106 − 105)
                     = 40 − 1
                     = 39 ms

clock offset       θ = ((T2 − T1) + (T3 − T4)) / 2
                     = ((105 − 0) + (106 − 40)) / 2
                     = (105 + 66) / 2
                     = 85.5 ms

Read those two results carefully, because the rest of the lab lives inside them. The round-trip delay δ of 39 ms says: 39 milliseconds were spent in the network, both legs combined. The offset estimate θ of +85.5 ms says: Bruno's clock appears to be 85.5 ms ahead of Alba's.

Alba adds 85.5 ms to her clock. Both machines now read the same thing. It looks like the job is done.

Checkpoint. You can now say why "just ask a server" is systematically biased, and how a round-trip measurement removes most of that bias. What follows is the discovery that "most" is doing an enormous amount of work in that sentence. The remainder is not a small imperfection waiting to be engineered away. It is a permanent feature of the problem.

Say it back. Without using the words offset, delay or timestamp, explain to a friend why Alba adding 20 ms to Bruno's answer is better than Alba using Bruno's answer as it arrived.

A good answer sounds like "Bruno wrote down the time and then posted it. By the time the postcard got to Alba, the time on it was already old. Alba knows the round trip took 40 milliseconds, so she guesses the postcard spent half of that coming back, and adds that half on. She is guessing at the split, but guessing half is much closer than pretending the postcard was instant."

6. Why can no algorithm ever find the exact offset?

In one breath: Alba assumed the message took the same time each way. It does not have to. Three different worlds hand Alba the same four numbers and three different truths, so no amount of cleverness separates them.

The θ formula contains an assumption so quiet that most people never notice they made it: the message took the same time in each direction.

Divide the round trip evenly, said Cristian. But Arjun's network is under no obligation to be symmetric, and he will tell you so at length. Consider three worlds. In all three, Alba and Bruno exchange exactly the packets above, and Alba records exactly the same four numbers.

🔮 Predict, before you read the three worlds: Alba is unhappy with one measurement, so she takes a thousand. Can a thousand round trips tell her which of the two legs was the slow one?

Reveal

No. Not a thousand, not a million, not with a faster CPU or a cleverer algorithm. Each round trip gives her one equation, and every one of those equations has the same shape: outbound plus return equals the round trip. There are always two unknowns and always one equation.

If you guessed yes, you were assuming the asymmetry is noise, and that noise averages away. §12.2 is the operational version of that guess, and it costs a permanent 3 ms.

Let out and back be the true time the two legs took, with out + back = 39 ms. Let the true offset be how far Bruno's clock is genuinely ahead of Alba's.

World A — the path is symmetric. out = 19.5 ms, back = 19.5 ms.

true time   0        Alba sends.       Alba's clock: 0
true time   19.5     Bruno receives.   Bruno's clock: 105    →  true offset = 105 − 19.5 = 85.5
true time   20.5     Bruno sends.      Bruno's clock: 106
true time   40.0     Alba receives.    Alba's clock: 40      ✓

True offset 85.5 ms. Alba estimated 85.5 ms. Perfect.

World B — out is fast, back is slow. out = 4.5 ms, back = 34.5 ms. Perhaps the return path is congested. Perhaps it takes three extra hops because Arjun's predecessor left a BGP policy routing asymmetrically. This is entirely ordinary.

true time   0        Alba sends.       Alba's clock: 0
true time   4.5      Bruno receives.   Bruno's clock: 105    →  true offset = 105 − 4.5 = 100.5
true time   5.5      Bruno sends.      Bruno's clock: 106
true time   40.0     Alba receives.    Alba's clock: 40      ✓

True offset 100.5 ms. Alba estimated 85.5 ms. She is wrong by 15 ms.

World C — the mirror image. out = 34.5 ms, back = 4.5 ms.

true time   0        Alba sends.       Alba's clock: 0
true time   34.5     Bruno receives.   Bruno's clock: 105    →  true offset = 105 − 34.5 = 70.5
true time   35.5     Bruno sends.      Bruno's clock: 106
true time   40.0     Alba receives.    Alba's clock: 40      ✓

True offset 70.5 ms. Alba estimated 85.5 ms. Wrong by 15 ms the other way.

Now put what Alba observed side by side. Read the bottom row against the four above it.

What Alba wrote down World A World B World C
T1, the moment she posted 0 0 0
T2, the moment it landed on Bruno's desk 105 105 105
T3, the moment Bruno replied 106 106 106
T4, the moment the reply landed 40 40 40
True offset 85.5 100.5 70.5

The evidence is byte-for-byte identical and the truth is different.

This is the same shape of argument as flp-impossibility, where a slow process and a dead process produce identical evidence, so no observation separates them. Here a fast-out/slow-back path and a slow-out/fast-back path produce identical evidence. No observation available to Alba separates them. Not a better algorithm. Not more samples. Not a faster CPU. She has one equation and two unknowns, and the network will not hand her a second equation.

This is worth stating as a law, because it is the load-bearing fact of the whole field:

From round-trip measurements alone, the split of delay between the two directions is unobservable. So the offset between two clocks connected only by a network cannot be determined exactly. It can only be bounded.

What does "bounded" actually mean here?

Take that word slowly, because it is what this lab is really about, and it arrives in three steps.

First, as a thing you can point at. Asha draws a line on the whiteboard. She marks 66 at the left end and 105 at the right end, and puts a tick in the middle at 85.5. Underneath she writes: "Bruno is ahead of Alba by some amount on this line. I do not know which point. I know it is on the line." That whiteboard line is what Alba's measurement actually produced. The tick in the middle was never the product.

Where did the two ends come from? From the two extreme worlds. Push all 39 ms into the return leg, so out is zero, and the true offset is 105. Push all 39 ms into the outbound leg, so back is zero, and the true offset is 66. Every physically possible world lies between those two.

                 66 ms                85.5 ms                105 ms
                   ├───────────────────────┼───────────────────────┤
              (all delay              (best estimate)         (all delay
               outbound)                                      on return)

              ◄────────────── δ = 39 ms wide ──────────────►
              ◄─── ε = 19.5 ───►

Second, as a sentence in words. Say this out loud before you meet its symbol, because Spanner's entire design is this sentence made operational:

Half the width of the honest bracket is how wrong this clock might be, right now, at worst.

Third, as the symbol. Engineers write that half-width as ε, said "epsilon". Here ε, half the width of the honest bracket, is 19.5 ms, which is exactly δ/2.

The honest output of Alba's measurement is not "85.5". It is "somewhere in [66, 105], and my best guess is the middle." The bracket is the product. The midpoint is a convenience.

You have met the letter ε before, in three labs. This section is where it comes from. It is not a hardware imperfection, and it is not caution bolted on by a nervous engineer. It is half the round-trip time. It exists because the network refused to tell Alba which direction the delay was in.

One thing ε is not, and google-spanner puts it the same way: it is not a reading of the actual error. It is a bound that the clock daemon is prepared to guarantee.

Go and see it. visualization.html → Panel 1, "The Measurement". One slider, called delay split, moves delay from one leg to the other while holding the round trip constant. Watch T1, T2, T3 and T4: they never move. Watch the true offset: it sweeps the whole interval. Seeing four numbers sit frozen while the truth slides underneath them does more than the table above can.

7. If exactness is gone, what is the real requirement?

In one breath: Stop trying to deliver a correct clock. Deliver a clock plus an honest promise about how wrong it might be. Two separate projects follow, and only one of them decides whether your system is correct.

Asha set out to build synchronised clocks. §6 says she cannot have them. So she restates the goal as something she can actually deliver:

We cannot deliver a correct clock. We can deliver a clock together with an honest, never-underestimated bound on how wrong it might be.

Everything from here divides cleanly into two projects. Keep them apart in your head, because they are done by different people with different budgets.

  1. Make ε small. Make the half-width of the honest bracket smaller. This is engineering, and §9 is eight moves' worth of it, from 100 ms down to under 40 µs, a factor of 2,500. Omar signs for it. It is bought with protocols, kernel features, network hardware and, in the end, GPS antennas.

  2. Carry ε truthfully to whoever reads the clock. Hand the half-width to the caller instead of hiding it. This is API design, and it is the part almost everyone skips. §10 shows that NTP has been computing this number correctly since 1985, and that essentially every application on earth throws it away at the last step.

Project 2 is the one that decides whether Priya's system is correct. Project 1 only decides whether it is fast. google-spanner made exactly this point, and it should land harder now: ε appears nowhere in Spanner's correctness argument and everywhere in its bill. Google's atomic clocks did not make Spanner correct. Refusing to hide ε made Spanner correct. The atomic clocks made it affordable.

8. What should a time API return instead of "now"?

In one breath: A time API should hand back two numbers, not one. Every API you have ever used hands back one, which is why every caller behaves as if the clock were perfect.

Here is the whole invention, in one line:

A time API should return [earliest, latest], not now.

That is it. Everything in §9 is engineering to bring those two numbers closer together. Everything in §10 is plumbing to stop people throwing the second one away.

Why does this feel so strange? Because every clock API Dev has ever used lies to him with a straight face. System.currentTimeMillis() returns a long. A long has no room for "± 12 ms". The API is shaped so the uncertainty cannot be expressed. So the uncertainty is dropped. So every caller behaves as though it were zero.

And here is the fact that should convince you this is not academic fastidiousness. Three organisations, working separately, on different problems, arrived at exactly the same conclusion.

Who What they built What the API returns
Google (2012) TrueTime TT.now() → [earliest, latest]; ε is half the width
AWS (2023) ClockBound a confidence interval, not a timestamp
Meta (2022–25) fbclock a "Window of Uncertainty", a range of possible nanosecond timestamps

Three independent reinventions of one idea, in the three companies that run the most clock-sensitive infrastructure on the planet. When that happens it is not a coincidence and it is not a fashion. It is the problem's actual shape showing through.

Checkpoint. You can now say why exact clock synchronisation is impossible, where the number ε comes from, and why the honest interface is an interval. That is the whole conceptual content of the field.

What you cannot yet do is say what your machine's ε is right now, or why it is 100 ms in one setup and 40 µs in another. §9 builds the machine that makes the difference. §10 teaches you to read the number off a live host.

9. How do you get from 100 ms down to 40 µs? Eight refinements

In one breath: Alba's version 1 is: exchange four times, work out the round-trip delay and the offset estimate, set the clock, and remember you are uncertain by half the round trip. Everything real is that plus eight improvements, and each one exists because a specific thing broke the one before it.

Everything in production is version 1 plus eight moves. ntpd, chrony, PTP, Google's time-slave daemon: all of them.

Read this section as a story, not a feature list. Asha ships a version. Meera, Priya, Dev or Arjun breaks it with a specific scenario. Asha ships the next one. Where a fix is a real configuration knob, the actual default is named.

9.1 One sample — what if that packet was unlucky?

Alba measures once, gets a round-trip delay of 39 ms, and sets her clock.

The next time she measures, the delay is 210 ms. Her request happened to arrive at a switch behind a 40 KB burst from Ben's backup job. Her offset estimate from that sample is uncertain by ±105 ms.

She has no way to know which of the two measurements was the good one. Worse: if she had taken only the bad one, she would have applied it with exactly the same confidence.

So a single sample is a lottery ticket, and network delay is enormously variable.

9.2 Many samples — average them, or keep only the best one?

Alba takes eight samples. What should she do with them?

🔮 Predict: Eight round-trip measurements to Bruno come back with these delays: 39, 41, 118, 52, 40, 210, 44, 39.5 ms. To get the best offset estimate, does Alba (a) average all eight offsets, (b) take the median, or (c) use only the sample with the smallest delay and throw the other seven away?

Reveal

(c), and it is not close. This is NTP's clock filter, and it surprises almost everyone, because the reflex from statistics is "more samples, average, noise cancels".

The reflex is wrong here because network delay noise is one-sided. There is a hard physical floor: propagation at roughly two-thirds the speed of light, plus the time to clock the bits onto the wire. Every source of noise can only add to that floor. Queueing, scheduling, interrupt coalescing, contention: all of them add, none of them subtract. No packet ever arrives faster than the speed of light. So the delay distribution has a wall on the left and a long tail on the right.

That changes what "best" means. Recall from §6 that a sample's error bound is half its delay. So a sample's quality is entirely decided by its delay.

| Strategy | Effective round-trip delay | Resulting ε (half of it) | |---|---|---| | Mean of all eight | 72.9 ms | ±36.5 ms | | Median | 42.5 ms | ±21.3 ms | | Minimum | 39 ms | ±19.5 ms |

Averaging contaminates the one good measurement with six bad ones. The 39 ms sample spent the least time in queues. That makes it the sample whose two legs are most likely to be nearly equal, which makes it the sample whose bracket is tightest and most honest. The other seven tell Alba nothing the minimum does not already tell her better.

"Take the minimum" is unusual advice in measurement generally. It is correct advice here specifically, because of the one-sided distribution. NTP keeps a register of the last 8 samples and selects on lowest delay.

Alba now polls Bruno eight times and keeps the best. Her ε has stopped bouncing.

And then Arjun points out the hole. All eight samples came from Bruno. If Bruno's clock is wrong, whether misconfigured, unsynchronised, or serving 1970 because his own upstream died, Alba will follow him precisely and confidently into being wrong.

9.3 Several servers — how does Alba spot the one that is lying?

Ask four servers instead of one. Each gives back a bracket, exactly as in §6: a best estimate with a half-width either side. Alba's job is now to find the truth among four claims, knowing some may be false.

Server Claims the correct time is in
S1 [98, 104] ms ahead of Alba
S2 [101, 107]
S3 [99, 106]
S4 [412, 418]

🔮 Predict: Which of those four is lying, and what rule tells Alba so without her knowing the true time?

Reveal

S4, and the rule is overlap. A correct server's bracket must contain the true time. So every correct server's bracket contains the same point, which means they all overlap each other. A bracket that overlaps nobody cannot be correct.

S1, S2 and S3 share the region [101, 104]. S4 shares no point with any of them. Alba discards S4.

If you guessed "she takes the majority vote on the midpoints", you get the same answer here and the wrong answer in general. Midpoints are single numbers with no width, so voting on them throws away exactly the information that identifies the liar.

A server whose bracket overlaps nobody's is called a falseticker, and the rule Alba just used is Marzullo's algorithm. NTP calls its variant the intersection algorithm. It is the piece named in passing in the google-spanner glossary; Google's time-slave daemons run a variant of it against several masters.

It does two jobs at once.

  1. It rejects liars without Alba needing to know in advance which server is lying. That is the same trick as quorums-and-replication: trust the intersecting majority, evict the outlier. It is also the same reflex as hybrid-logical-clocks, where CockroachDB makes a node kill itself when it detects it is outside the max-offset window. Better to have no clock than a confidently wrong one.
  2. It narrows ε. The intersection [101, 104] is 3 ms wide. Every individual bracket was 6 ms wide. Combining independent honest sources halves the uncertainty here for free. This is why NTP configurations conventionally list at least four servers: three is the minimum to out-vote one falseticker, and four keeps that property when one is unreachable.

Alba has now discovered she is 102.5 ms behind. She sets her clock forward by 102.5 ms. And Dev, whose code was in the middle of timing something on Alba, walks over with a question.

9.4 Jump or glide? What happens to software when time moves sideways

Setting the clock instantly is called stepping. It makes time discontinuous, and if the correction is backwards, it makes time run backwards.

Dev's software assumes time does not do that. So does everybody's. §12.1 and §13 carry the production outages; here is the fix.

Slewing means changing the clock's rate for a while instead of its value. Rather than jumping forward 102.5 ms, Alba runs her clock 1% fast for 10.25 seconds. The clock never jumps, never repeats a value, and never goes backwards. It just runs slightly wrong on purpose, so that afterwards it can be right.

Asha checks chrony's actual defaults, and they surprise the team. Read the middle column: three of these five are not what people assume.

Directive Default What it means
(slewing) on, always "Normally chronyd will cause the system to gradually correct any time offset by slowing down or speeding up the clock as required"
makestep not configured stepping does not happen at all unless you ask for it
maxslewrate 83333.333 ppm ("one twelfth") the fastest chronyd will slew
corrtimeratio 3 prefers slower, longer corrections over fast ones
maxdistance 3 seconds the worst root distance chronyd will accept from a source — see §10.5, and brace yourself

Do the arithmetic on that slew rate, because it has an operational consequence. One twelfth means the clock can run at most 1/12 fast or slow. Correcting Tuesday morning's 4.32 s skew by slewing alone takes at least 4.32 × 12 ≈ 52 seconds. With corrtimeratio 3 in play, in practice it takes several minutes. Throughout, the machine's clock is deliberately running at the wrong rate, and any duration it measures is off by up to 8%.

That is why nearly every distribution ships a line like makestep 1.0 3. In words: step the clock if the offset exceeds 1 second, but only for the first 3 updates after startup. Step once at boot when nothing is running, then never again. That is the compromise the whole industry settled on, and you can now see both sides of it.

Alba's clock is correct at 10:00:00. At her next poll, 64 seconds later, she is 1.28 ms out again. Correcting the offset did nothing about the reason the offset appeared.

9.5 Why does correcting the clock not stop it drifting again?

This is §3's offset-versus-drift distinction cashed in, and it is the single highest-leverage refinement in the list.

Alba drifts at +20 ppm. Her poll interval is 64 seconds. Between polls she accumulates 64 s × 20 µs/s = 1.28 ms of new error, every time, forever. She is on a treadmill: correct, drift, correct, drift.

The fix is to stop correcting the clock's reading and start correcting the number of ticks the kernel counts as one second. Alba notices over several polls that she consistently gains about 20 µs/s. She tells the kernel: count as if the crystal were 20 ppm slower than it is.

On Linux that is adjtimex/ntp_adjtime with ADJ_FREQUENCY. The frequency correction is expressed in ppm with a 16-bit fractional part, so a value of 1 means 2⁻¹⁶ ppm. The daemon persists what it learned in a drift file, so after a reboot it starts with the right frequency instead of spending an hour rediscovering it.

The payoff. Read the right-hand column, and notice which term stops mattering.

Alba's strategy Error accumulated between 64 s polls ε just before the next poll
Offset correction only (20 ppm raw) 1.28 ms ~20 ms (network) + 1.28 ms
Plus frequency correction (residual ~0.1 ppm) 6.4 µs ~20 ms (network) + 6.4 µs

A 200× reduction in the drift component, bought with a few bytes of state. After this refinement the drift term stops dominating ε and the network starts dominating it. That is a change of regime. It is why every remaining refinement attacks the network rather than the crystal.

An honesty note about Spanner's 200 µs/s. google-spanner told you TrueTime assumes a worst-case drift of 200 µs/s, which is 200 ppm. That is ten times worse than the ±20 ppm part in §2, and two thousand times worse than the ~0.1 ppm residual a disciplined clock actually achieves. It is not what Google's crystals do. It is what Google is willing to promise they will never exceed, including a machine in a hot aisle with a failing oscillator. ε is built from the number you are prepared to be held to, not the number you usually see. That gap, between typical and guaranteed, is where the cost of correctness lives. It is exactly why google-spanner insisted that ε is a latency purchase and not an accuracy one.

Alba's ε is now about 20 ms, and essentially all of it is half the round-trip delay. To go further she must shrink that delay, which means asking where those milliseconds actually go.

9.6 Where exactly is T1 taken, and what happens before the wire?

Go back to T1 and T4, the two moments Alba wrote on the postcard. When exactly is T1 taken?

In versions 1 to 5 it is taken by the NTP daemon, in userspace, just before it calls send(). Between that instant and the moment the first bit hits the fibre, the packet must cross the socket layer, then the network stack, then a possible queue on the NIC. And, most importantly, whatever the CPU scheduler decides to do in the middle. If the machine is busy, the daemon may be descheduled for milliseconds right after taking T1.

That whole delay is indistinguishable from network delay. Worse, it is variable and asymmetric, because the send path and the receive path have different costs and different queueing. It goes straight into the round-trip delay, and half of it goes straight into ε.

The fix is a ladder. Each rung moves the timestamp closer to the physical wire.

Where the timestamp is taken Typical uncertainty it contributes
Userspace, before send() hundreds of µs, ms under load — scheduler-dependent
Kernel, in the network stack (SO_TIMESTAMPING, software) tens of µs
NIC hardware, as bits cross the interface tens of nanoseconds

Hardware timestamping is not a software optimisation. It requires the network card to contain a clock and stamp packets itself. AWS describes exactly this capability on Nitro: hardware packet timestamping "add[s] a 64-bit nanosecond-precision timestamp to every incoming network packet". The reason it matters is stated plainly: "because hardware packet timestamping occurs at the hardware level—before the packet reaches the kernel, socket, or application layer—you bypass any delays added by software timestamping."

Alba's endpoints are now honest to nanoseconds. Her round-trip delay is still milliseconds, because the packet still spent all that time queued inside three of Arjun's switches that nobody asked.

9.7 What if the switches stopped being a black box?

Everything so far treated the network as an opaque box. Put a packet in, get a delay out, divide by two, hope. §6 proved that hoping is the best you can do with an opaque box.

So Arjun stops using an opaque box.

PTP, the Precision Time Protocol, IEEE 1588, requires the switches themselves to take part in the measurement. There are two ways to do it.

A transparent clock measures how long the timing packet sat inside the switch, which is its residence time, and writes that number into a correction field in the packet as it leaves. The receiver subtracts it. Queueing delay stops being unknown asymmetry and becomes a measured, subtracted quantity.

A boundary clock synchronises itself first, then acts as a time server for the next hop down. One long uncertain path becomes a chain of short certain ones.

Look at what a transparent clock does to §6's argument. Alba's problem was one equation and two unknowns. The switch hands her the second equation. This is the only refinement in the list that reduces the irreducible part of ε rather than the noise around it. It is why PTP reaches sub-microsecond accuracy while NTP over the same physical cable does not. Not better maths. A network that stopped being a black box.

The price is that PTP needs the network gear to support it. You cannot deploy PTP over the public internet, or over switches that do not speak it. That is a capital and topology cost, not a config change. Omar signs for it. Meta moved its fleet from NTP to PTP in late 2022 and has published extensively on what that took.

Alba still depends on a chain of Arjun's devices between her and the reference clock, and on that chain staying healthy.

9.8 What if there were no network at all?

The final move: put the reference clock inside the machine.

Why GPS solves the asymmetry problem. Each GPS satellite carries an atomic clock. It continuously broadcasts its own time and its own orbital position. A receiver on the ground picks up several satellites and solves for four unknowns: its own x, y and z, plus the current time. The propagation delay is no longer an unknown to be halved. The receiver knows where the satellite is, so it can compute how long the signal took. §6's missing second equation comes from geometry.

The receiver then emits a PPS signal, a pulse-per-second: a physical electrical edge, once a second, on a wire, which the server latches directly. No packets. No queues. No asymmetry.

You have already met the production form of this in google-spanner. GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every Google datacenter, plus Armageddon masters backed by atomic clocks, specifically so that a GPS-correlated failure does not take down everything at once. Jamming, spoofing, a constellation-wide bug: any of those hits every GPS receiver simultaneously.

The cloud form is newer and much more accessible. AWS puts GPS-disciplined reference clocks in the Nitro System itself, and exposes a PTP Hardware Clock, or PHC, as a device on the instance. There is no network between your machine and the reference clock. The clock is on the card your machine is plugged into. AWS's published figures:

Path Typical clock error bound
Amazon Time Sync over NTP under 100 µs
Amazon Time Sync via PHC under 40 µs

That second row is worth sitting with. Sub-100-microsecond synchronisation is now a checkbox on a cloud instance. In 2012, the fact that Google could achieve about 4 ms was the reason nobody else shipped Spanner's design. The moat drained.

What did each of the eight moves actually buy?

Read the last two columns together. The third column says what the move attacks; the fourth says how much it moved the number.

10. Your machine already computes ε — so why can't your code see it?

In one breath: The number is already there. Every NTP daemon works it out, prints it on request, and hands it to nobody, because the function your code calls has nowhere to put it.

This is the most useful section in the lab. Its claim is simple and slightly shocking:

Every NTP daemon has been computing ε correctly since 1985, prints it on request, and essentially every application on earth discards it at the last step, because clock_gettime() has nowhere to put it.

TrueTime is not a new measurement. It is the decision to stop throwing the existing one away.

10.1 What does RFC 5905 already call the bound?

NTPv4's specification defines a quantity it calls the root synchronisation distance. The RFC describes it as "the maximum error due to all causes of the local clock relative to the primary server."

Read that description again. That is the definition of ε, half the width of the honest bracket, written in 1985 and refined through 2010, sitting in an IETF standard.

Two terms dominate the formula. In words first: take half the total round-trip time all the way up to the reference clock, then add everything that has gone wrong or could have gone wrong since your last successful sync.

Term In words Where it came from
root delay ÷ 2 half the round trip, accumulated over every hop back to the reference clock §6, applied transitively
root dispersion the residual error at the last sync, plus growth since then §9.5's drift term, kept as a running total

The same thing with the symbol in it, the way the RFC writes it:

root distance  Λ  =  root delay / 2  +  root dispersion
                     └─────┬─────┘     └──────┬───────┘
                           │                  │
        §6's ±δ/2 — accumulated       everything that has gone
        over every hop from here      wrong or could have: the
        back to the reference         residual error at last sync,
        clock, halved                 plus growth since then

Root delay is the total round-trip delay all the way up the stratum chain to the reference clock. Halving it is §6, applied transitively: Alba's uncertainty includes her server's uncertainty about its server, and so on to the top.

Root dispersion is the accumulated maximum error, and here is the part that makes it honest: it grows on its own between updates. RFC 5905 defines a constant called PHI, the assumed worst-case frequency tolerance, conventionally 15 ppm. Dispersion grows at PHI continuously.

This gives Ben a control knob most people never connect to correctness. Dispersion grows at 15 µs per second of elapsed time since the last successful sync. So the poll interval sets ε.

| Poll interval | Dispersion growth before the next update | |---|---| | 16 s | 0.24 ms | | 64 s | 0.96 ms | | 1024 s (NTP's max) | 15.4 ms |

A machine that polls rarely is not "saving bandwidth". It is choosing a wider ε, and, if anything downstream reads that ε as Spanner's commit wait does, it is choosing higher latency. This is the same sawtooth google-spanner showed you, and now you can derive it rather than take it on faith.

10.3 Why can't your code see it?

So the number exists. Why does no application use it?

Because of the API. clock_gettime(CLOCK_REALTIME, &ts) returns a struct timespec, which is seconds and nanoseconds. There is no field for "± 85.8 µs".

The kernel does track an error estimate. ntp_adjtime() exposes maxerror and esterror in microseconds, and the daemon maintains them. But no ordinary program calls ntp_adjtime() to read the time, because that is not what the time API looks like.

Dev's Java is the same story one layer up:

System.currentTimeMillis()   // long. wall clock. no error bar. can go backwards.
Instant.now()                // richer type, same missing information

Neither can express uncertainty, so neither does, so every caller behaves as though ε were zero. The uncertainty is not lost to physics. It is lost to a type signature.

Which is precisely why the three systems in §8 had to build new APIs rather than new clocks:

System The API's whole point
TrueTime TT.now() returns [earliest, latest]. There is no method that returns a scalar, so you cannot accidentally ignore ε
ClockBound returns a confidence interval; AWS describes it as giving "a reliable confidence interval by automatically calculating the clock accuracy" rather than an estimate
fbclock returns a "Window of Uncertainty", a range of possible nanosecond timestamps

All three made the same design choice: remove the scalar from the API, so that callers are forced to confront the interval. That is the entire innovation. The GPS receivers came second.

10.4 Can you rebuild Spanner's sawtooth from scratch?

You now have every part needed to derive google-spanner's central number without being told it.

Google's time-slave daemon polls its time masters every 30 seconds and assumes a worst-case drift of 200 µs/s. Apply §10.1's dispersion rule with Google's PHI instead of NTP's 15 ppm:

dispersion growth over one poll interval  =  30 s × 200 µs/s  =  6 ms
communication delay term to the masters   ≈  1 ms          (§6's δ/2, on a LAN)

ε immediately after a poll   ≈  1 ms
ε just before the next poll  ≈  1 + 6  =  7 ms

The 1–7 ms sawtooth, derived. And now every knob on it is visible. Poll faster and the tooth shrinks. Buy a better-specified oscillator and the slope flattens. Move the time master closer and the floor drops.

google-spanner told you commit wait costs about 2ε on every write. You can now say precisely what buys it down. More usefully, AWS's 40 µs PHC figure would make that same wait about 175× cheaper than the paper's average. That is the shape of what changed between 2012 and now.

10.5 Which default should worry you?

One line from §9.4's table deserves its own section.

maxdistance is the maximum root distance chronyd will accept from a source before refusing to use it. Root distance is ε (§10.1). So the out-of-the-box posture of the most widely deployed NTP client on Linux is:

I will happily synchronise to a source that admits it might be wrong by up to three seconds.

This is a defensible default for its actual job. It is a sanity limit meant to exclude unreachable and grossly broken sources, not a service-level objective, and in practice a healthy source is thousands of times better than the limit.

But notice what it means for Ben. Nothing in the default configuration enforces a tight ε, and nothing warns him when ε is bad. A machine whose upstream has quietly degraded to a root distance of 800 ms will keep synchronising, keep reporting Leap status: Normal, and keep serving confident timestamps. His alerting has to ask for the bound. It is never volunteered.

That is §15's monitoring list, and it is the practical reason this section exists.

11. Which clock should Dev have used? The other one

In one breath: Your machine has two clocks. One tries to tell you the time in the world and is allowed to jump. One is a stopwatch that can never go backwards. Using the first to measure a duration is the most common clock bug in application code.

Every operating system offers at least two clocks, and confusing them is the most common clock bug in application code by a wide margin. Dev has shipped it. So has almost everyone.

The wall clock, which is CLOCK_REALTIME, System.currentTimeMillis() and Instant.now(), tries to tell you what time it is in the world. It is meaningful across machines. And, because of everything in §9, it is adjusted. The man page is explicit: CLOCK_REALTIME is "affected by discontinuous jumps in the system time and by the incremental adjustments performed by adjtime(3) and NTP". It can jump forward. It can jump backward. It can run at 92% or 108% of normal speed while a slew is in progress.

The monotonic clock, which is CLOCK_MONOTONIC and System.nanoTime(), counts elapsed time from an arbitrary, meaningless origin. It cannot be set. It never goes backwards. It is the right tool for every duration you will ever measure.

Here is the rule, and it is absolute:

Wall clock for when. Monotonic clock for how long. Never the other way round.

// WRONG — subtracting two wall-clock readings
long start = System.currentTimeMillis();
doWork();
long elapsed = System.currentTimeMillis() - start;   // can be negative

// RIGHT
long start = System.nanoTime();
doWork();
long elapsed = System.nanoTime() - start;            // cannot be negative

🔮 Predict: Dev argues the wrong version is fine, because his clock is only ever adjusted by a few microseconds and elapsed will just be a few microseconds off. What is he missing?

Reveal

He is missing that the size of the error does not matter. The sign does. A duration that comes back negative is not a slightly wrong number. It is a number that violates an invariant every line downstream assumes.

This took Cloudflare's DNS down on 1 January 2017. Their RRDNS resolver, written in Go, used time.Now() (Go's wall clock) to compute how long upstream servers had been taking. A leap second (§13) made the clock step backwards. A duration went negative. The negative value was passed to rand.Int63n(), which panics on a negative argument. At peak, about 0.2% of DNS queries to Cloudflare were affected.

The language itself was changed in response. Go 1.9 made time.Time carry a monotonic reading alongside the wall reading, specifically so that t2.Sub(t1) uses the monotonic one automatically.

If you guessed "the error is bounded by ε, so it is small", you had the right instinct about magnitude and the wrong model of the failure. Nothing crashes because a number is 3 ms out. Things crash because a number is −3 ms when the type system said it could not be.

Two further clocks are worth knowing, because they close real gaps.

CLOCK_MONOTONIC_RAW is monotonic and not slewed. Plain CLOCK_MONOTONIC never jumps, but it is "affected by the incremental adjustments performed by adjtime(3) and NTP". So during a slew it runs at up to 1/12 wrong rate. If Dev is measuring a hardware interval where an 8% rate error matters, this is the one he wants.

CLOCK_BOOTTIME is like monotonic, but it includes time the system was suspended. On a laptop or a VM that can be paused, CLOCK_MONOTONIC stops during suspend. A 6-hour sleep looks like zero elapsed time, and every timeout Dev set survives it. See §12.4.

A trap specific to System.nanoTime(). Its origin is arbitrary and unrelated to any other machine's. Two nanoTime() values from two JVMs cannot be compared, subtracted, or ordered, even two JVMs on the same host. The only legal operation is subtracting two readings from the same process. Monotonic time is not a "safer wall clock". It is a stopwatch, and stopwatches do not agree on what time it is.

12. What does this actually look like when it breaks?

In one breath: Six ways clocks break in production. Each one is chosen because it reveals a different part of the design, and each one happens to Alba, Bruno or somebody on Asha's team.

visualization.html Panel 4 runs several of these as predict-then-observe scenarios.

12.1 The step — what breaks when time jumps, in each direction?

A step (§9.4) is a discontinuity, and the two directions fail differently.

A backward step makes time repeat. Every duration computed as end − start across the step is wrong, and some are negative. §11's Cloudflare outage is the canonical case. The subtler damage is in things that wait. A cache entry stamped "expires at 10:00:05" gains five extra seconds of life. A rate limiter counting requests per second sees a window it has already counted. An append-only log with wall-clock keys writes a key it has already written.

A forward step makes nothing go negative, so it feels safe. It is not. A forward step of 30 seconds causes everything with a deadline in the next 30 seconds to expire at once. Every lease, every cache TTL, every retry timer, every connection idle timeout, simultaneously. The symptom Ben sees is a thundering herd with no traffic spike to explain it. This is the failure mode that makes people conclude "the network had a blip" when Arjun's network was fine.

What Asha watches for: chronyc tracking's Last offset jumping by orders of magnitude, Leap status, and a spike in Meera's duration metrics at exactly the same second on many hosts.

What survives it: nothing about ordering. The clock is more accurate after the step than before it, which is precisely why the step was applied. That is the trade §9.4 makes explicit, accuracy bought with continuity.

12.2 The route that is longer one way — why can't averaging remove it?

Two datacenters, London and Frankfurt. Traffic London to Frankfurt takes a direct fibre path, 9 ms. Traffic Frankfurt to London is routed via Amsterdam because of a BGP policy nobody has looked at in two years, 15 ms.

The round-trip delay is 24 ms. NTP halves it and assumes 12 ms each way. The truth is 9 and 15, so every offset estimate is wrong by (15 − 9)/2 = 3 ms, permanently, in a fixed direction.

This is §6's World B as an operational condition rather than a thought experiment. Three things make it nasty.

It does not average out. Taking a million samples gives Asha a beautifully stable estimate that is beautifully stably wrong. §9.2's minimum-delay filter does not help either, because every sample carries the same bias.

It looks like success. Low jitter, tight RMS offset, small root dispersion. Every health indicator is green, because the daemon is measuring exactly what it claims to measure. It just cannot see the asymmetry.

It survives everything. Restarts, failovers, daemon upgrades. It changes when the route changes, which is why it sometimes appears to be fixed by an unrelated network maintenance window, and then comes back.

The only real defences are §9.7, where Arjun's switches report residence time, and §9.8, where the network is removed. Plus the honest one: Alba's bracket of ±12 ms does contain the true offset. The bound was never violated. Only the point estimate was wrong, which is exactly why §8 says to consume the interval and not the midpoint.

12.3 The liar — how far does one wrong clock spread?

A time server's own upstream fails. Depending on the implementation, it may keep serving its last known time. Or serve something absurd. Or, the dangerous case, keep serving plausible time that is slowly drifting away from reality.

With one source (§9.2) Alba follows it. With intersection over four (§9.3) she rejects it. That is why "at least four sources" is conventional advice and not superstition.

But the important part is what happens downstream, and hybrid-logical-clocks already put a number on it. One node running +60 in a cluster with ±8 skew infected everyone it talked to, pushing the bound from 16 to 67, and the logical reading never came back down. HLC's max rule propagates a high value and has no mechanism to lower it. A single lying clock is not a single wrong machine. It is a wrong cluster, and the damage outlives the liar.

That is why the production answers are so blunt.

  • CockroachDB makes a node terminate itself if it detects it is outside the configured max-offset, which defaults to 500 ms. No clock is safer than a wrong one.
  • MongoDB HMAC-signs $clusterTime, so a client cannot inject a fabricated future timestamp.
  • Google runs Armageddon masters on atomic clocks, so a GPS-correlated failure cannot make every master lie in the same direction at once.

12.4 The pause — what happens to a stopwatch that was stopped?

Virtual machines get suspended, migrated, and descheduled by their hypervisor. During that gap, the guest's view of time can stop entirely.

🔮 Predict: Alba is a VM. Her hypervisor pauses her for four and a half minutes. Dev set a 30-second health-check timeout before the pause. Does it fire?

Reveal

No. And that is the surprising half.

The consequences of a pause are asymmetric, and both are bad.

The wall clock is stale on resume. Alba wakes believing it is still 10:00:00 when it is 10:04:30. Then she gets corrected by a large forward step (§12.1), and everything expires at once.

CLOCK_MONOTONIC does not count the suspended interval. A 4½-minute gap looks like zero elapsed time, so every timeout Dev set survives the pause. The 30-second health check does not fire. A lease Priya believed had expired has not, from the holder's point of view. CLOCK_BOOTTIME is the clock that counts suspend, and it exists for exactly this reason.

If you guessed "yes, it fires immediately on resume", you were picturing the wall clock. Dev used the monotonic one, correctly, for a duration. Being right about §11 is what caused this.

This is also the cleanest illustration of why a lease-based design cannot be made safe by shortening the lease. A process that is paused does not know it was paused, and shortening the timeout does not change that.

12.5 The cold boot — how do you fetch the time when you don't know the time?

A machine boots. Its hardware real-time clock is off by minutes. Or its battery is dead and it comes up in 1970. Or it is a container with no such clock at all. The NTP daemon has not yet made contact, so Leap status is not yet Normal and no correction has been applied.

For a few seconds to a few minutes, the machine is confidently wrong and serving traffic.

The interesting part is the deadlock this creates with Omar's security systems. To fetch the correct time securely you would like to use TLS. To validate a TLS certificate you must check notBefore/notAfter against your clock. If your clock says 1970, every certificate on earth is "not yet valid" and the fetch fails. So you cannot get the time, so you cannot fix the clock.

That is why NTP is traditionally unauthenticated UDP. It is why chrony's makestep exists with a large threshold for the first few updates (§9.4). And it is why "just use HTTPS to get the time" is not the obvious improvement it appears to be.

12.6 The lease two machines both held — whose fault was it?

This is distributed-locking's advice, with the number attached. Priya owns the lock service.

Priya's service grants Alba a lease: "you hold the lock until 10:00:05.000." Alba's clock reads 10:00:04.900. She believes she still has 100 ms, and proceeds to write.

But Alba is the machine from §2, running 1.73 s fast. True time is 10:00:03.17. Meanwhile Priya's lock service, whose clock is correct, sees the lease expire at true 10:00:05.000 and grants it to Bruno. Bruno's clock is 2.59 s slow, so he believes he has until his own 10:00:02.41. He starts writing at his 09:59:58, which is true 10:00:00.6.

Both machines believe they hold the lock, and both are obeying the protocol correctly. No message was lost. No node crashed. The only fault is that three clocks disagreed.

This is why distributed-locking insisted on fencing tokens. A fencing token is a monotonically increasing number issued with the lock and checked by the storage system, which rejects any write carrying a token lower than one it has already seen. A fencing token is a logical clock doing the safety job, precisely because the physical clock cannot be trusted to do it.

The general rule, worth memorising: if the correctness of your system depends on comparing two clocks on two machines, you have a bug whose severity is set by ε, and ε is a number you probably have not looked up. Use physical time for timeouts and heuristics, where being wrong costs latency. Use logical mechanisms for safety, where being wrong costs correctness. Fencing tokens, epochs and quorums are the logical mechanisms. split-brain made this same argument from the other end.

13. Why does the world deliberately get one second wrong?

In one breath: Twice a definition of "second" has to be reconciled with the Earth's actual rotation, and the fix is to make one minute 61 seconds long. Almost no software can represent that, so most of it fakes the second by stepping backwards, all over the planet at once.

This deserves its own section because it is the one clock problem that is scheduled, global, and intentional. It has caused more famous outages than drift ever has.

What is a leap second, and why is 23:59:60 legal?

Two definitions of the second have to coexist. Atomic time counts caesium transitions and is perfectly regular. Astronomical time follows the Earth's rotation, which is irregular and slowly slowing.

UTC is defined to follow atomic time in rate but stay within 0.9 s of astronomical time in value. The mechanism that reconciles them is the leap second: occasionally, a minute contains 61 seconds.

23:59:58
23:59:59
23:59:60   ← this second exists
00:00:00

Which means 23:59:60 is a legal time, and almost no software can represent it. POSIX time_t counts seconds since the epoch on the assumption that every day has exactly 86,400 of them. There is no room in that model for a 61-second minute. So implementations fake it, usually by repeating 23:59:59, which is a one-second backwards step (§12.1) delivered simultaneously to every machine on Earth.

What has it actually broken?

🔮 Predict: A leap second arrives. Asha's fleet is fully patched and its code never subtracts two wall-clock readings. Is she safe?

Reveal

Not necessarily, because one of the two famous outages had nothing to do with application code at all.

30 June 2012. The leap second triggered a livelock in the Linux kernel's hrtimer/NTP subsystem. Inserting the leap second caused a cascade of timer recalculations that pinned CPUs at about 100%. Reddit, Mozilla, LinkedIn and Qantas were among the publicly affected. The fix reworked the locking between the timekeeping and NTP subsystems.

1 January 2017. Cloudflare's RRDNS, as in §11. A duration went negative, because Go's time.Now() was not monotonic.

If you guessed "safe, because the bug is always in application code", 2012 is the counterexample. Both outages are the same root cause seen from different layers: software assumed time does not go backwards, and once in a while the standard says it does.

How does everyone dodge it now? Smearing

Rather than insert a discontinuity, spread the extra second out. Run every clock slightly slow for hours, so the extra second is absorbed without any jump. This is §9.4's slewing, applied on purpose at planetary scale.

Who Smear window Note
Google 24 hours the original public smear, from 2016
AWS 24 hours applied automatically on both local and public (time.aws.com) Time Sync
Meta 17 hours chosen because the smear happens on Stratum 2, where hundreds of NTP servers must smear in step

Meta's PTP-era version is the most interesting, because it is §8's idea applied to a leap second. Rather than smearing the clock, the fbclock library widens the Window of Uncertainty, shifting time by one nanosecond every 62.5 microseconds. The system does not pretend to know the time more precisely than it does during the event. It admits the extra doubt and lets callers decide.

The hazard nobody expects: mixing smeared and unsmeared sources. During the smear, a smeared server and an unsmeared server genuinely disagree, by up to a full second, and both are behaving correctly. Feed both to one client and §9.3's intersection algorithm sees two mutually inconsistent sources and cannot resolve them.

AWS states this plainly: because smeared and non-smeared time differ during a leap second event, "we do not recommend using both smeared and non-smeared time sources in your time client configuration". Note that AWS's own PHC (§9.8) does not smear. It follows UTC and inserts the leap second. So an instance using both the PHC and a smeared NTP source is exactly the configuration being warned about.

They are being abolished — so why keep this in your head?

At the 27th General Conference on Weights and Measures in November 2022, the CGPM resolved to stop inserting leap seconds by or before 2035, letting UTC drift from astronomical time instead. A "leap minute", or some other mechanism, is to be decided later. Meta campaigned publicly for this. AWS has stated it is "in full support".

Three reasons this does not remove leap seconds from your mental model.

  1. Until 2035, they can still happen, and any one of them can be scheduled with six months' notice.
  2. Smearing means your clock is deliberately wrong for a whole day, by up to a second, on purpose, during every smear window. If Priya has a design that assumes ε is a few milliseconds, a smear day violates it by two to three orders of magnitude, and no daemon will report an error, because nothing is wrong.
  3. The historical record keeps them forever. 27 leap seconds have already been inserted. Any system converting between UTC and TAI across a past date still needs the table. That table lives in tzdata, which is a data file that must be updated, so it is a dependency that can go stale.

Say it back. Without using the words leap, smear or UTC, explain to Omar why a scheduled, announced, well-understood one-second change has taken down more large services than years of ordinary clock drift.

A good answer sounds like "Drift moves every machine a tiny bit at a time, in its own direction, and nothing notices. This one moves every machine on the planet by a whole second at the same instant, and it moves them the way software is least prepared for: backwards. So instead of a slow disagreement you get one synchronised jolt, everywhere, hitting every program that assumed the clock only ever goes forwards."

14. What exactly is guaranteed, and what merely usually happens?

In one breath: Clock synchronisation promises one thing: the clock is within ε of the truth. Almost everything your code assumes about clocks is not that promise, and each of those assumptions fails in its own way.

What does clock synchronisation actually guarantee?

One thing only. The clock's reading is within ε of true time, where ε is the number computed in §10.1 and reported by Asha's daemon. In symbols: |clock_reading − true_time| ≤ ε.

That is a real, useful, checkable guarantee. It is also much weaker than what most code assumes.

What does it not guarantee, and why do people assume it anyway?

Read the right-hand column, because it is the reason each of these survives code review.

Not guaranteed Why people assume it anyway
That the wall clock is monotonic it usually is, between steps (§12.1)
That two machines' timestamps are comparable they have the same format and look like numbers on one line (§2)
That a timestamp difference is a duration it is, on one machine, between steps (§11)
That ε is small right now it usually is, and nothing tells you when it isn't (§10.5)
That ordering by timestamp reflects causality it correlates well enough to pass testing (hybrid-logical-clocks still counted 79 ties at skew zero, from finite resolution alone)
That a sub-ε difference means anything at all two timestamps 2 ms apart with ε = 12 ms carry no ordering information whatsoever

That last row is the one to internalise. Comparing two timestamps whose difference is smaller than ε is not a weak signal. It is noise. If ε is 12 ms and Meera orders two events 2 ms apart, she is ordering them by coin flip. That is the mistake that produces "impossible" traces and multi-day investigations.

Which assumption fails first?

Every guarantee above rests on a stack, and each layer can fail on its own. Read the last column first: it is the only one that tells you whether you would ever find out.

But it cannot be found and removed. Only §9.7 and §9.8 address it, and they do so by changing what information is available, not by measuring better.

What should Ben alert on?

Ordered by how often the alert would have caught a real incident.

  1. The error bound itself. System time + 0.5 × Root delay + Root dispersion, from §10.2. This is the metric almost nobody has and everybody needs. Alert on it exceeding whatever Priya's design actually assumes, and if she cannot say what her design assumes, that is the finding.

What are the four rules for Dev's code?

  1. Monotonic for durations, wall clock for wall time. System.nanoTime() for elapsed, Instant.now() for "when". Never subtract two wall-clock readings. (§11)
  2. Never compare timestamps from two machines for correctness. For display and debugging, fine, and label the axis "±ε". For a decision that must be right, use a fencing token, an epoch, or a quorum. (§12.6)
  3. Never order events whose separation is smaller than ε. If you need that ordering, you need a logical clock, and HLC exists for exactly this. (§14)
  4. If you must use physical time for correctness, read ε and pay it. That is what commit wait is. If you are not willing to wait 2ε, you are not willing to have the guarantee, and you should stop claiming it.

16. Which seven ideas about clocks are wrong?

In one breath: Seven sentences that sound reasonable in a design review and are not true. Each one is a different way of forgetting that a clock's real output is a bracket.

1. "NTP keeps my clocks in sync." It keeps them bounded, and it tells you the bound. Those are different claims. §10.2's two hosts differ by a factor of 212 in ε, and both are "in sync" by any check Ben is currently running. Replace it with: NTP makes ε small and reports it; whether small enough is a question about your design, not about NTP.

2. "Offset and drift are the same thing." Offset is position, drift is velocity (§3). Correcting offset alone puts Alba back where she started one poll interval later. §9.5 put the number on it: 1.28 ms of fresh error every 64 seconds, forever. This confusion is why people "fix" a clock repeatedly and are surprised it keeps breaking.

3. "Better hardware makes ε zero." Hardware shrinks ε. Nothing makes it zero, because §6's asymmetry is a property of the network, not the clock. And the deeper error: a design that never reads ε is unsafe at any ε. google-spanner's central lesson, restated: Google's atomic clocks made Spanner fast; refusing to hide ε made it correct.

4. "RTT/2 is the one-way delay." It is the midpoint of the possible one-way delays. §6's three worlds have identical round trips and one-way splits of 19.5/19.5, 4.5/34.5 and 34.5/4.5. Using RTT/2 as a measurement is exactly the assumption §12.2 punishes.

5. "The monotonic clock is a safer wall clock, so I'll use it everywhere." System.nanoTime() has an arbitrary origin that is meaningless outside its own process. Two monotonic readings from two machines cannot be compared, ordered, or subtracted. The operation is not merely inaccurate, it is undefined. It is a stopwatch, not a clock.

6. "We're in one datacenter, so skew is negligible." Possibly true, and nobody has checked. chrony's default maxdistance is 3 seconds (§10.5). Nothing in a default install enforces anything tighter, or warns you when a source degrades. The distributed-transactions lab found 0 stale snapshots at skew ≤ 2 and 25 at skew 20 using the same design. A system with no guarantee is indistinguishable from a correct one right up until the day it is not. Go and read the number.

7. "Leap seconds are being abolished, so this is historical trivia." They can still be inserted until 2035. Smearing makes your clock deliberately wrong by up to a second for a whole day, with every health check green (§13). And the historical table of 27 past leap seconds lives in tzdata, which is a dependency that can go stale.

Say it back. Without using the words bound, interval or epsilon, tell Ben what to put on the clock dashboard he started building in §3, and why the obvious metric is the wrong one.

A good answer sounds like "The obvious metric is 'is the clock synced', and it says yes on a machine that is a hundred microseconds out and on one that is eighteen milliseconds out. What he actually wants on the wall is the machine's own admission of how wrong it might be right now. The daemon already works that number out and prints it. Nobody reads it, and it is the only number that tells him whether our design's assumption still holds."

Clock Synchronisation — Solutions

Attempt EXERCISES.md first. Levels 7–8 are design discussions: what follows is a worked reasoning process, not the only defensible answer, and where that is true it is said so.

Level 1 — Explain

1.1 Two reasons.

  1. The answer is stale. Bruno's reply was true when he wrote it, and it spent the return leg travelling. Alba would set her clock behind by exactly the return-leg delay — systematically, in one direction, never averaging out. This one is fixable: measure the round trip and add half of it (Cristian's algorithm, §5).
  2. She does not know how the round trip split between the two legs. Half is a guess. If the outbound leg was 4.5 ms and the return 34.5 ms, her answer is 15 ms wrong and every number she can observe is identical to the case where it is exactly right. This one is not fixable from round-trip measurements — one equation, two unknowns (§6).

1.2

  • ε is not a hardware quality problem because its floor is δ/2 — half the network round trip — and that is a property of the path, not of the clock at either end. Two atomic clocks connected by a 40 ms link still cannot determine their offset to better than ±20 ms by talking to each other.
  • A better oscillator does not remove the need for ε because it only flattens the drift term between polls; it cannot tell you which direction the network delay went. And the deeper reason: a design that never reads ε is unsafe at any ε — the number being small does not make the code that ignores it correct.

1.3

"NTP does not keep clocks equal — it keeps each clock within a bound of UTC and tells us what that bound currently is. Until we know that number for both hosts, two timestamps closer together than the bound tell us nothing about which event came first."

And if it matters for correctness rather than debugging:

"For anything where getting the order wrong is a bug rather than an inconvenience, don't order by timestamp at all — use a fencing token, an epoch, or a single sequencer."

1.4 Three questions physical time answers and logical time cannot:

  1. "Has this lease/certificate expired?" — expiry is not an event anybody sent a message about, so there is no happens-before edge leading to it.
  2. "How long did this take?" — a Lamport counter difference of 4 could be 4 µs or 4 hours. The hybrid-logical-clocks lab measured one counter value spanning 34 ticks of real time.
  3. "What happened between 09:00 and 09:05?" — auditors, regulators and billing systems ask this, and "between counter 4100 and 4900" is not an answer to it.

What these have in common: logical time is built only out of messages that were actually sent. All three questions are about events that generated no message, or about a quantity (duration, calendar position) that message-passing does not carry.

Level 2 — Identify

2.1

(a) ε = system time + ½ × root delay + root dispersion

  = 0.247 ms  +  (0.5 × 4.820 ms)  +  0.934 ms
  = 0.247     +   2.410            +  0.934
  = 3.591 ms

(b) ½ × root delay = 2.41 ms, which is 67% of the total. This is §6's irreducible term: the machine is several network hops from its reference.

(c) The refinement is §9.8 — move the reference clock next to the machine (or §9.7, PTP, if you own the switches). Dropping root delay from 4.82 ms to a cloud-local source's ~0.112 ms takes the half-delay term from 2.410 ms to 0.056 ms; dispersion falls with it. The bound goes from ~3.6 ms to well under 100 µs — the figure AWS publishes for its local NTP path. Nothing about the crystal changed.

2.2

  1. Drift (a rate, in ppm).
  2. Skew (a difference between two clocks; says nothing about whether either is right).
  3. Offset (a position error against a reference).
  4. Jitter (variability of the measurement, not of the clock).
  5. Drift, expressed in seconds per day instead of ppm. 20 ppm and 1.73 s/day are the same statement.

2.3 v7 (PTP transparent/boundary clocks) and v8 (GPS/PPS/PHC).

What they have in common: both obtain information the round trip cannot provide. A transparent clock supplies the missing second equation by reporting its own residence time; GPS supplies it by geometry — the receiver knows where the satellite is, so it can compute the propagation delay instead of halving a round trip. Every other refinement takes the same information and processes it better; these two change what information exists.

2.4 v4 — slew instead of step. It makes the clock less accurate in the short term, because the correction is applied gradually instead of instantly. It is in the list because accuracy is not the only property a clock has: v4 buys monotonicity, without which every duration measured on the machine is unreliable. It is the refinement whose absence has caused the most production outages, and the one that improves no metric anybody puts on a dashboard.

Level 3 — Predict

3.1

δ = (T4 − T1) − (T3 − T2) = (12.000 − 0) − (250.900 − 250.400) = 12.000 − 0.500 = 11.500 ms
θ = ((T2 − T1) + (T3 − T4)) / 2 = (250.400 + 238.900) / 2 = 244.650 ms
honest interval = [T2 − δ, T2] = [238.900, 250.400] ms      (ε = 5.750 ms)

The sentence Alba is entitled to say:

"Bruno's clock is ahead of mine by somewhere between 238.9 ms and 250.4 ms. My single best guess is 244.65 ms, and nothing in this exchange can narrow it further."

Note that the interval is exactly [T2 − δ, T2]: at one extreme all the delay was outbound, at the other all of it was on the return.

3.2 (a) 15 ppm × 1024 s = 15.36 ms. (b) 15 ppm × 64 s = 0.96 ms — sixteen times tighter. (c) The long interval saved fewer than 10 KB per host per hour of network traffic (an NTP exchange is roughly 90 bytes each way). It bought that with 15 ms of uncertainty on every timestamp the machine produces. If anything downstream consumes ε — a commit wait, an uncertainty restart, a decision about whether two events can be ordered — the operator traded a rounding error in bandwidth for a first-order cost in latency and resolution. Poll interval is not a bandwidth setting; it is an accuracy setting that happens to consume bandwidth.

3.3 With ε = 12 ms on each machine, two clocks can differ by up to 24 ms (ε + ε) while both remain within their bounds.

  • 3 ms apart: you can conclude nothing. Either order is consistent with both clocks being correct. Ordering these two events is a coin flip that produces a confident-looking answer.
  • 40 ms apart: 40 > 24, so the order is real — provided both bounds actually hold, which is a conditional worth saying out loud, because it fails exactly in the cases (§12.2, §12.3) where you most want the answer.

The general rule: you can only order events separated by more than the sum of the two machines' error bounds.

3.4 4.32 s ÷ (1/12) = 51.84 seconds minimum, and in practice longer, because corrtimeratio 3 makes chronyd prefer slower corrections.

During that period the clock is running at up to 1/12 off nominal rate — so every duration the machine measures is wrong by up to 8.3%, and it is wrong in a way no error will reveal. Note the trade this makes explicit: the clock is never discontinuous (good), but it is knowingly running at the wrong rate for almost a minute (bad). Neither choice is free, which is why distributions ship makestep 1.0 3 — step once at boot, slew forever after.

3.5 The two questions have opposite answers, which is the point.

  • ε goes up. If you average n estimates each bounded by δᵢ/2, the tightest bound you can honestly claim on the average is mean(δᵢ)/2 — and that is always ≥ min(δᵢ)/2. Averaging can only loosen the provable bound. (Panel 1's sampling table shows this on every draw.)
  • The actual error may well go down, if the delay split varies randomly between samples, because random errors partly cancel.

So averaging buys you a typically-better answer and a definitely-worse guarantee. NTP chooses the guarantee, and it is right to: the actual error is something you got lucky about and cannot publish, whereas the bound is something you can put in a contract. And note the case that destroys the consolation prize entirely — a fixed path asymmetry (§12.2) — where averaging improves neither the bound nor the error.

Level 4 — Modify

4.1 (a) 5 s × 200 µs/s = 1 ms of drift growth. The sawtooth becomes 1 ms → 2 ms (the 1 ms floor is the communication delay to the time masters, which the poll rate does not touch). (b) The paper's ε averages ~4 ms, so commit wait ≈ 8 ms. The new ε averages ~1.5 ms, so commit wait ≈ 3 ms. About 5 ms saved on every write, fleet-wide. (c) Two costs, and a third worth noticing:

  • 6× the load on the time-master tier, which must be sized for the whole fleet's poll rate. Time masters are special hardware; this is a capital cost.
  • 6× the polling traffic, on every machine, forever.
  • Diminishing returns. The 1 ms communication floor now dominates the sawtooth. Halving the interval again to 2.5 s takes the peak from 2 ms to 1.5 ms — a quarter of the benefit for the same doubling of cost. The next real improvement has to attack the floor (§9.6–§9.8), not the poll rate. (d) Panel 2 → drift 200 ppm, poll interval to its lowest, network uncertainty 1 ms, strategy "slew + correct the frequency". The steady-state ε readout is the answer.

4.2 The bound stops being a bound.

ε is only meaningful as a promise, and the promise is conditional: it holds while the oscillator drifts no faster than the assumed worst case. Measuring your crystals today tells you what they do today, in this rack, at this temperature, at this age. It does not tell you what a machine in a hot aisle does in eighteen months, or what a badly virtualised timer does under a noisy neighbour, or what a part does as it degrades toward failure.

When would you find out? Not from the clock system — every indicator stays green, because nothing in the daemon is checking the assumption. You would find out from a downstream correctness violation: a read that returned stale data when the protocol said it could not, a commit that appeared out of order. That is the worst possible detector — indirect, rare, non-reproducible, and it looks exactly like an application bug.

This is why Google assumes 200 µs/s when its crystals do far better (§9.5), and why CockroachDB makes a node kill itself rather than continue outside its max-offset window. The gap between typical and guaranteed is the safety margin.

4.3 makestep 0.001 -1 means: step whenever the offset exceeds 1 ms, with no limit on how many times.

A healthy host's offset crosses 1 ms constantly — that is just the normal sawtooth between polls. So the fleet now steps continuously, many times an hour, in both directions, on every host.

They have enabled: every wall-clock duration on every host is now unreliable, all the time. Negative durations (the Cloudflare failure mode) stop being a once-a-decade leap-second event and become a routine occurrence. Mass simultaneous expiries (§12.1) happen hourly.

And it does not even achieve its stated goal. Stepping on each measurement chases the noise instead of filtering it — the daemon's clock filter and frequency discipline (§9.2, §9.5) exist precisely to avoid reacting to individual samples, and constant stepping perturbs the discipline loop so it never settles. They will end up with a less accurate clock that also moves backwards.

4.4 From the public NTP pool (ε ≈ 18.2 ms):

  • terms are system time 0.51 ms, ½ root delay 15.62 ms, dispersion 2.10 ms.
  • Single largest win: move to a nearby source. Root delay 31 ms → 0.11 ms takes the dominant term from 15.62 ms to 0.056 ms.
  • Second: the dispersion base (2.10 ms), which largely falls out for free with the same change, because a nearby well-disciplined source has a much smaller accumulated error to report.

Once on the cloud-local deployment (ε ≈ 86 µs): terms are 8.3 µs / 56 µs / 22 µs. The dominant term is still half the root delay — so the answer's identity is unchanged, but the remedy is completely different. You can no longer fix it by moving the server closer; you fix it by changing where the timestamp is taken (§9.6 hardware timestamping), then by making the switches report residence time (§9.7), then by removing the network (§9.8). That shift — same diagnosis, different cure at a different scale — is the useful thing to take from this exercise.

Why the comparison is the interesting part. The intersection is narrower than any individual source. Combining honest sources is not merely a liar- detection mechanism that costs you nothing; it is an ε-reduction mechanism that leaves you more certain than your single best source could make you. That is the second, less-quoted reason the conventional advice is "configure at least four servers" — and it is the argument against exercise 6.1's single excellent appliance.

Level 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 — the single excellent source.

Smallest failing scenario: the appliance's GPS antenna cable is disconnected during rack work. The receiver enters holdover, keeps serving time from its internal oscillator, keeps advertising stratum 1, and keeps reporting a small dispersion. It drifts. Every host follows it precisely and confidently.

Guarantee violated: the ε bound itself — the true error leaves the interval, and no observation available to any host reveals it, because they all agree with each other perfectly. (This is §6's structure a second time: identical evidence, different worlds.)

The correct reading: quality of a source and independence of sources are different purchases. Four mediocre servers can detect a liar; one excellent server cannot. Note also 5.3's result — four sources would have given a tighter ε than the single appliance, not merely a safer one. Google's Armageddon masters run on atomic clocks rather than GPS specifically so that a GPS-correlated failure — jamming, spoofing, a week-number rollover bug — cannot make every source lie in the same direction at once.

6.2 — the monitoring agent.

Smallest failing scenario: the monitoring server's own clock drifts by 500 ms. Every host in the fleet alerts simultaneously. The on-call engineer sees a fleet-wide clock event and starts investigating the fleet — which is fine.

The worse scenario: the monitoring server takes time from the same upstream as the fleet, and that upstream becomes a falseticker. Everything drifts together, the differences stay under 100 ms, and nothing alerts at all while the entire fleet is wrong.

A third flaw: the reported difference includes the heartbeat's network delay, which is unbounded and asymmetric. A congested link is indistinguishable from a clock problem.

Guarantee violated: none — it never had one. This metric is a comparison of two clocks, which §12.6's rule says can never be a correctness signal.

The fix: alert on each host's self-reported error bound (root distance from chronyc tracking). That is a local, absolute quantity that requires no comparison, includes the network term honestly, and grows on its own when a host loses contact with its sources.

6.3 — the distributed rate limiter.

Smallest failing scenario: limit is 100 requests/second. Host A's clock is 5 ms fast, host B's is 5 ms slow. At true time 10:00:00.999, A computes bucket 10:00:01 while B is still computing bucket 10:00:00. For the 10 ms window around each second boundary, two buckets are simultaneously live across the fleet, so up to 200 requests can be admitted within one real second.

Symmetrically, at the far edge of the window the effective second is shortened and the limiter under-admits.

Guarantee violated: the rate limit — the one property the component exists to provide. And note the shape of the failure: it is worst exactly at the boundary, which is where bursty traffic tends to land.

The fix: there must be exactly one clock in the decision. Have the store assign the bucket (Redis TIME, or a Lua script using server time), or use a token bucket whose refill is computed from server-side timestamps. The distributed-rate-limiting lab's designs all have this property; this exercise is what happens when you lose it.

6.4 — measuring one-way delay directly.

It is circular. The receiver computes receive_time − send_time, but those two readings are on different clocks, so the result is:

observed = one-way delay + clock offset

One equation, two unknowns — the same unknown pair §6 started with. To extract the delay you would need the offset, which is what you were trying to compute from the delay.

It becomes possible only if you already have synchronised clocks (circular), or if something outside the round trip supplies the missing equation: PTP's transparent clocks reporting residence time, or a hardware clock on the machine with no network in between. This is exactly why AWS lists "measure one-way network latency" as a benefit of the PTP Hardware Clock and hardware packet timestamping, rather than as something you can do with plain NTP.

6.5 — timestamps as the log's ordering key.

Smallest failing scenario: ε is 12 ms. Service A emits order-created at its 10:00:00.100; service B, having received the order, emits order-paid at its 10:00:00.090. B's event sorts before its own cause. The UUID tiebreak does not fire (the timestamps differ), so the system produces a stable, deterministic, wrong order — and reproduces it identically on every replay, which is what makes it so hard to diagnose.

Counterfactual — if wall-clock timestamps were unique and monotonic per machine: the design is still broken. Per-machine monotonicity guarantees that A's own events are correctly ordered among themselves, and says nothing at all about A's events relative to B's. Machine A's 10:00:00.100 can still be genuinely later than machine B's 10:00:00.050.

What is missing: a causal link — an ordering derived from the messages that actually passed between the services, not from two independent readings of two independent rocks. That is Lamport's clock, a vector clock, or an HLC. This is the exact gap HLC was invented to close, and it is the reason the hybrid-logical-clocks lab exists: keep a stamp that reads like a wall-clock time so "as of 5 p.m." still works, while guaranteeing that a cause always stamps below its effect.

Level 7 — Handle failure

7.1 — a lease design safe at any ε.

The honest starting point: you cannot make lease expiry safe when ε is unknown. The exercise's real answer is to stop asking expiry to carry the safety property.

The design:

  1. The lock service (backed by something linearizable — Raft, ZooKeeper, etcd) issues, with every lock grant, a monotonically increasing fencing token.
  2. Every write to the protected resource carries its token.
  3. The resource — the storage layer, the database, the file service — remembers the highest token it has accepted and rejects any write carrying a lower one.

Which component enforces safety: the resource, not the lock holder and not the lock service. This is the crucial relocation. A paused, slow, or wrong-clocked holder cannot know it has lost the lock — that is the whole problem — so the check must live somewhere that does know, and the only place that knows is the thing being written to.

What the lease duration is now for: liveness, not safety. It determines how long the system waits before handing the lock to somebody else — i.e. how long an outage lasts when a holder dies. Getting it wrong now costs availability, which is a tuning problem, rather than correctness, which is a bug. That is exactly the §12.6 split: physical time for timeouts and heuristics, logical mechanisms for safety.

What to say if the resource cannot check tokens (a plain S3 bucket, a third-party API): then you do not have a safe design, and you should say so rather than tune the lease. The remaining options are to partition the work so that only one writer can ever touch a given key, to use a compare-and-set on a version the resource does support, or to accept and document the risk. Shortening the lease is not on the list — it reduces the window, not the possibility, and it makes spurious lock loss more frequent.

7.2 — the mixed smeared/unsmeared fleet.

Now (six months out):

  • Audit which hosts take time from which sources. The mixed configuration is the actual defect; find every instance of it.
  • Pick one regime fleet-wide. Either everything follows the smeared NTP endpoint, or everything follows the unsmeared PHC and the software is verified to tolerate a repeated second. Do not run both. AWS says this plainly: because smeared and non-smeared time differ during the event, do not configure both.
  • Verify tzdata / the leap-second table is current on every host and inside every container image — a stale image is a host that does not know the leap is coming.
  • Confirm stepping is off (or bounded to the first few updates after boot), so that whatever happens, nothing jumps.
  • Audit code for wall-clock durations. Every currentTimeMillis() subtraction is a latent Cloudflare. This is the item with the longest lead time, which is why it starts now.

On the day:

  • Freeze deploys across the window.
  • Watch Leap status, per-host offset, source reachability, and — the one people forget — a counter of negative durations anywhere in the fleet.
  • Do not run reconciliation, ordering-sensitive batch jobs, or anything that compares timestamps across services.

What to tell dependent teams:

"For 24 hours starting at midnight UTC, our clocks are deliberately wrong by up to one second, and that is the correct behaviour. Any design assuming millisecond-scale skew is suspended for that day. Nothing will alert, because nothing is broken."

If the configuration cannot be changed in time: remove one of the two sources from clients — either one. The mixed configuration is strictly worse than either regime alone, because it defeats the intersection algorithm (§9.3): the client sees two mutually inconsistent sources and no majority to break the tie. And disable stepping, so that whatever disagreement remains is absorbed as a rate error rather than a jump.

7.3 — the 4.3-second p99.

What is happening: this is not latency. request_sent_at is read from the client's clock and request_received_at from the server's, so the difference is

observed = true latency + (server clock − client clock)

and the second term is the fleet's skew (§2). A 5 ms hop between two machines 4.3 s apart reports as 4.3 s. Nobody can find the slow path because there is no slow path.

The one measurement that confirms it: the falsifying one. A true latency is positive in both directions; a clock skew is positive one way and negative the other. So measure the same pair of services in reverse — client → server and server → client — using the same cross-clock subtraction. If one direction shows +4.3 s and the other shows −4.3 s, it is skew, conclusively. (The weaker confirmation is to measure the round trip entirely on the client's own monotonic clock: one clock, no skew, and if that shows 5 ms you are done.)

What the dashboard should be built from: durations must be measured on one machine, on its monotonic clock. That means client-side round-trip time, or server-side processing time, or a proper distributed trace in which each span's duration comes from a single machine's monotonic clock and only the span nesting is cross-machine. Cross-clock subtraction is never a latency measurement; at best it is a skew measurement wearing a latency label — which, incidentally, makes it a perfectly good clock-skew monitor if you relabel the axis.

Level 8 — Design

8.1 — 100 µs to UTC, three datacenters plus a colo, provable.

What survives from the lab's model: everything in §6 — ε is still δ/2 plus dispersion, and the interval is still the honest output. All of §9's ladder still describes your options.

What changes: the requirement is accuracy, not merely precision (§3). It is not enough for your machines to agree with each other; they must agree with UTC, and that means a traceable link to a national standard. Internal consistency, the thing most distributed systems actually need, is not the deliverable here.

Which refinements you need:

  • §9.8 GPS + PPS at each site — mandatory. 100 µs is not reachable over any wide-area network path, so each site needs its own reference. Two independent references per site (GPS plus a rubidium or caesium holdover oscillator), so a constellation-level failure degrades to holdover instead of to drift.
  • §9.7 PTP with boundary or transparent clocks inside each site — mandatory, because the last hops must not reintroduce δ/2 at millisecond scale.
  • §9.6 hardware timestamping — mandatory, and it is what makes §9.7 worth anything.
  • §9.3 multiple sources / intersection — needed, but for a different reason than usual: not only falseticker rejection but the independence the regulator will ask about.
  • §9.2, §9.5 — still running underneath, unremarkable.
  • What you do not need: the public NTP pool, anywhere, for anything.

What "prove it" adds beyond "achieve it":

  • You must record ε continuously, not just achieve it. The error bound becomes a logged, retained compliance artifact — ClockBound-style interval logging is now the deliverable, and this is the requirement most teams miss because it is invisible until an audit.
  • Traceability: a documented chain from your clock to a national standard, including the holdover specification — how long, and how badly, you degrade when GPS is lost.
  • Evidence of the failure modes, not just the happy path: what the record looks like during a holdover event.

The hard part is the colo. You do not own those switches, so PTP transparent clocks may simply be unavailable, and the answer becomes a GPS antenna in your own cage — which is a contractual and physical problem (roof access, cable runs, lightning protection) long before it is an engineering one. Budget for it as a lease negotiation, not a sprint.

8.2 — external consistency on stock VMs with ε = 18 ms.

Option Mechanism Cost Honest assessment
1. Commit wait as-is Stamp with latest, hold the ack until earliest has passed ~36 ms added to every write Correct, no clock spend, and usually unacceptable
2. Shrink ε first, then commit wait Move to the platform's local time service (ε → ~86 µs) or a PHC (→ ~40 µs) A config change, or an instance-type constraint Commit wait becomes ~80 µs. Usually the right answer if the platform offers it
3. Don't use clocks at all Route the ordering decision through a single sequencer or timestamp oracle (Percolator/TiDB's PD) One coordination hop; a scaling bottleneck to manage Zero clock dependence. Correct even at ε = 3 s
4. Causal tokens (HLC) Client carries a token from its write; reads wait for it Almost nothing Partial — and you must say so. Covers a client that can see its own write; does not cover a stranger who learned of the write by some other path, which is precisely what external consistency means

Choice: (2), falling back to (3). If the platform has a local time service, option 2 turns a 36 ms tax into a rounding error and the design stays decentralised — this is the single biggest practical change between 2012 and now, and it is why Spanner's design stopped being exotic. If it does not, take (3): one coordination hop is almost always cheaper than 36 ms on every write, and it degrades predictably.

Option 4 is included because it is what teams reach for first, and the important skill is being able to say exactly which guarantee it does and does not provide. hybrid-logical-clocks measured that gap directly: 86 of 86 reads stale for a fast-clock writer and a slow-clock reader with no causal path.

8.3 — Counterfactual: a perfectly symmetric network.

(a) ε does not become zero. The measurement term collapses — δ/2 becomes the exact one-way delay rather than a midpoint — but ε still contains the dispersion term (drift since the last poll), the resolution of the timestamping itself, and the reference clock's own uncertainty. ε becomes the dispersion term alone, which with frequent polling and hardware timestamping could be tens of microseconds. Smaller by orders of magnitude; still not zero, and still something the API must return.

(b) Unnecessary: v7 (transparent/boundary clocks) — its entire job was recovering the delay split, which the fabric now guarantees. v8's motivation weakens but does not vanish: a symmetric network tells you the delay, not the time, so you still need a reference clock somewhere, and still need it to be traceable to UTC. v1, v3, v4, v5, v6 all survive unchanged — you still need round trips, falseticker rejection, monotonicity, frequency correction, and low-jitter timestamps. v2's min-filter matters less for the bound and still helps with jitter.

(c) Commit wait = 2ε where ε is now the dispersion term — tens of microseconds rather than milliseconds. Notably, this is roughly what a PTP Hardware Clock already achieves today without changing the network, which tells you something about which rung of the ladder was actually load-bearing.

(d) What is still true — the interesting half of the answer:

  • The interval API argument is unchanged. Dispersion is still real, so a scalar now() still lies; TrueTime, ClockBound and fbclock would still have had to be invented.
  • Falsetickers still exist. A symmetric network does not make sources honest.
  • Steps still break durations; monotonic vs wall clock is untouched.
  • Leap seconds are unaffected — they are a definitional problem, not a network one.
  • §12.6's lease problem is smaller in magnitude and identical in kind: a paused process still does not know it was paused.
  • And the deepest one: physical time still cannot express causality. Every reason hybrid-logical-clocks exists survives this counterfactual completely intact.

The lesson: the network asymmetry is the most famous obstacle, and it is not the most important one. Most of this lab is not about the network at all.

8.4 — auditing "reliable to within 10 ms".

What to check first — and why the obvious first check is wrong.

The obvious first check is "is the NTP daemon synchronised?" — ntpstat, chronyc tracking | grep Leap, "are all hosts green?". It is the wrong first check because it returns green on both of §10.2's hosts, one of which has ε = 86 µs and one of which has ε = 18.2 ms. It cannot distinguish a fleet that satisfies the design document from one that violates it by a factor of two. A check that cannot fail in the interesting case is not a check.

The right first measurement: collect the three terms — System time, Root delay, Root dispersion — from every host, and compute ε per host. Then look at the distribution, not the mean. The design document's claim is a statement about the worst host, not the median one.

Then, in order:

  1. The tail. The p99.9 host, and specifically hosts in a different region, different provider, or different network segment. Fleet-wide averages hide exactly the hosts that will break you.
  2. Source diversity. How many hosts share a single upstream? A common upstream is a correlated failure: 6.1's scenario applied to the whole fleet at once, and it will not show up in any per-host metric.
  3. Asymmetry. Compare the offset between two sites measured from each side. A fixed, opposite-signed disagreement is §12.2, and it is the one condition that no health indicator will ever show you.
  4. What consumes the assumption. Grep for cross-machine timestamp comparison — ordering, lease expiry checks, rate-limit buckets, dedup windows. This is the list that determines whether the answer matters.

If the sentence is true (ε comfortably under 5 ms fleet-wide including the tail): write the assumption down as a measured number with a date, add the alert from §15 so it stays true, and note explicitly that it is conditional on the monitoring existing. An unmonitored true assumption becomes a false one silently.

If the sentence is false: do not open with "the clocks are wrong". Open with the list from step 4 — the designs that actually depend on it — because that is the list that carries the cost. Then, for each, choose one of three: shrink ε (often a one-line config change to a local time service — start here), widen the design's margin, or replace the timestamp comparison with a fencing token or a sequencer.

The deliverable is a measured number and a list of dependent designs — not a config change. The config change is the easy part, and it is not what the week was for.
