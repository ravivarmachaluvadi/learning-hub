Lab 14 · One Machine, Done Right

TLS — how Nikhil sends a secret to a bank he has never met, and knows it is the bank

Tell a friend: Nikhil wants to send a secret instruction to Meera at his bank, and every courier in between is a stranger who can read the letter or forge a reply on bank letterhead. Two strangers who have never met can agree on a secret code over a wire everyone can read; that turns out to be the easy half. The hard half is Nikhil proving he reached Meera and not Omar, a courier answering in her name. TLS solves it by letting a notary Nikhil already trusts vouch for the bank, in writing he can check by himself, before he sends a word. So the padlock in your browser only means "encrypted to whoever answered"; the notary's seal, checked against a list you chose in advance, is what says who answered.

Watch the two guarantees arrive separately in visualization.html, published as The Padlock And The Seal. How this lab was built, and where it sits among your other labs, is in section 19 at the end.

BEGINNER MODEL

1. Why does this problem exist at all?

In one breath: Everything you send across a network passes through machines other people own. Each of them can read it, and each of them can change it. Worse, one of them can answer as the place you meant to reach.

Everything you send across a network passes through machines you do not own: your router, your ISP, a dozen routers in between, whoever runs the coffee shop wifi. By default every one of them can read what passes. And, this is the part people forget, every one of them can change it.

Without a fix, passwords, card numbers and API tokens travel in plain text. Worse, anyone in the middle can quietly pretend to be the server you meant to reach. The cost of getting this wrong is not "slightly less privacy". It is handing your credentials to someone else while your code reports complete success.

Hold on to that last sentence. Your code reports success. Nothing throws. That is the shape of every serious failure in this lab. It is why the story that follows is about a forged reply, not a stolen letter.

2. Nikhil and the sealed envelope: who is he really talking to?

In one breath: Nikhil has to send a secret to a bank clerk he has never met, through couriers he cannot trust. A stronger envelope stops them reading it. It does nothing if the courier is the one who answers.

Nikhil needs to send his bank a signed instruction. The person who should receive it is Meera, a clerk at the bank's counter. Nikhil has never met Meera, or anyone else at the bank. And every courier between his door and hers is a stranger.

His first worry is obvious: any courier could open the envelope and read it.

His second worry is worse and less obvious. One of the couriers, call him Omar, does not deliver the letter to Meera at all. Instead, Omar writes back on bank letterhead, in a convincing hand, asking Nikhil to confirm his account number. Nikhil replies. Omar now has everything.

Notice that a stronger envelope does nothing about Omar. Nikhil could seal his letter in a steel box, and he would still be sealing it to the wrong person. Privacy and knowing-who-you-are-talking-to are two different problems. Solving one does not touch the other.

And there is a third problem hiding underneath. To use any kind of secret code with Meera, the two of them need to agree on the code first. But every message they could use to agree on it travels through the same untrusted couriers. Through Omar.

3. Why not just agree a password beforehand?

In one breath: If Nikhil and Meera shared a password, they could scramble every letter with it. That is how secrets travelled for most of history. It only works if the two of them can meet first.

The obvious answer, and the naive solution: Nikhil and Meera agree on a shared password in advance, and scramble every letter with it. Omar opens the envelope and sees gibberish. Problem solved.

This is genuinely how secret communication worked for most of history. For two parties who can meet in advance, it is fine. Spies did it with code books. Banks did it with couriers they had vetted in person.

🔮 Predict: Nikhil has never met Meera. Could he just post her a password today, and use it from tomorrow?

Reveal No. The password travels through Omar, who copies it. From tomorrow, Omar reads every "scrambled" letter as easily as Meera does. If you said "post it in a sealed envelope", ask what seals that envelope. It would need a secret too, and you are back where you started. Section 6 has a trick that does not need a secret to travel at all.

4. Why can't you meet a website in advance?

In one breath: Two things break. You cannot agree the password, because everything you send is readable. And even if you could, ten million customers would need ten million passwords.

Two things break, and the second one breaks harder.

You cannot agree on the password. You have never met your bank's server. To agree on a shared secret you must send something, and everything you send is readable by the couriers. Whatever you use to establish the password, the eavesdropper sees it too. So the eavesdropper can derive the same password.

And it does not scale even if you could. A bank with ten million customers needs ten million pre-agreed passwords, distributed somehow, kept secret forever. The web has billions of visitor–site pairs. There is no meeting.

Fact Number What it means
Customers at one bank ten million ten million secrets to hand out and guard forever
Visitor–site pairs on the web billions there is no meeting, and there never can be one

🔮 Predict: Suppose we somehow solved the first problem. Nikhil and whoever is at the other end can establish a shared secret key over a public wire, with no prior meeting. Are we done? Is the conversation now safe?

Reveal

No, and this is the single most important idea in the lab.

You have established a secret key with whoever answered. Nothing in that process said who that was. If Omar intercepted Nikhil's very first message and answered it himself, Nikhil has now established a perfectly secure, unbreakable channel. To Omar.

If you said "yes, done", you are treating a locked door as proof of whose building it is on. Encryption without identity is a locked door on a building you cannot identify. You have privacy from everyone except the one person who matters.

Experiment 3 in this lab does exactly this. It connects to an impostor with full TLS 1.3 encryption, cipher TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384, and hands over a password. The connection is flawless. It is also completely compromised, and nothing in the protocol complains.

In one breath: Three separate things: nobody reads it, nobody changes it, and Nikhil knows it is Meera before he writes a word. The third is the whole subject.

Three things, and they are separate. Engineers would call this list the real requirement. Watch how each one maps onto Nikhil's worries from section 2.

6. What is the key idea?

In one breath: Strangers can build a shared secret by each sending a public half that only combines correctly with the other's private half. And identity comes from a notary Nikhil already trusts, who vouches for the bank in writing he can check.

Two ideas, and the second is the one people underestimate.

First: two strangers can agree on a shared secret over a public wire. Each side sends a public value. Each combines the value it received with a private value it never sends. Both arrive at the same secret. The eavesdropper, holding only the two public values, cannot. Omar can copy every note that passes and still not get the secret.

Second: identity is solved by delegating trust in advance. Nikhil cannot know every bank clerk in the country. But he can decide, ahead of time, to believe a small number of authorities. Say there is a notary, Asha, whose seal Nikhil knows and can recognise. Asha can vouch, in writing Nikhil can verify, for any number of clerks Nikhil has never seen. Meera included.

That is the whole trick. You cannot know every server. You can decide, ahead of time, to believe a handful of authorities. They can vouch, in writing you can check, for any number of servers you have never seen.

7. What do engineers call the things Nikhil just met?

In one breath: Every word in this section names something that already happened in Nikhil's story. Here is each one, earned where it happened, then two small maps back to the story.

The terminology, in the order the story earned it. Each word is a thing Nikhil already touched. The two tables at the end of the section map every word back to where it happened.

One key, or a pair of keys?

The password from section 3 was one key that both locks and unlocks. Engineers call that a symmetric key. The real algorithm behind it is AES, and it is fast.

The trick from section 6 used a pair instead. One half is shown to everyone. The other half never leaves your hands. The two are mathematically linked: what one does, only the other can undo. Engineers call that an asymmetric keypair, made of a public key and a private key. The mathematics is slow.

Why does TLS need both? Because it uses them for different jobs. The slow asymmetric mathematics runs once, during the opening conversation, to establish trust and agree a key. The fast symmetric encryption then protects the actual data. In experiment 1 the two sides agreed on a cipher whose name you will see often in this lab, TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384. Read the middle of it: AES-256 is the symmetric part, doing the real work.

How do two strangers end up with the same secret?

The "each sends a public half" step from section 6 is called key exchange. Each side generates a keypair, sends only the public half, and combines its own private half with the other side's public half. Both arrive at the same value. An observer holding both public halves cannot.

The term exists to name the step that solves section 4's first failure, the one about agreeing a password. It is also there to separate that step from authentication, which key exchange does not do. Omar can run a key exchange with Nikhil just as well as Meera can. The certificates in this lab do their key mathematics on an elliptic curve named secp256r1.

What is the letter of introduction?

Asha's written vouching from section 6 is a certificate: a document saying "this name belongs to the holder of this public key", signed by someone else. Precisely, it is an X.509 structure that binds a public key to one or more names, with a validity period, signed by an issuer.

The term exists to make identity transferable. Because the document is signed, it can be handed to strangers and still be checked. Here is the one the lab's good server, Meera's stand-in, presents. It lives in pki/good.p12.

Field Value in pki/good.p12 In words

Who is the notary, and what is the chain?

Asha is a certificate authority, usually shortened to CA: one of the small set of organisations you decided in advance to believe. A CA holds a private key and signs certificates.

The paperwork linking a server back to a CA is the chain of trust. It runs from the server's certificate, up through any intermediate certificates, to a root that is already on your list. The point is that you need to trust a handful of authorities rather than millions of servers. This is the delegation from section 6, made mechanical.

Experiment 1 prints the lab's chain. It has two entries: [0] CN=localhost, issued by [1] CN=TLS Lab Root CA. The second one is self-signed, and it is in our trust store. Which brings us to the trust store.

Which notaries does Nikhil recognise?

The list of authorities Nikhil is willing to believe is his trust store. In engineering terms it is a set of root certificates. Your operating system and your JDK each ship one, containing roughly 150 commercial CAs. The JDK's is a file called cacerts.

This list matters more than any other configuration in TLS. It is the only thing deciding whose word you accept. Everything about identity reduces to this list. Experiment 3 changes nothing except this list, and turns a rejected impostor into a successful one.

What is the opening conversation?

The negotiation that sets everything up is the handshake. In it the two sides agree a version and a cipher, do the key exchange, present and verify certificates, and check that nobody tampered with the negotiation itself.

You already paid for one. In the networking lab, the handshake to cloudflare.com cost 46 ms on top of a 13 ms TCP connect. That is roughly one extra round trip plus certificate work.

Is the letter about the bank Nikhil actually wrote to?

Asha's seal proves the letter is genuine. It does not prove the letter is about Meera. Nikhil must also read the name on it. Engineers call that check hostname verification: matching the name you connected to against the certificate's Subject Alternative Name list, the SAN list for short.

Why is it a separate check, and separately forgettable? Because a valid signature proves the certificate is genuine, not that it is genuine for this site. Without this check, any certificate a trusted CA ever issued would work for every site. Someone who legitimately owns other.example could impersonate your bank.

The lab shows this with a certificate called wronghost. Ask it for localhost and it is rejected: No subject alternative DNS name matching localhost found. Ask the same certificate for other.example and it is accepted. Nothing about the certificate changed. Only the name you requested did.

In Java this check is opt-in on a raw socket. That is why it is the second most common way to disable TLS security by accident. Failure 6 in section 11 is that accident.

What if Meera's key is stolen next year?

Suppose the bank's long-term key is stolen tomorrow. Can today's recorded letters be read? The property that says no is forward secrecy.

It works because the session key comes from an ephemeral key exchange, one whose keypairs are thrown away after the connection. The certificate's private key never encrypts data. It only signs. The term exists because an attacker who records everything now and steals a key later would otherwise decrypt years of history in one go.

TLS 1.3 makes this mandatory: every cipher suite is ephemeral. In TLS 1.2 it was optional, which is why you see ECDHE in the names of the good 1.2 suites. Section 10 shows the design that lacked it, and what that costs.

8. What two pictures should you keep in your head?

In one breath: The simple picture is a letter of introduction with a notary's seal. The engineering picture is the same thing with every step named. The story lies to you in three places, and each lie is a real failure.

Two mental models, one simple and one precise, and then the places where the simple one lies.

The simple picture: the notarised letter. Nikhil does not know Meera. But he knows Asha the notary, whose seal he can recognise and which is hard to forge. Meera shows Nikhil a letter of introduction bearing Asha's seal, naming the bank. Nikhil checks the seal. He checks that the letter names the bank he actually wrote to. He checks the date on it. Then they talk.

The engineering picture. The same events, with the engineer's names, one step per sentence. A negotiation produces a symmetric session key via ephemeral key exchange. The server then proves control of a private key. The public half of that key appears in a certificate. That certificate chains to a root in your trust store. The name you requested is checked against that certificate's SAN list. And the whole transcript is signed, so the negotiation itself cannot be tampered with.

🔮 Predict: Omar gets hold of a photocopy of Meera's letter of introduction, seal and all. Certificates are public; anyone can copy one. Can Omar now pass as the bank?

Reveal No. The letter is public. What Omar does not have is Meera's *private key*. The handshake makes the server sign something fresh, for this conversation only, and Omar cannot produce that signature. If you said "yes, he has the sealed letter", you are trusting paperwork. TLS trusts *possession*, and the first lie below is exactly this.

Where the story lies to you. Carry these three corrections with it.

A seal proves the letter's origin; a certificate proves nothing on its own. Anyone can copy a certificate. They are public, and your browser shows you every one. What cannot be copied is the private key. That is why the handshake makes the server sign something fresh. Possession, not paperwork, is the proof. A notary analogy quietly skips this, and it is the step that stops replay.

A notary can be phoned; a CA cannot. Verification is entirely offline. You check a signature with a key you already have. Nothing contacts the CA. This is why a revoked certificate is so much harder to deal with than an expired one (failure 7). Expiry is self-announcing. Revocation needs someone to be asked.

There is no honest analogy for "you chose the notaries in advance, and someone else may have added to that list." Your trust store was populated by your OS vendor, your JDK, and possibly your employer's laptop management. Anyone who can add an entry can impersonate anything, undetectably. That is experiment 3. It has no equivalent in the letter story, which is exactly why the analogy must be retired before section 10.

INTERMEDIATE MODEL

10. What does the handshake actually do, message by message?

In one breath: The first obvious design encrypted the session key with the bank's long-term key, so one stolen key would unlock years of recordings. The real handshake builds a throwaway key first, then proves identity inside the sealed envelope, in one round trip.

Mechanism v1: the obvious design, and its fatal flaw

A first attempt, which is roughly what SSL looked like early on. Nikhil and Meera, in four steps:

  1. Nikhil says hello.
  2. Meera sends her certificate, containing her public key.
  3. Nikhil invents a random session key, encrypts it with Meera's public key, and sends it.
  4. Only Meera can decrypt it. Both now share a key. Encrypt everything.

This works. It authenticates the server, because only the real key holder can decrypt step 3. It establishes a shared secret. And it is simple.

🔮 Predict: Omar records this entire conversation today but cannot read it. Three years later, the bank's server is decommissioned and its private key leaks. What can Omar do with the recording?

Reveal

Decrypt all of it. Every session, retroactively.

In step 3 the session key was encrypted with the server's long-term public key. Anyone holding the matching private key can decrypt that message and recover the session key. Years later, for every conversation ever recorded.

If you said "nothing, those sessions are over", you are picturing the key as something that dies with the connection. In v1 it does not. It was mailed, and the mail was kept.

One key compromise retroactively breaks all past traffic. This is why v1 is gone. TLS 1.3 removed this key-exchange style entirely, and it is what forward secrecy means. The fix is that the session key must never travel, even encrypted. Instead both sides derive it from ephemeral keypairs thrown away when the connection ends. The certificate's private key then only ever signs, never encrypts. Stealing it lets you impersonate the server in future but not read the past.

Mechanism v2: ephemeral key exchange, and the handshake as it runs

Here is a real TLS 1.3 handshake. The third column is Nikhil's version of each message. The last two columns are the ones that matter. They are why the visualization has two lamps rather than one.

2 ServerHello: chosen version and cipher, my key share "we will use this code; here is my public half" yes, from here no

Two things to take from that table.

Steps 3 and 4 are already encrypted. So an eavesdropper on TLS 1.3 cannot even see which certificate the server presented. In TLS 1.2 they could. The certificate went in the clear. That is a real privacy improvement, not just a speed one.

Step 4 is what makes step 3 mean anything. A certificate is public; anyone can copy one. The signature over the transcript is what proves this server holds the matching private key right now, for this conversation. It is Meera signing in front of Nikhil, which the photocopy in section 8 could never do.

🔮 Predict: Omar sits on the wire and records everything. Which is the first message he cannot read?

Reveal Message 3, the Certificate. After message 2 both sides hold the key, so everything from 3 onwards is sealed. If you said "message 1, because it carries the key share", remember that a key share is the *public* half. It is designed to be seen. Omar holding both public halves still cannot build the secret.

Why is TLS 1.3 one round trip when 1.2 was two?

The saving comes from guessing. In TLS 1.2 the client had to learn which key exchange the server chose before it could send its key material. That is a full extra round trip. TLS 1.3 has the client send a key share in its very first message, guessing the server will accept it. It nearly always does.

Round trip time What the guess saves per new connection What it means
16 ms, the figure timed in the networking lab 16 ms one whole round trip gone, every time
250 ms, a far-away region 250 ms the same saving, now a quarter of a second

0-RTT goes further. On a repeat visit, the client sends application data with its first message, using a key from the previous session. That costs nothing in round trips and buys a real problem. Failure 8 is that problem.

🔮 Predict: Every message from step 3 onwards is encrypted, including the certificate. So Omar sees nothing but noise after step 2. What can he still learn about your traffic?

Reveal

Who you are talking to. Because you told him in step 1.

The ClientHello carries the hostname in plain text, in a field called SNI, short for Server Name Indication. It has to. One IP address may serve thousands of sites, so the server must know which certificate to present before it can encrypt anything.

If you said "nothing at all", you forgot the envelope has an address on the outside. Experiment 4 points a TLS client at a plain listener and reads its first 456 bytes. The hostname payments.internal.example appears in readable ASCII at byte offset 153.

| What Omar reads | Where | What it means | |---|---|---| | payments.internal.example | byte 153 of the first 456 | the name of every service you talk to | | the IP address, the timing, the size of everything | the outside of every packet | that the conversation happened, when, and how big it was |

TLS hides the contents of your conversation. It does not hide that the conversation happened, who it was with, when, or how big it was.

ENGINEERING MODEL

11. What are the eight ways it fails, and how do you tell them apart?

In one breath: Four failures are Nikhil correctly refusing a bad letter. Two are Nikhil accepting a bad one because someone changed his rules. Two are problems no lab can stage. All the paperwork ones throw the same exception, so the cause underneath it is the diagnosis.

Eight failure scenarios. All the certificate ones surface as SSLHandshakeException, so the cause chain, the exception's chain of underlying causes, is what tells them apart. Each has a different owner. The stories come first; the comparison table at the end of the section holds the shared facts.

1 — Untrusted issuer: a notary Nikhil does not recognise

Meera's letter arrives with a seal Nikhil has never seen. He refuses it. That is the right answer, and it is the first thing to notice: every guarantee was kept. The client refused correctly.

Run java TlsLab.java 2 and look at case 2, a self-signed server. Your code sees SunCertPathBuilderException: unable to find valid certification path to requested target. In words: I tried to build a chain from this certificate to something I trust, and there is no such path. Nothing in your trust store signed this certificate.

The owner is usually you: a private CA that was never added to the trust store, or a server that skipped its intermediate certificate. The tell is the words "certification path". In production the most common cause is a missing intermediate, not a missing root. The server was misconfigured to send only its leaf.

2 — Hostname mismatch: a genuine letter about somebody else

The seal is real, the date is fine, and the letter names a different bank. Nikhil refuses. Again, correctly.

Run java TlsLab.java 2 and look at case 3. Your code sees No subject alternative DNS name matching localhost found. The certificate is genuine, correctly signed and in date, for a different name. The owner is whoever chose the URL, or whoever requested the certificate's SAN list.

The identical certificate is accepted when you ask for other.example. The tell is the words "no name matching" or "subject alternative".

3 — Expired: the date on the letter has passed

Nothing about trust or identity is wrong. A date passed. Run java TlsLab.java 2 and look at case 4: CertificateExpiredException: NotAfter: Fri Jul 24 …. The owner is the server team's renewal process.

In production this is the single most common TLS outage, and it is entirely self-inflicted. It is also why ACME and Let's Encrypt automation exist.

Watch out for one trap. An expired certificate also fails path building, and also mentions PKIX in its message. A diagnostic that tests for PKIX first will misreport it as an untrusted issuer and send you to the wrong team. The author of TlsLab.verify() made exactly that mistake while building it, and the fix is a comment in the code.

4 — Clock skew: Nikhil's own calendar is wrong

Set your machine's clock forward two years. Every letter now looks expired. Your code sees CertificateExpiredException, or CertificateNotYetValidException if the clock is behind instead. Validity is checked against your clock, so a wrong clock invalidates perfectly good certificates.

🔮 Predict: Nikhil's letters from three different banks all start being refused on the same morning. Whose problem is it?

Reveal Nikhil's. Three banks do not let their letters expire on the same day. The tell for clock skew is that it fails on **every** TLS connection, to every host, all at once. If you said "the banks", check the scope first: one host is a certificate problem, every host at once is a client problem.

In production this is classic on devices with dead batteries, fresh containers with no NTP, and CI runners.

5 — The trust store is not what you think it is

Somebody added Omar's own notary to Nikhil's list of recognised seals. Now Omar's letter passes every check. This is the one failure where Nikhil is wrong and nothing tells him.

Run java TlsLab.java 3, the impostor experiment. With the interceptor's CA trusted, an impostor gets a perfect TLS 1.3 connection and reads the password. No error, no warning, no clue. Except the issuer name, which nothing checks.

Why: trust is a list, and anyone who can write to that list can impersonate anything. The guarantee lost is identity, completely and silently. Privacy is maintained, which is what makes it so hard to notice. In production this is exactly how corporate TLS inspection works. It is also what a compromised CA gets you: the 2011 DigiNotar breach is the canonical case.

6 — Hostname verification silently switched off

Nikhil stops reading the name on the letter. Any genuine letter, about any bank, now passes.

Delete p.setEndpointIdentificationAlgorithm("HTTPS") from TlsLab.connect(). The wronghost certificate is now accepted. The chain still verifies; only the name check is gone.

🔮 Predict: You delete that one line and re-run all four cases of experiment 2. How many verdicts change?

Reveal One. Only the wrong-name case flips to accepted. The self-signed case still fails path building and the expired case still fails on the date, because those are separate checks. If you said "all of them", you are picturing one big "is it valid?" switch. There are four checks, and this line owns exactly one of them.

Why: on a raw SSLSocket in Java, hostname verification is opt-in. HttpsURLConnection and HttpClient do it for you; a hand-rolled socket does not. The guarantee lost: any certificate your trusted CAs ever issued now works for any host. The tell is nothing. It fails open, the most dangerous kind of default.

7 — Revoked, but still accepted

Asha announces that one of her letters was stolen and must not be honoured. Nobody phones Asha, so nobody hears. This lab cannot stage this one; section 19 says why.

What happens: a certificate whose private key was stolen is revoked by its CA, but clients keep accepting it. Verification is offline (section 8), and revocation checking is frequently disabled, soft-failing, or unreachable.

Why: revocation lists, CRLs, are large. The alternative, OCSP, adds a network call to every handshake. That call must not become an availability dependency, so it usually soft-fails. The response in production is short-lived certificates, 90 days, or hours in a mesh, rather than reliable revocation. Expiry is the revocation mechanism that actually works.

8 — 0-RTT replay

Nikhil, in a hurry, puts his instruction in the very first envelope, sealed with last week's key. Omar copies the envelope and posts it again. The bank carries out the instruction twice. This lab cannot stage this one either.

What happens: TLS 1.3's 0-RTT lets a client send data with its first message. An attacker who captures that data can send it again, and the server cannot tell the difference. Why: the anti-replay protection of the full handshake has not happened yet.

The response: only ever send idempotent requests in 0-RTT, requests that are harmless to repeat. This is the same rule your communication-protocols lab established for retries, arriving from a completely different direction.

12. What does TLS actually promise Nikhil, and what does it not?

In one breath: After a good handshake, nobody reads, nobody changes, and it really is the bank. TLS does not promise the bank is honest, that your notary list is honest, or that nobody can tell who you wrote to.

This is the correctness section: what TLS actually guarantees, and what it does not.

Guaranteed, once the handshake completes successfully. Each row is one of Nikhil's worries, answered.

15. What changes when Nikhil's bank is a real system?

In one breath: A real system replaces Asha with a public notary, opens the sealed envelope at a load balancer rather than at Meera's desk, and spends most of its effort renewing letters before they expire.

From this lab to a real deployment

This lab runs a JDK TLS client and server on loopback with a private CA. Asha is certs.sh; Meera is TlsServer.java.

A real deployment replaces the private CA with a public one (via ACME) or an internal PKI. It usually terminates TLS somewhere other than your application: a load balancer, an ingress controller, or a service-mesh sidecar. That termination point is where the guarantees actually end. Traffic behind it may well be plaintext. In the story, the sealed envelope is opened at the bank's front door, and the letter walks to Meera's desk unsealed.

Production is mostly certificate lifecycle: issuing, deploying, rotating before expiry, and revoking. Failure 3 is not an edge case. It is the recurring cost of the whole scheme.

What does mTLS buy a service mesh?

Experiment 6 is the whole idea behind "mTLS everywhere". Each service gets a short-lived certificate, and every connection authenticates both ends at the transport. Every service is both Nikhil and Meera at once. The identity arrives before any application code runs, and there is no shared secret to leak.

The cost is a certificate lifecycle for every workload. That is why this is almost always a sidecar's job rather than your code's.

19. About this lab

What is this lab made of, where do its numbers come from, and what could it not do? Everything about the lab itself lives here, so that Nikhil's story above can run uninterrupted.

What it is made of. This README; visualization.html, a handshake stepper, also published at claude.ai/code/artifact/c9e77989; certs.sh + TlsLab.java + TlsServer.java, six runnable experiments; BrokenTrust.java, a planted bug; EXERCISES.md; SOLUTIONS.md.

Nothing in this lab is simulated. These are real handshakes between the JDK's TLS client and the JDK's TLS server, using certificates keytool actually issued. Every rejection in this document was produced by the same code path that protects your production traffic. Timings vary between runs; treat quoted milliseconds as one representative run.

Where this sits relative to what you already know. In the networking-for-developers lab you measured the TLS handshake as one opaque phase: 46 ms to cloudflare.com on top of a 13 ms TCP connect. You learned to recognise SSLHandshakeException as "the connection succeeded, so this is not a connectivity problem." This lab opens that box. It assumes you already have: TCP handshakes, round trips, connection reuse, and the diagnostic habit of reading a failure by its symptom. It does not reteach them.

How the numbers were produced. Every figure in sections 7, 10 and 14 came from running the experiments on this machine. Both directions of the hostname check were run, not inferred: the wronghost certificate was rejected when asked for localhost and accepted when asked for other.example. The experiment 5 timings are from a warmed JVM; the unwarmed run's 8.6× "speedup" is reported so that you recognise the trap, not as a result. The SNI offset of 153 bytes in a 456-byte first message is what experiment 4 printed. Every measurement is from one machine on one JDK. The ratios transfer; the microseconds do not.

Honest limits. The certificates, the four checks, the exception text and all six experiments are real and were executed. Failures 7 and 8, revocation and 0-RTT replay, are described but not reproduced. Both need infrastructure this lab does not have: a CA that publishes revocations, and a server that accepts early data. The visualization's message sequence is faithful in ordering and in when each guarantee arrives, but simplified: the key schedule, extensions and the alert protocol are omitted.

The four checks, by number. The stepper and the exercises refer to the identity checks as check 1 to 4. In order: the chain reaches a trusted authority; the certificate is issued for the name asked for; it is in date; the server holds the matching private key. BrokenTrust.java disables check 1 (and, because the same TrustManager runs the date check, effectively check 3). Removing the HTTPS line disables check 2.

Solutions

Every rung-4 and rung-6 answer below was produced by running the modification described, against real TLS. Rungs 7–8 are worked design arguments, not single correct outputs.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 "Encrypted with AES-256" establishes that nobody on the network path can read the traffic. It establishes nothing about who is on the other end.

The concrete scenario is experiment 3, and it is not hypothetical. An impostor holds a certificate saying CN=localhost, signed by its own authority. If that authority is in your trust store, you get a flawless TLSv1.3 connection with TLS_AES_256_GCM_SHA384 — and you hand it your password. Every word of "it's encrypted with AES-256" is true, and the situation is a total compromise.

1.2 A certificate is a document saying "this name belongs to whoever holds this key." It needs someone else's signature because otherwise anyone could write one about anyone — a claim about yourself proves nothing.

You can check the signature offline because signature verification only needs the signer's public key, which you already have: it is in your trust store, put there before you ever made the connection.

1.3 The padlock proves: the traffic is encrypted and unmodified, and the server holds the private key for a certificate covering the name in the address bar, issued by an authority your browser trusts.

Two things people wrongly assume it proves:

  • That the site is honest. paypa1.com can have a perfectly valid certificate. TLS proves identity, not virtue.
  • That nobody is reading it. If a proxy's CA is installed on the machine (corporate laptops, very commonly), the padlock shows while the proxy reads everything.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 Privacy turns on at step 2 (ServerHello — both sides can now derive the key). Identity turns on at step 5 (after the client verifies the chain).

They cannot coincide because the certificate is sent encrypted, at steps 3–4. Encryption must come first for the certificate to be private at all. So there is necessarily a window — steps 2 to 5 — where you have a perfectly private channel to a party you have not identified. That window is the entire subject of the lab.

2.2 In TLS 1.2: (a) there are more messages — two round trips instead of one, and (b) the certificate travels unencrypted. The second is the privacy improvement in 1.3: an eavesdropper on 1.2 sees exactly which certificate the server presented; on 1.3 they see nothing after the ServerHello.

2.3 Check 1 fails — the chain does not reach a trusted authority. Checks 2, 3 and 4 are never reached.

Order matters for two reasons. Practically, once you have established that the document is not genuine there is no point examining its contents — the name and the dates in a forged certificate mean nothing. Diagnostically, it means the first failure is the one reported, so a report of "untrusted issuer" tells you nothing about whether the name or dates were also wrong.

2.4 Entry [1], CN=TLS Lab Root CA. You know because it is self-signed — its subject and issuer are identical, so nothing else vouches for it. A self-signed certificate is only meaningful if you already had it, and you did: it is in truststore.p12.

2.5 In TlsLab.connect():

p.setEndpointIdentificationAlgorithm("HTTPS");

It enables hostname verification — checking the certificate's SAN list against the name you connected to. Without it, the signature is still checked but the name never is.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 Asked for localhost → rejected: No subject alternative DNS name matching localhost found. Asked for other.example → accepted. Verified by running both. The certificate is byte-for-byte identical in each case; only the question changed. That is what hostname verification is.

3.2 Both lamps go green. The connection is fully encrypted, and an eavesdropper on the wire sees nothing useful — the impostor's TLS is genuine. The compromise is not on the wire; it is that the far end is the wrong party. This is the point people miss: a MITM with a trusted CA is invisible to network inspection, because there is nothing wrong with the network traffic.

3.3 With the good server, switching to "trust everything" changes nothing visible — it connects, exactly as before. With the impostor, it also connects, which is the disaster.

That asymmetry is precisely why the bug survives review: in every environment where things are configured correctly, the broken code and the correct code behave identically. Tests pass, staging works, production works. It only differs on the day it matters, and then it fails open.

3.4 It will be found, because the protocol has no alternative. One IP address can serve thousands of sites, so the server must know which certificate to present before any key exists. The hostname therefore has to travel in the first message, in the clear. Measured: found at byte offset 153 of 456.

3.5 Check 3 (validity dates) fails, for every server you connect to, with CertificateExpiredException — because a clock two years fast makes every certificate look expired. The give-away is the scope: a certificate problem affects one host, a clock problem affects all of them simultaneously.

Rung 4 — Modify (all measured)

4.1 — Removing setEndpointIdentificationAlgorithm("HTTPS")

Case Before After
correct name, trusted CA ACCEPTED ACCEPTED
self-signed REJECTED (path) REJECTED (path)
issued for other.example REJECTED ACCEPTED — "identity proved: CN=other.example"
expired REJECTED (expiry) REJECTED (expiry)

Only the hostname case changes; chain-building and expiry are still enforced, because they are separate checks.

The guarantee destroyed: any certificate any trusted CA ever issued now works for any host. With a public CA in your trust store that means anyone who can obtain a certificate for a domain they legitimately own — a few minutes and no money — can impersonate your bank to your client. The output printing "identity proved: CN=other.example" while we asked for localhost says it perfectly.

4.2 — good certificate with SAN dns:localhost only

Asked for Result
localhost ACCEPTED
127.0.0.1 REJECTED: No subject alternative names matching IP address 127.0.0.1 found

Nothing "breaks" — the certificate is completely valid. The SAN list is not a description of the server, it is an exhaustive list of the names this certificate may be used for, and an IP address is a different kind of entry from a DNS name. localhost and 127.0.0.1 point at the same machine and are still two distinct identities as far as TLS is concerned.

This is the real cause of a very common production surprise: health checks that hit a pod by IP fail while the same service works fine by name.

4.3 — -startdate +30d instead of -400d

Measured: CertificateNotYetValidException: NotBefore: Sun Sep 27 …

A different exception, because the failure is the opposite direction on the same check. Real-world equivalents: a certificate issued in advance and deployed too early, or — far more often — a client whose clock is behind. That second case is why this exception is worth recognising: it usually means the problem is on your machine, not the server's.

4.4 — setNeedClientAuth → setWantClientAuth

Measured: case A (no client certificate) now succeeds and receives secret-balance:42.

  • need — the server requires a client certificate and fails the handshake without one.
  • want — the server asks, accepts whatever comes, and continues regardless.

"Want" is a trap if you read it as "prefer": it provides no enforcement whatsoever. It is the right choice only when you use the certificate for optional purposes — attribution, logging, gradual rollout — and enforce authorisation elsewhere. Any time the certificate is what authorises the call, it must be "need".

The migration path is real, though: enable "want" first, confirm from logs that every legitimate caller is presenting a certificate, then switch to "need".

4.5 — An empty trust store

Measured: InvalidAlgorithmParameterException: the trustAnchors parameter must be non-empty.

A different failure, at a different stage. The self-signed case fails during path validation — the machinery ran and found no route to a trusted root. An empty trust store fails at initialization: the validator refuses to start, because "verify against nothing" is not a coherent request.

Worth noticing what this rules out: TLS has no "trust nothing, accept everything" mode. Getting that behaviour requires deliberately writing a TrustManager that does nothing — which is exactly BrokenTrust.java. The default fails closed; only code can make it fail open.

Rung 5 — Implement

5.1 The distinction to get right: a diagnostic tool that deliberately does not validate is safe because it never sends anything. It looks and reports. The same non-validating code inside an HTTP client is failure 6 because that client then transmits credentials over the unverified connection. The danger is not "skipped validation", it is "skipped validation followed by trusting the result".

5.2 Pin the public key, not the certificate — a certificate is renewed routinely and its bytes change, while the key can be kept across renewals.

What breaks on rotation: everything, instantly and completely, if the new key was not pinned in advance. The standard mitigation is a backup pin — pin two keys, keep the second one offline and unused until you need it. Pinning without a backup pin has bricked real applications, which is why browsers abandoned HTTP Public Key Pinning entirely.

5.3 The subtlety worth handling: check the expiry of the whole chain, not just the leaf. An intermediate expiring takes you down exactly as hard, and it is the one nobody is watching.

Rung 6 — Debug

6.1 Measured output:

CHECK 1  connects_to_the_real_server
         server replied : secret-balance:42 (you sent: my-password)   -> PASS

CHECK 2  refuses_an_impostor
         impostor cert  : CN=localhost, signed by "Interceptor Root CA"
         we sent        : my-password
         outcome        : CONNECTED — impostor replied: secret-balance:42 ...
         -> FAIL  (the password was handed to an impostor, over a perfectly
                   encrypted channel)
  • Which check fails: check 2. The client connected to an impostor and sent my-password, which the impostor received in full.
  • Which method: checkServerTrusted. Its body is empty, and an empty body is a silent success — the contract is "throw a CertificateException if the certificate is unacceptable", so doing nothing means accepting everything. There is no compiler warning, no runtime warning, and the method looks implemented.
  • The correct fix: import the CA's certificate into a trust store and point the client at it — TlsServer.context(null, "pki/truststore.p12"), which is what every working experiment does. It is more work because it needs the CA certificate obtained, distributed to every environment, and rotated when the CA rotates. That is real operational work, and it is why the one-line "fix" is so attractive.
  • The second, independent way: omitting p.setEndpointIdentificationAlgorithm("HTTPS"). Even with a correct TrustManager, the impostor's certificate says CN=localhost — the name we asked for — so a correct trust check alone still passes here. It is only rejected because its issuer is untrusted. Had the attacker obtained a genuinely-signed certificate for any name at all, the missing hostname check would let it through. Both checks are load-bearing, and disabling either is sufficient to lose the guarantee.

6.2 The test needs something a normal unit test does not have: a second, adversarial server with a certificate the client must reject. Testing only against the real server can never catch this, because the broken client and the correct client behave identically there.

@Test void client_refuses_a_certificate_from_an_untrusted_authority() {
    try (TlsServer impostor = new TlsServer("pki/mitm.p12", false)) {
        assertThrows(SSLHandshakeException.class, () -> client.call(impostor.port()));
    }
}

The general principle: a security control can only be tested by attempting the thing it is supposed to prevent. A test that exercises the happy path tests nothing about the control.

6.3 setDefaultHostnameVerifier((h, s) -> true) disables check 2 (hostname verification) globally for HttpsURLConnection. BrokenTrust disables check 1 (chain building) — and, because the TrustManager is the thing that runs the date check too, effectively check 3 as well.

They are different bugs, and equally fatal, for the reason in 6.1: the two checks answer different questions ("is this certificate genuine?" and "is it genuine for this site?"), and an attacker only needs one of them to be missing.

setDefaultHostnameVerifier is arguably worse in one respect — the word setDefault means it applies process-wide, including to libraries that never opted in.

Rung 7 — Handle failure (worked discussions)

7.1 — Make the expiry outage impossible.

Automate issuance and renewal. ACME with cert-manager, or your internal PKI's equivalent, renewing at one-third of remaining lifetime. Short lifetimes are a feature: a 90-day certificate forces the automation to run often enough that breakage is discovered in days rather than in the one annual attempt that fails.

Monitor the outcome, not the job. Alert on days-until-expiry measured from the served certificate — connect and read what the server actually presents. A renewal job that "succeeded" while writing to a path nobody reads is the classic failure, and only the served certificate reveals it. Alert at 30 days, page at 7.

Include the whole chain, per 5.3, and include certificates you depend on but do not control: your payment provider's expiry takes you down too, and you will not be told. A daily probe of every outbound HTTPS dependency is cheap.

When automation fails, the runbook needs a manual issuance path that has been rehearsed. An untested emergency path during an outage is not a plan.

7.2 — Detect a rogue root.

Record the issuer and public-key fingerprint of every certificate your services accept, and ship it off-box. The signal is a chain terminating in a root you did not put there, or a familiar hostname suddenly presenting a certificate from a different issuer.

The fundamental limit is worth stating plainly: an attacker who can add a root to your trust store can very likely also alter what you record. Detection from inside a compromised machine is unreliable by construction. That is why the strong answers move the check outside — Certificate Transparency logs, which make mis-issuance publicly visible, and pinning, which ignores the trust store entirely for the connections you care most about.

7.3 — Rotate a possibly-leaked key.

Ordering, and each step has a reason:

  1. Generate a new keypair and get it signed — before touching anything else, because until you hold the replacement you cannot act without an outage.
  2. Deploy the new certificate everywhere and verify it is being served.
  3. Then revoke the old one. Revoke first and you have an outage with no replacement ready.
  4. Rotate anything the key protected — session tickets, and any secret that travelled over connections you now believe were exposed.

Why revocation is not enough: per failure 7, many clients do not check revocation, or soft-fail when the check is unreachable. Assume the old certificate keeps working for its remaining lifetime, and let that shape your urgency.

Client impact: if the CA is unchanged, none — clients trust the CA, not the specific key. This is exactly why key pinning (5.2) makes rotation dramatically riskier, and why it is a deliberate trade rather than a free improvement.

What forward secrecy changes: enormously. Because TLS 1.3 always uses ephemeral key exchange, the leaked key cannot decrypt previously recorded traffic — it only enables impersonation from now until it is replaced. That converts "potentially years of history exposed" into "a window we can close today", and it is the single biggest reason the v1 design in stage 10 was abandoned.

Rung 8 — Design (worked arguments)

8.1 — Where TLS terminates. A defensible design: TLS terminates at the load balancer for the public edge (so certificate management is centralised and the public CA integration lives in one place), and internal traffic runs mTLS via sidecars.

Then answer the question honestly: between the load balancer and the first service, traffic is plaintext unless you re-encrypt. Anyone with access to that network segment, to a host in it, or to a packet capture on it can read everything — including the credentials your edge just validated.

Whether that is acceptable depends entirely on who "anyone" is: your cloud provider's network, other tenants, your own operators, and anything that gets a foothold in the VPC. For most systems handling payments or personal data the honest answer is no, and the fix is re-encryption to the backend or end-to-end mTLS with the load balancer passing through rather than terminating.

8.3 — The case against mTLS internally.

Every workload now needs an identity, which means a certificate, which means issuance, distribution, rotation and revocation for every pod — at a lifetime short enough to be safe, so it is happening constantly. When that machinery breaks, it does not break one service, it breaks the mesh, and the failure presents as universal connection errors with no obvious owner.

Meanwhile the threat it addresses — an attacker already inside our network passively reading service-to-service traffic — is one we may have better and cheaper answers for, and mTLS does nothing about the far more common case of a compromised service with a perfectly valid identity, which it will happily authenticate.

What would change my mind: a compliance requirement that names encryption in transit; a genuinely multi-tenant or untrusted network; or a mature platform team already running a mesh, where the marginal cost is close to zero because someone else owns the certificate lifecycle. The argument above is really about operational maturity, not about cryptography.
