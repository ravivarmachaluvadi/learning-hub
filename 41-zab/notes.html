Lab 41 · Copies and Agreement

ZAB — how five coffee shops keep one ticket queue

Tell a friend: Ana, Ben, Cara, Dev and Eve each run one shop of the same coffee chain, and all five keep a copy of one master ticket list. Only one of them at a time is the manager, and only the manager may add a line. She sends out the finished answer, "ticket = 44", never the instruction "add one", so a shop can copy the same line twice and still be right. When she dies mid-sentence, the next manager must be someone whose copy already holds everything most shops hold, and her first act is to finish her predecessor's business before starting her own. That last rule is the one thing ZAB has that plain consensus does not.

The interactive version is The Epoch Ledger, also saved beside this file as visualization.html. What this lab is made of, what you already knew before opening it, and how its numbers were made are all in section 17 at the end.

BEGINNER MODEL

1. Why does this thing need to exist?

In one breath: Every big system has a handful of tiny facts that absolutely everyone must agree on. The facts are small. The damage from disagreeing about them is not.

Every large system ends up with a short list of facts that everyone must agree on. Which server is currently in charge of customer shard 7. Which machines are alive right now. What today's configuration is. Who holds the lock on the nightly billing job.

Add all of those up and you have a few kilobytes. Now look at what one disagreement costs.

The fact everyone must share What one extra answer does
who owns customer shard 7 two servers both write to it, and neither notices
which machines are alive work is handed to a machine that is already gone
what today's configuration is half the fleet quietly runs yesterday's rules
who holds the nightly billing lock two schedulers charge every customer

Read the last row again. The cost of disagreement here is not a slow page. It is money moving twice.

So people build a small, separate, extremely careful service. Its only job is to hold those facts and never give two different answers. Apache ZooKeeper is the best-known one. HBase uses it to decide which server owns which region. Kafka used it for years to decide which broker is the controller. Thousands of in-house systems use it as the place where "who is the leader" lives.

ZAB is the engine inside ZooKeeper. It takes a change, gets it onto enough machines, and makes sure the replacement carries on from exactly the right place when the machine handling changes dies. Which it will. In the middle of something.

That last clause is the whole lab. Here is what it looks like when it happens to people rather than to servers.

2. What is the problem? Five shops and one ticket queue

In one breath: Five coffee shops share one ticket queue, so only one person at a time is allowed to add to it. She writes down the answer, not the sum. Then she dies halfway through, and somebody has to take over.

Forget servers for a moment.

A coffee chain has five shops, and one person behind each counter: Ana, Ben, Cara, Dev and Eve. A customer can order at one shop and collect at another, so all five shops must show the same ticket queue.

There is one master list of everything that has happened. Every shop keeps its own copy of it.

One of the five is the manager. Today that is Eve. She is the only one allowed to add a line to the master list. The other four just copy what she sends. That is deliberate. If five people could write, you would need a way to reconcile five versions, and there is no version of that story that ends well.

Meera walks in and wants a coffee. Here is exactly what Eve does.

  1. She looks at her own copy of the list. The last ticket issued was 43.
  2. She adds one, getting 44.
  3. She writes down the line "ticket = 44", and sends that line to all five shops.
  4. When enough shops have written it down, she hands Meera ticket 44.

Why does step 3 send the answer instead of the sum?

Stop and look at step 3, because everything in this lab hangs on it.

Eve sends "ticket = 44". She does not send "add one to the ticket number".

🔮 Predict: Ana, Ben, Cara and Dev all have to copy that line down. What goes wrong if the line says "add one" instead of "ticket = 44"?

Reveal

A shop that copies "add one" twice ends up somewhere different from a shop that copies it once. And shops will copy things twice. They write a batch down after a power cut. They replay yesterday's whole list from the beginning after a crash. They receive the same shout twice from a flaky phone line.

A line that says "add one" gives a different answer depending on how many times it was applied, and on what the state was when it was applied. A line that says "ticket = 44" gives the same answer every time, forever.

If you guessed "nothing, as long as every shop applies it exactly once", you are assuming exactly-once delivery over a network. That assumption is the one thing you never get. The design here is cheaper: make the line safe to apply twice, and stop worrying about how many times it arrives.

Eve does the thinking once. The shops do no thinking at all. That is the whole design in one sentence: one brain, many notebooks.

What happens the moment the manager dies?

Now Eve has a heart attack.

She had just written "ticket = 44" and sent it out. Two shops had written it down. She had not yet said "go", and she had not yet handed Meera anything. Then she was gone.

Someone has to take over. And the moment they do, the trouble starts. The person who takes over is the rest of this lab.

3. Why not just let the most senior person take over?

In one breath: The obvious fix is what any sensible shop would do — if the manager goes quiet, whoever notices first takes charge. Write that rule down, because we are about to break it three ways.

The obvious fix is what any sensible person would do.

"If the manager goes quiet, the most senior person present takes over and carries on."

It is not a stupid idea. It is roughly what a real coffee chain would do. Let us write it down as a rule and then break it.

Rule v1. When the manager has been silent for a while, whoever notices first announces that they are the manager now. They look at their own copy of the list, and carry on issuing tickets.

Ana notices Eve's silence. Ana announces. Ana starts issuing tickets. What could possibly go wrong?

4. Where does the obvious fix break?

In one breath: Three different ways, and each teaches something different. Two people take over at once. The person who takes over is missing a line. The person who takes over holds a line nobody else has.

It breaks in three completely different ways. Each one teaches you a different thing. Take them one at a time.

4a. What if two people take over at once?

Ana and Ben both notice the silence at the same second. Both announce they are the manager. Both start issuing tickets.

Now there are two master lists and no way to merge them. The lists are not sets of independent facts. They are ordered instructions, and each one was computed from the one before it.

You have met this one before. Your split-brain lab was entirely about it, and its conclusion was blunt: you cannot stop two machines believing they are in charge; you can only stop the loser's actions counting. Your raft lab measured two leaders coexisting routinely, and showed that the fix is not prevention but harmlessness.

So we already know the shape of the answer. Require a majority to agree before anyone may act. And stamp everything with a number that lets the old manager's work be recognised and ignored.

Good. That is one of three problems solved, and it is the one you already knew. Keep going. The other two are the ones this lab is actually about.

4b. What if the stand-in never saw the last few lines?

Cara takes over. But Cara's shop is at the far end of the country on a bad connection, and she never received "ticket = 44". Ana and Ben did.

Cara looks at her own copy. The last ticket was 43. So she issues 44 to the next customer, Omar.

Meanwhile Meera is standing at Ana's counter holding ticket 44.

Two customers, one ticket. Notice that nothing "went wrong" mechanically. Every shop followed its rules faithfully. The problem is that we let the wrong person take over.

🔮 Predict: What is the minimum thing we must require of whoever takes over, so this cannot happen?

Reveal

That they hold everything that any majority already holds. Not "everything" — nobody can know what everything is. Just this: whatever a majority of shops already wrote down must be in the new manager's copy.

And the way to get that for free is the counting argument you already know from quorums-and-replication. If the new manager is chosen by a majority, and any two majorities share at least one member, then the group that elects her contains at least one shop that has "ticket = 44". So if you additionally insist that the winner is the most complete copy in that group, the winner cannot possibly be missing it.

If you guessed "require the new manager to ask the others what she is missing", hold that thought. It is a real design, and section 10 shows why ZAB deliberately refuses it.

That is not luck. It is arithmetic. It is the same arithmetic that made Raft's up-to-date check work. ZAB just pushes it one step further, and section 12 shows exactly how far.

4c. What if the stand-in holds a line nobody else has?

Now the mirror image. Cara takes over, and Cara is the only shop that received "ticket = 44" before Eve died. Nobody else has it. No customer was ever handed anything.

Does Cara keep the line or throw it away?

🔮 Predict: Keeping it seems obviously right — it is real data, honestly written down. What could keeping it possibly cost?

Reveal

It costs you the ability to ever agree again, in one specific run.

Suppose Cara also dies before she publishes it. Dev takes over without the line, issues ticket 44 to Omar, and then Cara comes back holding her own different version of what happened at that position in the list. Now two shops have two different lists that both look legitimate. There is no rule that can ever reconcile them, because both of them are somebody's genuine record.

If you guessed "keep it, because deleting written data is never acceptable", you are applying a rule that is correct for money and wrong for this. Nobody was ever promised anything about that line.

If Cara keeps it and publishes it, fine — no harm done. The problem is only the run above, where she keeps it and then vanishes.

The only safe answer is uncomfortable at first. A line that no majority ever confirmed may be deleted. And if the new manager does not have it, it must be deleted from anyone who does.

That feels like destroying data. It is not, and here is the exact reason. Nobody was ever promised anything about that line. Meera never walked away holding ticket 44. Deleting it costs precisely zero promises. Keeping it costs you the ability to ever agree again.

This asymmetry is what makes the whole thing affordable.

The line's situation Promise made? What must happen
a majority wrote it down and Meera was told yes it can never be removed
fewer than a majority wrote it down, nobody was told no it may be freely deleted

5. What do we actually need to guarantee?

In one breath: Four promises, in plain English. Three of them you would have guessed. The third one is the reason ZAB exists at all.

Four promises. No jargon. The rest of this lab is the pursuit of these four.

P1 — One list, everywhere. Every shop's copy is the same list, in the same order, with no gaps. Not "eventually similar" — the same. If Ana's list says the third thing that happened was "ticket = 44", then every shop's third entry is "ticket = 44", or that shop has not got to the third entry yet.

P2 — Once you are told it happened, it happened. If Meera walks away holding ticket 44, no sequence of crashes, network failures or handovers may ever produce a world in which ticket 44 was not issued.

P3 — A new manager finishes the old manager's business before starting her own. This is the one that is easy to skip and impossible to recover from. A new manager must publish everything she inherited and act on it herself before she computes anything new. Otherwise she computes ticket 44 from a picture of the world that is missing ticket 44.

P4 — Anything nobody was promised may be deleted, and must be, if it stands in the way of P1.

Read P3 again. It is the promise that is unique to this lab. P1 and P2 are what Raft and Paxos give you as well. P3 is the one that comes from "one brain, many notebooks" — from the fact that what gets broadcast is an answer somebody computed, not an instruction anybody can compute.

Section 12 shows precisely where Paxos fails P3, and why the ZooKeeper authors decided that was a reason to design something new.

6. What is the key idea?

In one breath: Number every line with a chapter and a line number. Only let the holder of the newest line take charge. Make her republish everything she inherited before she writes a word of her own.

Three sentences.

  1. Give every change a two-part serial number: which manager wrote it, and which change of theirs it was. The pair only ever increases. So any two changes anywhere in the world can be put in order by comparing two integers, and any change can be recognised as belonging to an older management.

  2. Only the holder of the highest serial number may take over, and they must be chosen by a majority. Because any two majorities share a member, this makes it arithmetically impossible for the new manager to be missing anything a majority already had.

  3. The new manager's first act is to make her entire inherited list final — and only then may she write anything of her own. Everyone else is forced to match her list exactly, deleting anything she does not have.

Picture the two-part serial number as it would look on paper. Each line of the master list carries a chapter number and a line number. Cara opens a new chapter when she takes over from Eve. Her first line is chapter 2, line 1 — not chapter 1, line 4.

Everything else in ZAB is the detail of making those three sentences true when machines die halfway through.

7. Which words do you need now?

In one breath: Every word below names something that has already happened to Ana, Cara, Eve or Meera. You have felt each one. Now each gets its real name, and the symbol arrives last.

Nothing here is new. Each heading is a question about the shop, and the answer ends with the word engineers use.

7.1 What is one line on the master list?

One line on the master list is "ticket = 44". That is the whole thing.

Say what it is in plain words first: a record of a change that has already been worked out, not an order to work something out. Eve did the arithmetic. The line carries the result of the arithmetic. Ana, Ben, Cara and Dev copy the result and run nothing.

Engineers call that line a transaction, abbreviated txn in ZooKeeper's source. It is a confusing name if you come from databases, because it has nothing to do with wrapping several statements in one commit. Here it means one recorded state change.

In real ZooKeeper, a client sends create("/queue/ticket-", ephemeral+sequential). The leader turns that into a transaction reading create /queue/ticket-0000000044 with czxid=0x100000005, ephemeralOwner=0x.... The sequence number, the timestamps, the generated node name: all computed once, by the leader, and shipped as facts.

Why does the word exist at all? To draw a line between what the client asked for and what actually gets replicated. They are not the same thing. Conflating them is the single most common mistake people make when reasoning about ZooKeeper.

Here is that line drawn concretely. Two clients concurrently call create("/lock-", sequential). The leader turns them into two different transactions, create /lock-0000000007 and create /lock-0000000008. Every follower stores those two literal names. No follower ever runs the sequence-number generator.

This is the exact opposite of a Raft log entry, which holds the command and lets every server compute the result. It is much closer to what change-data-capture ships, and to the "store the effect, not the intent" idea in event-sourcing. Because transactions are results, replaying them twice changes nothing — the same idempotence property you studied in idempotency. Section 13 shows ZooKeeper leaning on it hard when it restores from a snapshot.

7.2 What is the serial number on a line?

Back to the piece of paper. Each line of the master list carries two numbers: the chapter it was written in, and its position within that chapter. Cara's first line after taking over from Eve is chapter 2, line 1.

In plain words: one number that answers two questions at once — whose change is this, and where in the sequence does it sit?

Now the names. The chapter number is the epoch. The position within the chapter is the counter. The two of them welded together are the zxid, the ZooKeeper transaction id.

The thing on the page In words The name and the notation
the chapter number at the top of the line which management wrote this line epoch — the top 32 bits
the line number inside that chapter which of that manager's lines this is counter — the bottom 32 bits
the two together, one number orders every line ever written, anywhere zxid, written 1.4 here, 0x100000004 in real logs

A zxid is a single 64-bit integer. Because it is one integer, comparing two zxids is a single comparison. "Which of these two changes came first" never needs any other information.

Why pack two things into one number? Because both questions have to be answerable at the same time, in the same breath, on every message. Welding them makes every comparison in the protocol trivial.

Watch the counter restart. 1.1, 1.2, 1.3 are the first three changes of the first manager. Her successor's first change is 2.1 — not 1.4. The counter goes back to the beginning; the epoch is what moves forward. A shop holding 1.3 and a shop holding 2.1 can tell instantly who is behind.

The epoch on its own is worth naming separately, because it does work without the counter. It is a monotonically increasing integer identifying one period of one manager's authority. Every change that manager creates carries it. Every shop refuses messages stamped with an epoch lower than the highest one it has accepted. Cara has accepted epoch 2. A proposal arrives stamped epoch 1. Cara refuses it in one integer comparison, without knowing or caring what the proposal says.

You have met the epoch under other names. It is your fencing token from distributed-locking. It is your epoch from split-brain, which already listed them as one idea with many names: "epoch = term = generation = zxid = mod_revision = sequencer = incarnation". It is Raft's term and Paxos's proposal number.

The one genuinely new thing here is the welding. In Raft, authority and position are two separate fields, term and index. ZAB makes them one number, so the epoch does double duty: it is both the authority stamp and the position in the log. Section 12 works through what that buys and what it costs.

7.3 Why does each shop need two chapter numbers, not one?

Every shop writes two numbers on the inside cover of its ledger. "The newest chapter I have agreed to join", and "the chapter my writing is actually in".

Those are two different facts, and this is the detail everyone skips and then gets confused by. Agreeing to join a new chapter and having actually joined it are different states, and a crash can happen between them.

The names are acceptedEpoch and currentEpoch. In real ZooKeeper they are literally two files with those names, sitting in the data directory. Both are written to disk before being used.

Suppose Ben has agreed to chapter 5, but the chapter-5 manager died before finishing the handover. Ben must still refuse chapter-4 messages, so his acceptedEpoch must be 5. But he must still honestly report that his data belongs to chapter 3, so his currentEpoch must be 3. Collapse them into one number and you either accept messages you should refuse, or misreport how current your data is.

Concretely: Ben has acceptedEpoch=5, currentEpoch=3, and his newest change is 3.9. During the next election he says two things, and they come from two different numbers.

What Ben says in the election Which number it comes from
"my data is from chapter 3, at position 9" currentEpoch, plus his lastZxid
"I will refuse anything stamped 4 or lower" acceptedEpoch

Your split-brain lab gave the rule that "the epoch number is persisted to disk before use, so a restart cannot reissue it". ZAB needs two of them, for exactly the reason that lab gave for needing one.

7.4 What are the three things the manager says?

Three sentences, in order: "write this down", then "written" coming back, then "it counts now" going out.

What is said Who says it The message name
write this down manager → shops PROPOSE(zxid, txn)
written, and on my disk a shop → manager ACK(zxid)
it counts now, act on it manager → shops COMMIT(zxid)

Those three are the broadcast phase, the ordinary steady state of the system. Each shop that receives a proposal appends the transaction to its own history, puts it on disk, and replies. When the manager holds replies from a majority including herself, she acts on the transaction locally, answers the customer, and sends the commit to everyone.

The reason there are three words and not two is that stored and final are different states. Confusing them is a correctness bug, not a wording problem. A stored-but-uncommitted transaction may still be deleted. A committed one may not.

Watch the counting. Eve proposes 1.2 = "ticket 2". Ana, Ben and Cara store it and acknowledge. That is three of five; Eve included makes four, past the majority of three. Eve acts on it, tells Meera "ticket 2 is yours", and broadcasts the commit.

This is two-phase commit with the abort branch deleted. Compare it directly with your two-phase-commit lab.

There, a participant's "yes" is a promise it cannot take back, and the coordinator's death leaves everyone holding locks forever. Here, "yes" costs the shop nothing it cannot undo. There is no abort message at all.

And the manager's death is survivable, because a majority of yes-votes is the decision. No single machine holds it. That is the precise sense in which quorum-based replication answers 2PC's blocking problem, which is what your 2PC lab said to come here for.

7.5 How many shops count as "enough"?

You know this one. One-line refresher: a majority of the servers, ⌊N/2⌋ + 1. Engineers call it a quorum.

Its whole job is that any two of them must share at least one member. That is why a decision made by one majority can never be contradicted by another. In a 5-server ZooKeeper ensemble the quorum is 3.

One thing worth re-noticing, from quorums-and-replication: this is a consensus quorum, one that decides. It is not an R+W>N read/write quorum, which merely intersects. ZAB uses it to decide, exactly like Paxos and Raft.

7.6 Who is who, and what are they doing right now?

The manager, the shops, and shops that get all the news but do not get a vote.

The manager is the leader, also called the primary. A shop is a follower. A shop that receives everything and serves customers, but is never counted in a majority, is an observer.

Every ZooKeeper server is in exactly one of four states.

State What the server is doing The shop version
LOOKING no leader; running an election shouting "who is in charge?"
FOLLOWING has a leader; applying what it sends copying lines down
LEADING it is the leader holding the marker pen
OBSERVING receives everything, votes on nothing reading the noticeboard only

Why do observers exist at all? Because every extra voting member makes writes slower, since there are more machines to wait for, while still adding read capacity. Observers let you add read capacity and geographic reach without making the write path wider. Section 13 comes back to this.

These are the same three roles as Raft's follower, candidate and leader, with one real difference. ZAB has no separate candidate state. In ZAB you do not ask for votes. You broadcast your opinion, and everybody converges on the best one. Section 10 shows how.

7.7 What does one shop actually hold?

The shop's copy of the list, and the serial number of the last line on it.

The list is the history — the ordered sequence of transactions the server holds. The serial number of the newest one is lastZxid.

lastZxid is the single number a server reports during an election, and it is enough. Because zxids are totally ordered and gap-free within an epoch, one number describes the whole history.

Dev reports lastZxid = 1.3. That tells everyone he has exactly 1.1, 1.2, 1.3. There is no way for him to have 1.1 and 1.3 but not 1.2 — section 10 explains what enforces that.

7.8 What is the difference between writing it down and acting on it?

Writing it down is "the line is on my page". Acting on it is "I will show this to customers now".

Engineers call the first one accept and the second one deliver. A server accepts a transaction when it stores and acknowledges it. It delivers the transaction when it is told to commit. Delivery is always in zxid order and never skips.

Delivering means applying the transaction to the in-memory data tree, so that clients reading from this server can see it.

The word exists because the whole protocol is about the gap between these two, and where crashes fall inside that gap. This is exactly Raft's stored versus committed and applied distinction. It is also the dashed-versus-solid cells in this lab's simulator.

7.9 What is "atomic broadcast", and why is that not just consensus?

Plain words first: "everybody gets every message, and everybody gets them in the same order — or the system stops rather than break that."

Precisely, it is a communication primitive with three classical properties. Integrity: nothing is delivered that was not broadcast. Total order: any two servers that deliver two messages deliver them in the same order. Agreement: if one correct server delivers a message, all correct servers eventually do.

The term exists because it names a primitive, not a system. "Consensus" is agreeing on one value. "Atomic broadcast" is agreeing on an unbounded ordered stream of them. They are known to be equivalent problems, in the sense that you can build either from the other. But they are different shapes, and ZAB is deliberately shaped as the second.

7.10 Does one shop do the thinking, or do all five?

One brain, many notebooks versus many brains all doing the same arithmetic. That is the whole distinction, and it is the point of this lab.

In primary-backup replication, one server executes the request and ships the resulting state change. Backups apply it without executing anything. In replicated state machine replication, the log holds the command. Every replica executes it deterministically, so all replicas independently arrive at the same state.

Why does the distinction matter? Because it decides what your protocol has to guarantee.

A state machine only needs the log to be the same everywhere. Each replica can compute its own state from it whenever it likes. A primary-backup system additionally needs the primaries themselves to be ordered, because each primary computes its changes on top of the previous primary's changes. Order the log but not the primaries, and you get two managers computing ticket 44 from the same stale picture — which is exactly section 4b.

The one-line contrast: Raft replicates INCREMENT ticket, and every server runs it and gets 44. ZAB replicates ticket = 44, and only the leader ran anything.

ZooKeeper needs primary-backup because its transactions carry generated values. Sequential node names, czxids, session owners, timestamps: every one of those must be identical on every server, and none of them can be recomputed. Section 12 shows what that forces the protocol to add.

7.11 What are the three ways a shop is brought back into step?

The new manager can say three different things to a shop that is out of step. "Here are the lines you are missing." "Cross out everything after line N." "Tear it up, here is a fresh copy."

Their names are DIFF, TRUNC and SNAP. The leader picks one per follower, by comparing that follower's lastZxid with its own.

The instruction In words When the leader picks it
DIFF here are the transactions you are missing the follower is simply behind
TRUNC discard everything after this zxid the follower holds something the leader does not
SNAP here is the whole data tree, start again the follower is so far behind that patching costs more than replacing

TRUNC is P4 from section 5 being enforced, in one message.

Watch all three chosen at once. The leader's newest change is 2.7.

The follower reports What it gets Why
2.4 a DIFF of 2.5, 2.6, 2.7 simply three lines behind
1.9 a TRUNC back to the last change they share, then a DIFF forward 1.9 is a change from the previous chapter that the leader never had
1.2, with the leader's log trimmed past that point a SNAP there is nothing left to patch from

This is the same repair job as Raft's nextIndex back-off, done in one round instead of by walking backwards. A zxid comparison answers in one step what Raft's back-off discovers by probing.

8. Which two pictures should you carry, and where does the nice one lie?

In one breath: Carry two mental models. One is the coffee chain, and it is right about almost everything. The other is five servers and their disks. Then learn the four places where the coffee chain quietly lies to you.

What is happening in the shop?

One manager. Five shops. A master list.

Eve reads her own copy, computes the answer, writes the answer down, sends it out, waits until a majority have written it down, and only then tells Meera her ticket number.

When Eve dies, the shop with the longest list takes over — chosen by a majority. Its first act is to make every other shop's list identical to its own, adding what they lack and crossing out what they have that it does not.

That is the entire protocol at story level. Everything below is the same thing with real names.

What does one server actually hold?

Five servers. Each holds five things on disk, and none of them may be lost in a restart.

Where does the story lie to you?

Four places. Each one matters. Each one is a rule you would get wrong if the shop were all you had.

Lie one: shops can tell "the manager is dead" from "the phone line is down". Servers cannot.

This is the whole reason the protocol is hard. A server that stops hearing from the leader has exactly one piece of information: silence. Silence is produced identically by a dead leader, a slow leader, and a cut cable.

Every timeout in ZAB is a guess. The protocol is built so that a wrong guess costs availability but never correctness. Your split-brain lab made this its central point. It is just as true here.

Lie two: in the story the new manager "takes over". In the protocol, nobody hands anything over.

There is no moment of transfer. Eve may still be alive, still believe she is the manager, and still be sending proposals. She is not stopped. She is outvoted and then ignored.

Her messages arrive stamped with an old epoch and are refused, one integer comparison at a time. Nothing ever tells her she has been replaced. She works it out on her own, when she notices she cannot reach a majority any more.

Lie three: shops would never delete a line they had written. Servers must.

TRUNC feels wrong the first time you meet it. In the human version, tearing a page out of the ledger is what fraud looks like.

In the protocol it is the opposite. It is the only mechanism that gets a divergent server back into agreement. And it is only ever applied to changes that no majority confirmed and no customer was ever told about. Scenario 8 in the simulator lets you switch it off and watch a server disagree with everyone else forever.

Lie four: the manager waits for shops to confirm before telling Meera. A real ZooKeeper read skips all of that.

In the story, every question and every answer goes through the manager. In ZooKeeper, writes go through the leader and are strongly ordered. But reads are answered by whichever server the client happens to be connected to, straight out of its memory, with no round trip to anybody.

That is how one ensemble serves enormous read volumes, and the price is that a read can be stale. It is not a bug and it is not a corner case. It is the single most surprising thing about ZooKeeper in production, and section 11.7 and simulator scenario 7 are entirely about it.

INTERMEDIATE MODEL

10. How is the mechanism built, one failure at a time?

In one breath: Start with the version a competent engineer would write first. Break it. Fix it. Repeat five times. Every strange detail in the finished protocol is the scar of one of those breaks.

We are going to build the real protocol by starting with the naive version, breaking it, and repairing it. Every "weird" detail in finished ZAB is a scar.

Throughout, the five shops are the five servers, and the engineers' names for them ride alongside: Ana (S1), Ben (S2), Cara (S3), Dev (S4) and Eve (S5). Five servers, so a majority is 3.

v1 — what if whoever notices the silence takes over?

Ana stops hearing from Eve, so Ana announces herself as manager and carries on. That is the rule, and it is section 3's rule v1 written for servers.

It breaks in the way section 4a described. Ana and Ben both notice at the same moment. Both start writing. Two lists now exist, and neither is wrong.

The repair is the one you already know. A would-be manager must be chosen by a majority. Two majorities always share a member, and a server votes once per election round, so two servers cannot both collect one.

v2 — what if a majority elects, but it elects anybody?

A majority of servers agree on one of them, and that one leads. Better. Not enough.

It breaks in the way section 4b described. The majority might pick a server that is missing a change the rest of them have. Say Dev wins while Ana, Ben and Cara all hold 1.2 and Dev does not.

Dev's list is now the official list. And because a ZAB leader only ever pushes, never pulls, everyone else is forced down to it. A committed change is deleted from the entire cluster.

🔮 Predict: The repair is obviously "elect the most up-to-date server". But how up to date does the winner have to be, and how does anybody know?

Reveal

The winner has to be the most up-to-date server in the electing majority — and that is automatically enough.

Any change that was committed was written down by some majority M. The electing group is also a majority E. M and E share at least one server, so E contains someone holding that change. If the winner is the most complete member of E, the winner holds it too.

Nobody needs global knowledge. Three servers comparing one number each is sufficient, and that is the whole argument.

If you guessed "the winner must hold everything that exists anywhere", that is unachievable: nobody can survey a cluster that is currently broken. If you guessed "any server that a majority is willing to accept", that is Raft's weaker rule, and it is safe — but it costs Raft a two-directional repair that ZAB avoids.

You can watch this fail. Simulator scenario 5 · Electing a server that is behind switches the rule to "lowest-numbered server wins", and lets you watch two committed tickets get hatched out of every ledger in the cluster.

The repair, then. Each server's vote is a triple: its currentEpoch, its lastZxid, and its own server id. Written together that is (currentEpoch, lastZxid, serverId), compared in that order, and the largest wins. The server id is only a tie-break, so that two equally up-to-date servers cannot deadlock.

This is a genuinely different election from Raft's, and the difference is worth holding on to.

How the election runs Who ends up winning
Raft a candidate sends RequestVote; each voter answers yes or no, using the rule "is this candidate's log at least as up to date as mine?" someone sufficiently up to date, not necessarily the most
ZAB every server broadcasts its opinion of who should win; on hearing a better one it adopts it and re-broadcasts the maximum within the electing group

Both are safe. ZAB's version buys one specific thing. Because the winner is the maximum, the leader can never need data from a follower. Synchronisation is therefore strictly one-directional, which is what makes ZAB's recovery a single push per follower instead of a negotiation.

v3 — what if the most up-to-date server starts writing straight away?

The winner takes over and issues the next ticket. This is the version that breaks in the way that ZAB exists for. It is P3 from section 5.

Work it through with real numbers.

  • Eve (S5) is the leader in epoch 1. She has committed 1.1 = "ticket 1".
  • Meera arrives. Eve computes ticket 2, writes 1.2 = "ticket 2" into her own history, and proposes it.
  • Only Ana (S1) receives it before Eve dies. No customer was told anything.
  • Ana now has the longest history, 1.1, 1.2, so Ana wins the election.
  • Under v3, Ana immediately serves the next customer, Omar. She looks at what she has delivered — which is only 1.1, because 1.2 was never committed. She computes ticket 2, and writes 2.1 = "ticket 2".

There are now two transactions in the cluster's history claiming ticket 2. If both survive, Meera and Omar are holding the same number.

🔮 Predict: Why did Ana not simply look at her whole list, see 1.2, and issue ticket 3?

Reveal

Because 1.2 was never committed, and an uncommitted transaction is not a fact yet.

Ana has no right to build on it. At the moment she looks, 1.2 might still be deleted — it would be, if some other server without it had won. A primary may only compute on top of things that are settled.

So the answer cannot be "peek at your own uncommitted entries". The answer has to be "settle them first": decide, definitively, that they are part of history, and only then compute.

If you guessed "she should ask the others whether they have it", notice what that costs. It turns recovery into a negotiation, and it makes correctness depend on reaching servers that may be down.

You can watch this fail. Simulator scenario 4 · The new primary jumps the gun switches this rule off. Every run ends with two ledger entries claiming the same ticket, and the invariant chip "each answer built on the last" turns red.

Here is the repair, and it is the heart of ZAB. Before a new leader may accept a single client request, it must do five things in order.

  1. Establish a new epoch that a majority have agreed to.
  2. Force every follower's history to be identical to its own.
  3. Get a majority to confirm they have done so.
  4. Deliver its entire history — declaring every inherited transaction final, including the ones its predecessor left hanging.
  5. Only now, start numbering its own transactions, at newEpoch.1.

Step 4 is the one people miss. It is not bookkeeping. It is what makes P3 true: the new primary acts on everything it inherited before it computes anything of its own.

There is a lovely equivalence here, if you have Raft fresh in your mind.

Raft has a famously counter-intuitive rule, the "Figure 8" rule. A leader may not commit an entry from a previous term just because a majority store it. It must first commit an entry of its own term, which in practice means appending a no-op and committing that. The no-op exists to drag the previous term's entries over the commit line safely.

ZAB's NEWLEADER round is that no-op, made explicit, mandatory, and moved to the front. Same job: make the inherited tail safe under the new authority before anything depends on it. Raft does it lazily and implicitly. ZAB does it eagerly and by name. ZAB has to do it eagerly, because in a primary-backup system the very next transaction is computed from that state.

v4 — what if everyone matches the leader, but nobody deletes anything?

The leader sends each follower whatever it is missing, and nothing is ever removed. That sounds humane. It is section 4c's trap.

A follower holding a transaction the leader does not have keeps it. Its history is now permanently different from everyone else's. Every later change sits at a different position on that server than on the others.

The repair is TRUNC. The leader compares each follower's lastZxid to its own. When the follower holds something the leader does not, the leader orders the follower to discard everything past the last zxid they share.

Why is that safe? Because the leader is guaranteed to hold everything committed. So anything being discarded was never committed, and was therefore never promised to anyone.

You can watch this fail in simulator scenario 8 · The change nobody deleted.

v5 — what about messages from the old manager that arrive late?

Followers apply what the leader sends. Simple, and not safe.

Eve is not dead, just slow, or partitioned, or her messages were sitting in a network buffer. They arrive after a new epoch has started, and nothing in the content of a transaction says "I am stale".

The repair: every message carries its epoch, and a server refuses anything stamped below its acceptedEpoch. One integer comparison, before looking at anything else.

This is why acceptedEpoch must reach disk before it is used. A server that restarted and forgot could be talked into accepting an old primary all over again.

Two honest notes about how this plays out in the real implementation.

The first is that a stale proposal usually cannot even be delivered. A ZooKeeper follower and its leader talk over one TCP connection, and moving to a new leader means that connection is closed. So the epoch check's day job is guarding the recovery handshake, by refusing a would-be leader whose proposed epoch is not actually newer.

The second is that the same TCP connection does more work than it looks. Because it is FIFO, a follower can assume that proposals arrive in order and without gaps. If it ever sees a gap, that does not mean "a message was lost". It means the connection broke. The correct response is to go back to LOOKING and re-synchronise from scratch.

This is why ZAB needs no per-message repair machinery at all. It is also a real assumption you would break if you re-implemented ZAB over UDP. The simulator enforces it: turn the message-loss dial up and watch followers drop their connection and re-sync, rather than quietly accept a gap.

You can watch this fail in simulator scenario 9 · A message from a dead primary arrives late.

What does the finished protocol look like?

Each server is in one of three states, and moves between them like this.

stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> LOOKING
    LOOKING --> LOOKING: no majority agrees yet
    LOOKING --> LEADING: a majority named me
    LOOKING --> FOLLOWING: a majority named someone else
    LEADING --> LOOKING: cannot reach a majority any more
    FOLLOWING --> LOOKING: silence from the leader, or a gap in its stream
    LEADING --> [*]: crash
    FOLLOWING --> [*]: crash

There are four phases, and they always run in this order.

Phase 0 — leader election. Every LOOKING server broadcasts its vote (currentEpoch, lastZxid, serverId). On hearing a better one it adopts that vote and re-broadcasts. When a majority hold the same vote, and that has stayed true for a short settling period, the winner moves to LEADING and everyone else to FOLLOWING. ZooKeeper's implementation of this is called Fast Leader Election. The settling period is finalizeWait, and it exists precisely so that a better vote still in flight is not missed.

Phase 1 — discovery. Each follower tells the prospective leader its acceptedEpoch. Once a majority have reported, the leader picks newEpoch = max(reported) + 1 and announces it. Each follower that accepts it writes acceptedEpoch = newEpoch to disk, then replies with its currentEpoch and lastZxid.

(In the ZAB paper, Phase 1 also lets the leader adopt a better history if one of the responses turns out to be more complete than its own. In ZooKeeper's implementation this can never happen, because Fast Leader Election already picked the maximum, so the implementation skips it. Worth knowing when you read the paper and find a step the code does not have.)

Phase 2 — synchronisation. For each follower, the leader compares lastZxid against its own and sends DIFF, TRUNC or SNAP. Then it sends NEWLEADER, stamped with the new epoch. A follower that applies the sync and accepts NEWLEADER writes currentEpoch = newEpoch to disk and acknowledges. When a majority have acknowledged NEWLEADER, the leader delivers its entire history and sends UPTODATE, which releases every follower to start serving clients again.

Phase 3 — broadcast. Now, and only now, client writes are accepted. For each one, the leader computes the transaction from its delivered state, stamps it newEpoch.(++counter), appends it, and proposes. Followers append and acknowledge. On a majority of acknowledgements the leader delivers it, answers the client, and broadcasts COMMIT.

11.1 What if the leader dies after a majority stored the write, but before COMMIT?

Stage it with simulator scenario 2. It is exactly the worked trace in section 10: Eve tells Meera her ticket, then dies before anyone else knows it was final.

The write survives. The electing majority must overlap the storing majority, and the winner is the most complete member of the electing majority, so the winner has it. Its NEWLEADER round makes it final.

All four promises hold. What you lose is time. The cluster is unavailable for writes from the crash until UPTODATE.

This is the ordinary case, and it is why "leader elections per minute" is the ZooKeeper metric that matters. A cluster doing this every few minutes is not broken. It is thrashing, and the cause is nearly always tickTime and syncLimit tuned tighter than your actual network or your JVM's GC pauses.

11.2 What if the leader dies with the write on only a minority?

Stage it with simulator scenario 3. Only Ana received 1.2, and Eve died.

The new leader may or may not have it. If it does, the change becomes final. If it does not, TRUNC deletes it from whoever had it. With fewer than a majority holding it, the electing majority need not contain a holder.

Everything is kept, and nothing is lost, because the client never received a success.

There is an important subtlety hiding in that sentence. The client got no answer, which is not the same as "it did not happen". Its request may still have been committed by a leader that died a microsecond later.

This is the uncertain write, and it is universal. Your quorums-and-replication lab measured 500 out of 500 "failed" writes being readable afterwards. The only defence is the one from idempotency: make the retry safe, and do not try to find out what happened.

11.3 What if the leader is stranded on the small side of a partition?

Stage it with simulator scenario 6, or the Split 2 | 3 button.

The isolated leader can append to its own history, but it never collects a majority of acknowledgements. So it never commits anything and never answers a client. After syncLimit ticks of being unable to reach a majority, it shuts itself down and returns to LOOKING. The majority side elects a new leader at a higher epoch and carries on. When the network heals, the old leader's uncommitted tail is TRUNC'd.

P1 and P2 hold absolutely. What is lost is availability on the minority side — completely, deliberately, by design. Clients there get timeouts and eventually session expiry.

This is the "CP" in ZooKeeper's CP. It is also the reason a 2-node ZooKeeper ensemble is worse than a 1-node one: the majority is 2, so a single failure stops everything. Ensembles are 3, 5 or 7.

Your pacelc lab's framing applies exactly. ZooKeeper is PC/EC: consistent when partitioned, latency-optimised when not. That second half is why reads are local, which is 11.7.

11.4 What if two servers both believe they are leader?

Stage it by partitioning the cluster while a leader exists, and watch the "two primaries live" chip appear.

Nothing bad happens. The stale leader cannot commit, because it has no majority. Its proposals are refused by anyone who has accepted a higher epoch. It resigns on its own. Everything is kept and nothing is lost.

This one matters enough to say out loud. The sentence "ZAB guarantees there is only one leader" is false, in exactly the way your raft lab established that "Raft guarantees one leader" is false.

The true sentence is: at most one leader per epoch, and only a leader that a majority has joined can commit anything. Two leaders coexisting is normal. It is harmless because the loser's writes cannot count. That is the same conclusion your split-brain lab reached: you cannot stop a machine believing; you can stop its writes counting.

11.5 What if a follower falls a long way behind?

Stage it by killing a follower, issuing several writes, and reviving it.

On rejoining, Dev reports an old lastZxid. The leader sends a DIFF if the gap is small. Otherwise it sends a SNAP, which is a complete copy of the data tree. That happens when the gap is large, or when the leader has already trimmed its transaction log past that point.

Everything is kept and nothing is lost, but a SNAP is expensive. It serialises the entire tree and ships it.

This is what initLimit is for: the number of ticks a follower is allowed for connecting and synchronising. A big data tree and a tight initLimit produces the classic ZooKeeper failure, where a restarted node can never rejoin, times out, restarts, and loops forever.

11.6 What if a follower sees a gap in the leader's stream?

Stage it by turning the message-loss dial up in the simulator.

The follower drops the connection and returns to LOOKING, then re-synchronises from the leader. Proposals travel over one FIFO connection, so a gap cannot mean "a message was lost". It can only mean the connection is broken. Accepting a proposal with a gap would put two servers' histories out of alignment permanently.

P1 is kept. What is lost is liveness. The follower is out of service while it re-syncs, and under heavy loss the cluster can churn.

The pattern is worth carrying. It is the same shape your raft lab measured: safety never degrades at any loss rate; liveness degrades smoothly and then collapses. Election churn is always a timing problem, never a correctness one.

11.7 What if a read returns an old value?

Stage it with simulator scenario 7.

🔮 Predict: A write has just been committed by the leader. Omar's client is connected to Dev, who has not yet received the COMMIT. What does Omar's read return, and what does his client see in the way of a warning?

Reveal

He gets the previous value, and there is no warning at all. No error, no flag, no staleness header.

ZooKeeper reads are served straight from the local server's memory, with no coordination with anybody. Dev is not making anything up. He is simply behind, and he has no way to know it.

If you guessed "the read blocks until the server catches up", that is what sync() buys, and it is opt-in precisely because it costs a round trip to the leader. If you guessed "the client gets an error", note that a server which knew it was stale would already have the fix — the whole problem is that it does not know.

P1 is kept, because the server is not inventing anything. What is lost is linearizability of reads. ZooKeeper does not promise it and never did.

What ZooKeeper does promise is narrower and still useful. Writes are linearizable. Each client sees its own operations in order and never goes backwards in time: a client that has seen zxid 2.7 will never be served by a server behind 2.7, because the session tracks it. Across different clients, one may see a write the other does not, briefly.

The fix, when you need it, is to call sync() on the path first, then read. sync() is queued to the leader and returns down the same ordered connection. So by the time it returns, the follower has everything the leader had committed when it was issued.

In production this is the single most common source of ZooKeeper surprise. It is where your distributed-locking lab's warning lands: a lock built on ZooKeeper is only as good as the freshness of the read that checked it. Use the zxid or czxid as a fencing token at the resource, exactly as that lab concluded, rather than trusting a read.

11.8 What if an old proposal arrives after a new epoch has begun?

Stage it with simulator scenario 9.

With the rule on, the follower refuses the proposal in one comparison and logs a single line. Nothing else in the cluster notices.

With the rule off, the stale transaction is inserted into the ledger underneath a change that was already final. A delivered change becomes un-delivered, and two entries end up claiming the same ticket. P1 and P2 break simultaneously.

Why does it matter so much? Because nothing in a transaction's contents marks it as stale. The epoch in the zxid is the only marker, and one integer comparison is the entire defence.

11.9 What if a server restarts and has forgotten which epoch it accepted?

You cannot stage this one in the simulator. It is prevented by construction, which is the point.

Here is what would happen. A server that agreed to epoch 5, and then restarted believing it is at epoch 3, could be persuaded to follow an epoch-4 leader that a majority had already moved past. It would accept that leader's proposals.

The fix is "write it to disk first". acceptedEpoch must be durable before the server acts on it. That is the same rule as Paxos's promise, and the same rule your split-brain lab gave for epochs generally.

This is also why ZooKeeper calls fsync. An acknowledgement that is not durable is a lie, and a majority of lies is not a majority.

11.10 What if the whole ensemble loses its majority?

Stage it by killing three of five servers.

No leader can be elected. The cluster refuses all writes and existing sessions expire. Reads may still be served briefly by servers that have not yet noticed, and then they stop too.

P1 and P2 hold perfectly. Everything else is lost.

This is the correct behaviour, and it is the reason ZooKeeper is deployed as an odd number across failure domains. It will not guess, and there is no configuration flag that makes it guess. Recovery is simple: bring servers back. Their on-disk state is intact. They elect and re-synchronise.

11.11 What happens when a partition heals?

Stage it with Split 2 | 3, write on the majority side, then press Heal the network.

The returning servers find a leader at a higher epoch, join it, and are brought into step. They get a DIFF for what they lack, and a TRUNC for anything they wrote alone. Everything is kept and nothing is lost, because the minority side never committed anything to lose.

One thing is worth noticing here above all. There is no merge step anywhere in ZAB. There is one ledger, and reconciliation means "become identical to the leader".

That is the deep difference from the AP world of crdts and anti-entropy, where both sides' writes are real and merging is the entire problem. ZAB's minority side has nothing to merge, because it was never allowed to write.

12. What is guaranteed, what is not, and what did it cost?

In one breath: Six properties, three of them ordinary and three of them about ordering the managers themselves. The last three are what Paxos does not give you, and they are the reason ZAB was designed at all.

Which six promises does ZAB actually make?

These are ZAB's properties as the paper states them. Every one is now something you have watched happen to Ana, Cara, Dev, Eve or Meera.

The first three are what make it an atomic broadcast at all.

  1. Integrity. A server delivers a transaction only if some primary broadcast it. Nothing is invented.
  2. Total order. If a server delivers a before b, then every server that delivers b has already delivered a. One list, one order.
  3. Agreement. If one server delivers a and another delivers b, then one of them eventually delivers both. No permanent forks.

The next three are what make it ZAB rather than generic atomic broadcast. These are the primary-order properties.

  1. Local primary order. If a primary broadcasts a and then b, every server that delivers b has already delivered a. One manager's own changes never reorder.
  2. Global primary order. If primary P1 in epoch 1 broadcasts a, and a later primary P2 in epoch 2 broadcasts b, then every server that delivers both delivers a first. Chapters do not interleave.
  3. Primary integrity. If P2 broadcasts b, and some server delivers a transaction a broadcast by an earlier primary P1, then P2 must have delivered a before broadcasting b.

Property 6 is the one you built in v3. It is the reason the NEWLEADER round exists, and it is the property that a plain consensus protocol does not give you. In shop terms: Cara must publish Eve's last sentence before she speaks her own.

Why not just use Paxos?

This is the question the ZAB paper opens with. The answer is more specific than "ZAB is faster".

🔮 Predict: Multi-Paxos gives you an agreed, ordered log. Cara could run it. What could a primary-backup system possibly still be missing?

Reveal

An order on the managers, not just on the lines.

Multi-Paxos treats each log position as an independent instance of agreement. So a new leader is free to fill an earlier gap with a value of its own choosing, and free to start proposing before it has delivered what it inherited.

If you guessed "Paxos is slower" or "Paxos needs more messages", those are true of naive Paxos and largely fixed by Multi-Paxos. Speed was not the argument. Shape was.

Multi-Paxos treats each log position as an independent instance of agreement. That has two consequences a primary-backup system cannot live with.

The first is that a new leader may fill an earlier gap with a different value. Suppose the previous leader's proposal for position 26 reached no majority. A new leader is free to choose any value for 26, including its own new one — while position 27 already holds something the old leader computed assuming its own value at 26. The resulting state is one that no primary ever computed, and no client ever asked for.

ZAB forbids that shape entirely. A new primary either keeps the whole prefix or truncates the tail. It never substitutes.

The second is that nothing orders the primaries themselves. Paxos gives you an ordered log. It does not give you property 6. A new leader may start proposing before it has delivered what it inherited, which is exactly the ticket-44 duplication of v3.

So the ZooKeeper authors' position was not "Paxos is wrong". It was this. We would have to bolt so much on top of Paxos to get primary order, gap-free delivery and the throughput we need. We may as well design the primitive we actually want.

If that sounds familiar, it is the same reasoning that produced Raft. Your paxos lab's closing line was that Raft is "bolt a leader on top", designed on purpose. ZAB is the other answer to the same closing line, arrived at three years earlier and aimed at a different requirement.

What is explicitly not guaranteed?

Six things. Each is a promise people assume ZooKeeper makes, and it does not.

  • Linearizable reads. By default a read may be stale. Writes are linearizable; reads are not.
  • Cross-client freshness without sync(). Client A's completed write may not be visible to client B immediately.
  • Any progress below a majority. Deliberate.
  • Byzantine tolerance. A lying or corrupted server is out of scope entirely.
  • Write scale-out. More servers means more fault tolerance and slower writes, never more write throughput. ZooKeeper is a coordination service, not a datastore. The whole tree lives in memory on every server, and every write goes through one leader. If you need more write throughput, you need a different tool, not a bigger ensemble.
  • That a request with no answer did not happen. See 11.2.

What did it cost?

Five things, and every one of them is a deliberate trade.

The cost What it means in practice
every write costs a majority latency is bounded below by the slowest member of the fastest majority, plus an fsync on each of them
the minority side of any partition is dead no writes there, by design
recovery is a full stop no client writes are served between a leader's death and UPTODATE
the data must be small the whole tree is in memory, and the default per-node limit is about 1 MB
freshness is opt-in you accept stale reads, or you pay for sync()

Why can bad luck never break safety?

This is worth stating plainly, because it is the property that makes ZAB operable.

No amount of message loss, delay, reordering or crashing can break P1 or P2. Those failures cost you progress. Elections churn, writes block, sessions expire. They never cost you correctness.

Every safety failure you can produce in this lab's simulator requires you to switch off one of the four rules. None of them can be produced by the network alone.

That asymmetry is what lets you tune ZooKeeper aggressively for latency without ever risking the data.

What did this lab leave out, and how does ZooKeeper handle each?

Eight pieces. Each one is a real production concern that the shop story has no room for.

What makes an acknowledgement honest?

A follower does not acknowledge a proposal until the transaction is in its log and fsynced. This matters more than it sounds. An acknowledgement that could vanish in a power cut turns a majority into a rumour.

forceSync=yes is the default. Turning it off is a way to make ZooKeeper much faster and no longer correct.

Why is a fuzzy snapshot safe?

The transaction log cannot grow forever. So ZooKeeper writes a snapshot of the data tree every snapCount transactions. That default is 100,000, with jitter added so that servers do not all snapshot at once.

These snapshots are fuzzy. They are taken while the tree is still being modified, so they are not a consistent point-in-time image of anything.

That is fine, and the reason it is fine is the thing you learned in section 7.1. Transactions are results, so applying them twice is harmless. Recovery is "load the fuzzy snapshot, then replay the transaction log over it", and the idempotence makes the overlap safe. A design decision and its correctness argument, in one line.

What holds a client's identity?

A client holds a session with a negotiated timeout, bounded by minSessionTimeout and maxSessionTimeout, which default to 2× and 20× tickTime. Ephemeral znodes are owned by a session and deleted when it expires.

This is the machinery behind every ZooKeeper leader-election and lock recipe. The caveat your distributed-locking lab already flagged applies in full: the ephemeral node disappearing is not instantaneous, and a client that is frozen rather than dead still holds its lock until its session actually expires. Use the czxid as a fencing token at the resource. Do not trust the node's existence.

How does a client hear about a change?

A watch is a one-shot trigger a client registers on a znode. It is ordered with respect to the data, so a client is told "this changed" before it can read the new value. Watches are re-registered on reconnect.

How do you add a region without slowing writes?

Use observers, configured with peerType=observer. They are non-voting members. They receive INFORM messages rather than participating in the proposal round.

So they add read capacity, and they let you place a ZooKeeper endpoint in a remote datacentre without putting that datacentre's latency into your write path. This is the right answer to "we need ZooKeeper in three regions": voting members in one or two low-latency regions, observers everywhere else.

How do you change the membership safely?

Since 3.5, reconfig changes ensemble membership safely at runtime. Before that, membership changes meant a rolling restart, with a real risk of an accidental change to what counts as a majority.

What can a cut-off server still do?

With readonlymode.enabled=true, a server cut off from the majority can keep serving reads instead of refusing everything. It is off by default, because it is a deliberate weakening.

How does the leader keep the pipe full?

The leader does not wait for one write to finish before proposing the next. It keeps many in flight and tracks acknowledgements per zxid.

Its own successive transactions still see each other's effects, through an outstanding-changes list. That is why two rapid create sequential calls get different names.

What surprises people about the zxid rollover?

The counter is 32 bits. After roughly 4.3 billion transactions within a single epoch, it would overflow.

🔮 Predict: What does ZooKeeper do when the counter is about to run out?

Reveal

The leader gives up leadership on purpose. That forces an election, which forces a new epoch, and the counter restarts at 1.

The cluster is briefly unavailable for writes for no visible reason. On a busy ensemble that has not had a leader change in a long time, this looks exactly like an unexplained blip.

If you guessed "the counter wraps to zero", that would put a fresh transaction behind every existing one in zxid order, and P1 would break instantly. If you guessed "it widens the field", it cannot — the zxid is a fixed 64 bits, and the epoch owns the other half.

Which inequality decides whether the cluster is stable?

One line. Arjun has it written on a sticky note.

GC pause + fsync latency + network round trip   <   syncLimit × tickTime

Everything on the left is your reality. Everything on the right is your patience. When the left exceeds the right, healthy servers are declared dead and the cluster churns through elections.

Almost every "ZooKeeper is unstable" incident is this inequality being violated by something on the left. The usual culprit is disk or GC, not the network.

The operational rules that follow from everything above:

  • Give dataLogDir its own physical disk. Not a partition — a disk. It is the fsync path.
  • Use an odd number of voting members, spread across failure domains. Three tolerates one failure, five tolerates two. Two tolerates none, and is worse than one.
  • Keep the heap comfortably inside RAM and disable swap. A swapping ZooKeeper is a dead ZooKeeper with extra steps.
  • Do not use it as a datastore. Small values, few nodes. The 1 MB default per-node limit, jute.maxbuffer, is a warning and not a target.
  • Configure autopurge, through autopurge.snapRetainCount and autopurge.purgeInterval, or your disk fills with old snapshots and logs. Historically this is the most common ZooKeeper outage of all.

Which things is this commonly confused with?

Seven pairs, and the one sentence that separates each.

  • ZAB vs Raft. Raft replicates the command and every server computes the answer; ZAB replicates the answer one server already computed — which is why ZAB has to order primaries, not just messages.
  • ZAB vs Paxos. Paxos agrees on one value per independent instance and permits a new leader to fill an earlier gap with a different value; ZAB agrees on an unbroken, never-substituted sequence.
  • ZAB vs two-phase commit. ZAB's broadcast round is 2PC with the abort branch deleted and the decision moved from one coordinator to any majority, which is exactly the cure for 2PC's blocking problem.
  • epoch vs zxid. The epoch is the top half of the zxid. Every zxid contains an epoch; not every use of the epoch involves a zxid, since acceptedEpoch is a bare number.
  • acceptedEpoch vs currentEpoch. "The newest chapter I have agreed to join" versus "the chapter my data belongs to". They differ exactly when a leader died mid-recovery.
  • accept vs deliver. Written down versus acted on. Accepted things can still be deleted; delivered things never can.
  • ZooKeeper vs a database. ZooKeeper holds small facts everyone must agree on, entirely in memory, with every write going through one server. It is not a datastore, and it does not become one with more machines.

ZAB — Solutions

Worked answers. For rungs 7 and 8 the "answer" is a design discussion, not a single output — yours may differ and still be right, but it should engage with the same trade-offs.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 A group of machines has to keep one identical list of changes, where each change was worked out by one machine on top of all the ones before it. The machine doing the working-out will eventually die halfway through. Without a protocol like ZAB, whoever takes over may be missing the last few changes, or may hold changes nobody else has, and either way it starts computing new answers from a wrong picture — so two clients end up being given the same answer, or an answer somebody was already promised quietly disappears.

1.2 Raft replicates the command and every server computes the answer itself; ZAB replicates the answer that one server already computed. Because ZAB's changes are results computed on top of earlier results, it is not enough for the messages to be ordered — the primaries themselves must be ordered, so a new primary must finish delivering everything it inherited before it computes anything of its own. Raft has no such requirement, because any server can recompute the state from the log at any time.

1.3 Correctness: a result is idempotent. Applying ticket = 44 twice, or replaying it over a half-written snapshot, or applying it after a crash-restart, all land in exactly the same state. Applying increment twice does not. This is what lets ZooKeeper take fuzzy snapshots — snapshots taken while the tree is changing — and simply replay the transaction log over them. Recovery: the result is fixed at the moment the leader creates it, so a follower never has to reproduce the leader's computation. Generated values — sequential node names, czxids, session owners, timestamps — are identical on every server without any server needing a matching random seed, clock, or code version. A follower running a slightly different ZooKeeper build still ends up byte-identical.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 Whichever server the pill reads PRIMARY. Its epoch is shown on its row (epoch 1 on a fresh run). acceptedEpoch and currentEpoch are equal once the primary has finished recovery — they differ only in the window between agreeing to a new epoch and a quorum confirming the synchronisation. Catch them differing by clicking a follower during the recovery phases in scenario 2.

2.2 It has the change on disk and has told the primary so, but it has not been told it counts. It must not show it to a client, and it must be prepared to delete it.

2.3 Three of five. quorum = ⌊N/2⌋ + 1. Notice it is three, not five — waiting for everybody would mean one slow machine stops the cluster, which is the tail-latency argument from your quorums-and-replication lab.

2.4 The hatched change is 1.2 on the server that was partitioned with the dying primary. The justifying sentence: fewer than a quorum ever stored it, so no client was ever told it happened, so deleting it breaks no promise — and keeping it would leave one server's list permanently different from everyone else's.

2.5 The dead server — the old primary. It still holds 1.2 un-delivered because it crashed before hearing anything back. Its disk state is intact and untouched; when it restarts it will report 1.2, discover the new primary does not have it, and be told to TRUNC. This is worth noticing: the protocol never reaches into a dead machine. It repairs it on the way back in.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 2.5 reached S1, S2 and S5 (its author) — that is 3 of 5, a quorum, so S5 had already committed it and answered the client. Any electing majority must overlap {S1, S2, S5}, and S5 is dead, so the electing group contains S1 or S2. The winner is the maximum of the electing group, and both S1 and S2 are at 2.5 while S3 and S4 are at 2.4 — so the winner is S1 or S2, and it holds 2.5. Its NEWLEADER round makes 2.5 final again. The write survives, and it is arithmetic rather than luck.

3.2 Now only S1 stored it, and S1 is gone too. S2, S3, S4 are all at 2.4; the highest server id wins the tie, so S4 leads. newEpoch = 3, and its first transaction is 3.1 — new chapter, counter restarts. When S1 returns it reports lastZxid = 2.5; the leader does not have 2.5, so it sends TRUNC to 2.4 followed by a DIFF of everything from 3.1 onward. 2.5 is deleted. No promise is broken, because only one server ever held it, so it can never have been committed.

3.3 It agreed to join epoch 7, but the epoch-7 leader died before bringing a quorum into step — otherwise currentEpoch would have been updated too. During the next election it reports currentEpoch = 5 and its lastZxid, i.e. it honestly says "my data is from chapter 5". It will refuse any message stamped epoch 7 or lower, including from a leader that thinks it is still running epoch 7. That combination — refuse aggressively, report honestly — is exactly why the two numbers cannot be merged into one.

3.4 Any value the primary has committed up to and including 3.9 — so the value at 3.9, or the value at 3.8, or older. All of those are legitimate: a ZooKeeper read is served from the local server's memory with no coordination. What would indicate a bug: a value from a change the primary never committed; a value that goes backwards for that same client (having once seen 3.9, a client must never be served something older — the session tracks the last zxid it saw); or a value that never appeared in any transaction at all.

3.5 The 3-server side elects a new primary at a higher epoch after its followers time out (roughly syncLimit × tickTime) and carries on serving reads and writes. The 2-server side: the old primary can append to its own history but never collects a quorum of acknowledgements, so it commits nothing and answers nobody; after it has been unable to reach a majority for long enough it resigns, and clients there get timeouts and then session expiry. On healing, the old primary's uncommitted tail is TRUNC'd and it joins as a follower.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Breaks P2 (once told, it happened) — a safety failure. The new primary cannot pull because ZAB synchronisation is one-directional by design: the leader pushes, the followers accept. That is what makes recovery one message per follower instead of a negotiation, and it is only safe because the election guarantees the winner is already the maximum. Remove that guarantee and the same one-directional push becomes a one-directional delete: the behind-the-times primary pushes its shorter history onto everybody, and committed changes are hatched out of the whole cluster.

4.2 Breaks P3 (a new primary finishes its predecessor's business first) — a safety failure. The first epoch is immune because there is nothing inherited. The primary's history is empty, so "deliver your inherited history before writing" and "do nothing" are the same instruction. The rule only has teeth at a handover, which is precisely why it is easy to omit and get away with it in testing: a system that is never failed over will never show this bug.

4.3 Breaks P1 (one list everywhere) — a safety failure. The divergent server holds an extra change that nobody else has, but as long as nothing new is written, every server's delivered prefix still agrees — the extra change is just sitting there. The moment the primary writes one more change, that change lands at position 3 on the divergent server and position 2 on everyone else, and now the same query to two servers returns two different answers.

That delay is what makes this class of bug dangerous: the damage is done at time T and only becomes observable at time T + something, by which point the event that caused it has scrolled out of your logs. It is the same shape as a missed fsync — invisible until the power fails.

4.4 Breaks P1 and P2 simultaneously. When the stale change is inserted underneath an already-final change, (a) a change that had been delivered becomes un-delivered, which violates the promise that delivery is permanent, and (b) two servers now hold different things at the same position. Note that the stale transaction's contents are perfectly valid — nothing about the data says it is stale. The epoch in the zxid is the only marker, and one integer comparison is the entire defence.

4.5 The invariant panel reports two servers claiming the same epoch, and then divergence once both sides commit. The counting argument: any two subsets of size ⌊N/2⌋+1 of a set of size N must intersect, because 2 × (⌊N/2⌋+1) > N. For N=5 that is 2 × 3 = 6 > 5 — true. For a threshold of 2, 2 × 2 = 4 ≤ 5 — false, so {S1,S2} and {S3,S4} are disjoint and can each "decide" without ever learning of the other. Half-plus-one is the smallest number that forces intersection, which is why it is not a tunable.

4.6 No invariant chip ever turns red. What degrades is progress: elections churn, followers detect gaps and re-synchronise, writes take longer or fail to commit at all. The principle, which you measured in the raft lab: safety never degrades with message loss; liveness degrades smoothly and then collapses. Election churn is always a timing problem, never a correctness one — which is why "leader elections per minute" is the alarm worth having and "did we lose data" is not a question loss rate can answer.

Rung 5 — Trace

5.1

Election. Votes are (currentEpoch, lastZxid, serverId), largest wins, compared in that order. All are at currentEpoch = 1, so the lastZxids decide: S1 1.3, S2 1.2, S3 1.2, S4 1.4, S5 1.1. S4 wins — it holds the most.

Phase 1 — Discovery. Everyone reports acceptedEpoch = 1; S4 sets newEpoch = 2 and announces it. Each follower writes acceptedEpoch = 2 to disk and replies with its currentEpoch and lastZxid.

Phase 2 — Synchronisation. S4's history is 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 1.4:

Follower reports receives
S1 1.3 DIFF containing 1.4
S2 1.2 DIFF containing 1.3, 1.4
S3 1.2 DIFF containing 1.3, 1.4
S5 1.1 DIFF containing 1.2, 1.3, 1.4

No TRUNC anywhere: every follower's history is a prefix of S4's. Then NEWLEADER(epoch 2); each follower writes currentEpoch = 2 and acknowledges.

End of the NEWLEADER round. With a quorum of acknowledgements, S4 delivers its entire history — 1.1 through 1.4 — and sends UPTODATE. Everyone delivers up to 1.4.

First client write: 2.1. New chapter, counter restarts at 1.

The point of this exercise: 1.3 and 1.4 were never committed by the old primary — they sat un-delivered on one and two servers respectively. They become final anyway, on the new primary's authority, because a quorum has now confirmed they hold exactly this history. ZAB never asks "was this promised?" — it only asks "does the new primary have it?", and the election rule makes those two questions have the same answer whenever it matters.

5.2

S4 is down, so the electing group is {S1, S2, S3, S5} with lastZxids 1.3, 1.2, 1.2, 1.1. S1 wins (1.3 is the maximum present). newEpoch = 2.

Sync: S2 gets DIFF 1.3; S3 gets DIFF 1.3; S5 gets DIFF 1.2, 1.3. NEWLEADER, quorum, deliver 1.1–1.3, UPTODATE. First client write is 2.1.

1.4 is not in the new history — and that is not a violation. Only S4 ever held it. One server is below the quorum of three, so 1.4 can never have been committed, so no client can have been told it happened. Deleting it breaks no promise. P2 is about promises, not about bytes.

5.3

S4 returns while the leader S1 has 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 2.1, 2.2.

S4 reports currentEpoch = 1, lastZxid = 1.4. The leader looks for 1.4 in its own history and does not find it. So: TRUNC to 1.3 (the newest zxid they share), then DIFF containing 2.1, 2.2.

S4's history afterwards: 1.1, 1.2, 1.3, 2.1, 2.2, with 1.4 deleted and recorded as gone. It writes acceptedEpoch = currentEpoch = 2, acknowledges NEWLEADER, receives UPTODATE, and starts serving again.

Worth noticing: S4 was the most up-to-date server in the cluster at the start of 5.1 and is the one that loses data here. Being ahead is not a claim on anything. Only having reached a quorum is.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 — no NEWLEADER acknowledgement round. Violates Agreement (property 3) and P2. Run: S3 wins the election holding 1.1, 1.2, where 1.2 was stored only on S3 before the old primary died. S3 delivers its whole history immediately, so clients reading from S3 now see 1.2, and S3 computes 2.1 on top of it. S3 then dies before any follower has stored 1.2. S1 and S2 (both at 1.1) elect a new primary; 1.2 and everything derived from it never existed for them. Clients were shown a change that the cluster has now unanimously forgotten. The acknowledgement round is what converts "I have this history" into "a majority has this history", and only the second one makes delivery safe.

6.2 — acceptedEpoch rebuilt from the log. Violates the at-most-one-primary-per-epoch invariant, and through it Total order. The whole point of acceptedEpoch is that it can be higher than any epoch present in the log — that is exactly the state of a server that agreed to join a chapter whose leader died before writing anything. Run: cluster at epoch 3. S4 proposes epoch 4; S1, S2, S3 accept, writing acceptedEpoch = 4; S4 dies before any epoch-4 transaction exists. S1 restarts and rebuilds acceptedEpoch = 3 from its log. Now S5 proposes epoch 4 as well, and S1 accepts — so two different servers have been granted epoch 4 by overlapping groups, and two primaries can stamp transactions with the same epoch. Every downstream comparison that assumes "same epoch means same primary" is now wrong. The extra fsync is not overhead. It is the mechanism.

6.3 — ask the leader to resend the missing zxid. Violates Total order (property 2). The design assumes the leader's history is append-only across the gap. TRUNC makes that false. Run: S2 is following a leader at epoch 2 and has 2.4. Its connection drops; it misses 2.5. Meanwhile the epoch-2 leader dies, a new leader at epoch 3 truncates 2.5 away and writes 3.1, 3.2. S2 reconnects, sees the next thing offered is 3.1, notices a gap and asks for 2.5. There is no correct answer: 2.5 no longer exists, and S2 has no way to discover whether its own 2.4 is still a valid prefix. The only procedure that answers "is my prefix still valid, and if not where does it diverge" is the discovery-and-synchronisation handshake — which is precisely why a gap must send the follower back through it rather than trigger a repair.

6.4 — acknowledge from page cache, fsync later. Violates P2 and Agreement. Run: the leader proposes 2.5; S1, S2, S3 acknowledge it out of page cache; the leader commits and tells the client "done". A rack loses power, taking S1, S2 and S3 with it. They restart without 2.5. S4 and S5 never had it. The election picks one of them and 2.5 is gone, though a client was told it succeeded. The argument "a majority are unlikely to lose power at once" is precisely backwards: the failures that matter in practice are correlated — one rack, one PDU, one hypervisor, one availability zone, one bad deploy. An acknowledgement is a durability claim, and a quorum of claims that are not durable is not a quorum of anything.

6.5 — the leader adopts a follower's extra transaction. Violates Primary integrity (property 6) and Total order (property 2). Run: the leader is at epoch 3 with history 1.1, 1.2, 3.1, 3.2 — the epoch-2 tail was truncated during its own recovery. A follower reports 2.7 and the leader adopts it. 2.7 now sorts between 1.2 and 3.1, so on any server that had already delivered 3.1, a change has been inserted underneath something already final, and 3.1 was computed on a state that did not include 2.7. There is also a structural objection: adopting from a follower destroys the invariant that the leader is the maximum, which is the single assumption that makes one-directional synchronisation correct. Once the leader can be behind, every argument in section 12 of the README stops working.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 The retry that issues two tickets

The bad run. A client asks for a ticket. The primary computes 1.2 = "ticket 2", gets a quorum of acknowledgements, delivers it, and dies before its response reaches the client. The client sees a timeout, reconnects to the new primary, and retries. The new primary has delivered its inherited history (so it knows about ticket 2) and computes ticket 3 for the retry. The client walks away with ticket 3; ticket 2 was issued to nobody, or — in the create sequential version of this — two nodes now exist where the client wanted one.

What the server side needs: the ability to recognise a retry as the same request, not a new one. That means a client-supplied identifier carried on the request and remembered by the server, which is the standard idempotency-key shape from your idempotency lab.

What ZooKeeper actually gives you, and it is most of the way there:

  • Conditional operations. create of a specific path fails with NodeExists on a retry; setData with an expected version fails with BadVersion. If your operation is conditional, the retry is naturally safe — this is the event-sourcing expected-version idea again.
  • The gap is sequential creates, because the path is generated server-side and a retry cannot name it. Apache Curator's answer is protection mode (create().withProtection()): it embeds a GUID in the node name, so after a connection loss the client can look for a node bearing its own GUID and adopt it instead of creating a second one. That is a client-side idempotency key implemented with the only tool available — the node name.

The transferable rule: do not try to discover what happened to a request with no answer. Make answering it twice harmless.

7.2 Three servers in DC-A, two in DC-B

Why the sentence is false. Quorum is 3. Lose DC-B and you have 3 — fine. Lose DC-A and you have 2, which is below quorum: no leader can be elected, no writes are served, sessions expire, and every system depending on ZooKeeper stops. So the deployment survives losing DC-B and does not survive losing DC-A. It is not a symmetric design and describing it as one is how people get surprised at 3am.

Worse, a partition between the datacentres — with both alive — produces the same outcome: DC-A keeps running, DC-B is dead, and DC-B's local applications cannot coordinate even though every machine in DC-B is healthy.

Better option 1 — three datacentres, 2 / 2 / 1. Quorum is 3, and any single datacentre's loss leaves at least 3. Cost: every write now needs acknowledgements from two datacentres, so write latency is bounded below by the cross-DC round trip. You need a genuine third site — a small one is enough, since it holds one voting member.

Better option 2 — three datacentres, 1 / 1 / 1. Cheapest way to get real symmetry. Cost: only tolerates one failure, and every write crosses a DC boundary.

Better option 3 — 3 voters in DC-A, observers in DC-B. Writes stay fast (all voters are local) and DC-B gets local reads and a local endpoint. Cost: this does not tolerate losing DC-A at all — it is honest about being asymmetric rather than pretending otherwise, which is already a large improvement.

The rule underneath: count how many voting members are in your largest failure domain. If that number is greater than or equal to the quorum, losing that domain stops everything.

7.3 The lock, the GC pause, and the database

What each party believes. The client creates /locks/recon-0000000031, sees it is the lowest, and begins writing. It then freezes for 60 s. ZooKeeper stops receiving heartbeats, the session times out, and — correctly, and with no error anywhere — deletes the ephemeral node. Client B sees it now holds the lowest node, acquires the lock, and starts writing. Client A wakes up still believing it holds the lock and continues writing. The database sees two writers, both legitimate as far as it can tell.

ZooKeeper did nothing wrong. It cannot distinguish a frozen client from a dead one, and the freeze was longer than the timeout the client itself negotiated.

The fix: fence at the resource. The lock holder passes a monotonically increasing token with every write, and the database rejects any write whose token is lower than the highest it has already accepted. The token comes free: use the czxid of the ephemeral node — ZooKeeper mints it, it never repeats, and it increases with every acquisition.

What the fix requires of the database: it must be able to compare-and-reject on the token atomically — a conditional update, a version column checked in the same statement, or a stored token with a WHERE token > current guard. If the resource cannot do that (a plain filesystem, a third-party API with no conditional write), the lock cannot be made safe, and the honest engineering answer is to make the operation idempotent instead so that two writers are harmless. That is the same conclusion your distributed-locking lab reached: the lock service is not the problem; the resource's inability to check a token is.

7.4 Losing the leader every ninety seconds

The inequality being tested:

GC pause + fsync latency + network round trip   <   syncLimit × tickTime

Diagnostic order, and why:

  1. JVM GC pause distribution on all five servers. First because it is the most common cause, the easiest to measure, and it produces exactly this symptom: a healthy server stops answering for longer than syncLimit × tickTime, is declared dead, and an election starts. Look at max pause, not mean.
  2. fsync latency and disk queue depth on dataLogDir. Second because it is on the critical path of every single write, and because it is the failure that also explains the latency spikes you described — a shared or contended disk gives you both symptoms at once. Confirm dataLogDir is on its own physical device.
  3. Network round-trip time and loss between ensemble members. Third, not first, because it is the thing everybody suspects and rarely the cause. Rule it in or out with actual measurements between the members' addresses, not from your laptop.

Only after those three do you touch configuration. Raising syncLimit and tickTime widens your patience, which stops the churn — but it also lengthens detection time for a genuinely dead leader, so you are trading write availability during real failures for stability during false ones. Do it knowingly, and only once you know which side of the inequality you actually moved.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 Coordination across three regions, 80 ms apart

Membership. Five voting members: 2 / 2 / 1 across the three regions. Quorum is 3, so no single region holds a quorum by itself, and losing any one region leaves at least 3 voters alive. A 3 / 1 / 1 layout would be cheaper but reintroduces exercise 7.2's asymmetry — losing the region with three stops everything.

Observers. Add one observer per region that holds application read traffic, so local reads never cross a region boundary. Observers never widen the write path.

Write latency — the number you must say out loud. A commit needs acknowledgements from 3 of 5 voters, which necessarily includes at least one remote region. So every write costs at least one 80 ms round trip, plus an fsync, and in the worst case the leader's own region is the slow one. Budget 100–200 ms per write and design so that nothing on a user-facing path does one.

Which operations pay it. Only writes. Reads are local and free — and stale, which is fine for a feature flag and not fine for lock ownership. So:

Data Where it lives Why
Merchant → settlement region ownership ZooKeeper, written rarely Small, changes at failover, everyone must agree
Feature flags ZooKeeper, read locally with watches Staleness of a few hundred ms is harmless
Reconciliation lock ZooKeeper ephemeral + fencing token Correctness depends on the token, not the lock
Anything per-transaction Not ZooKeeper One leader, one write path, 80 ms — this is the mistake that kills coordination services

Region failure vs region partition. A failure of the 1-voter region: quorum survives, nothing notices. A failure of a 2-voter region: 3 voters remain, quorum survives, writes continue at higher latency. A partition isolating one region: that region becomes read-only and stale, its clients' sessions expire, and — crucially — its ephemeral nodes disappear, so any lock held there is released and may be granted elsewhere. Design for that: locks must be fenced, and long-running jobs must tolerate losing their lock mid-run.

The reconciliation lock, avoiding 7.3. Acquire an ephemeral sequential node; take its czxid as the fencing token; pass the token into every settlement write; the settlement store rejects writes carrying a token lower than the highest it has seen. The job must be built to be stopped and resumed, because an 80 ms-apart three-region deployment will lose sessions occasionally, and that is not an incident.

What to monitor: leader elections per hour (the leading indicator); zk_synced_followers on the leader (if it sits at exactly 3, you are one failure from a stop); cross-region RTT p99 against syncLimit × tickTime; fsync latency; and, at the application layer, fencing-token rejections — a non-zero rate there is your evidence that the lock alone would not have been enough.

What survives from this lab's model at 80 ms and what does not. The protocol survives entirely — ZAB does not care about latency, only about timeouts being generous enough. What does not survive is the usage pattern: at 2 ms you can afford a ZooKeeper write on a request path, and at 80 ms you cannot. tickTime, initLimit and syncLimit must all be raised, and every one of those makes real failure detection slower. That is the actual trade, and it is not the protocol's fault.

8.2 "Just use PostgreSQL"

What it genuinely gives you. A great deal, and it deserves a fair hearing. One less system to run, patch, monitor and staff. Real transactions across multiple keys, which ZooKeeper does not offer beyond multi. SQL for queries, existing backups, existing access control, a data model with no 1 MB limit. For leader election and simple locks, SELECT ... FOR UPDATE or an advisory lock genuinely works, and a table with a version column gives you fencing tokens directly. Many teams should do this.

What it does not give you.

  • Failure semantics. A single Postgres instance is a single point of failure — exactly the thing the coordination layer exists to route around. With streaming replication and a failover controller, you now need something to decide when to fail over, which is the original problem again. (This is worth saying plainly: consensus does not disappear, it moves.)
  • Session-tied liveness. ZooKeeper's ephemeral nodes tie lock ownership to a live connection, so a dead holder's lock is released automatically. A Postgres row does not know its owner died. Advisory locks tied to a connection get you closer, but connection pools break the connection–client identity that makes it work.
  • Watches. Push notification on change. Replacing it with polling changes your latency and your load profile, and polling at ZooKeeper-like latencies against a database is expensive.
  • Read scale-out with local reads. Every ZooKeeper server answers reads from memory; Postgres replicas can approximate this with their own staleness caveats.

When it is the right call: you already run Postgres with a mature HA story; your coordination needs are locks and a small amount of config; your tolerance for a coordination-layer outage is the same as your tolerance for a database outage (often true — if the database is down, the work stops anyway); and you would rather have one well-understood system than two. That last point is a real engineering argument and not a lazy one.

When it is not: you need dead-client detection without polling; you need many services watching for changes; your coordination layer must survive failures your database does not; or — the decisive one — your database's own failover is itself decided by the thing you are proposing to replace.

8.3 50 GB of configuration in ZooKeeper

Why it fails. The entire data tree is held in memory on every server, and every write goes through a single leader. 50 GB means 50 GB of heap on all five machines, plus room for snapshots and garbage collection.

What breaks first, in order:

  1. GC pauses. Long before you run out of memory, a heap that size produces pauses longer than syncLimit × tickTime — so the servers start declaring each other dead. The cluster churns through elections and becomes unavailable. This is the real answer to "what breaks first", and it is not memory exhaustion.
  2. Snapshot time and size. Every snapCount transactions ZooKeeper serialises the tree to disk. At 50 GB that is minutes of IO, repeatedly.
  3. Server startup and rejoin. A restarted server must load the snapshot and replay the log before it can serve. With initLimit at any sane value it will never finish in time, so it can never rejoin — the loop described in README section 11.5.
  4. The per-node limit. jute.maxbuffer defaults to about 1 MB, so you cannot store large values in individual nodes anyway, and working around it with many small nodes multiplies the node count and the watch load.

What should hold it instead. An object store or a proper database — S3/GCS with versioned objects, or a relational store — with a CDN or local cache in front of it for read volume. Configuration is large, read-mostly, and does not need consensus.

What should still live in ZooKeeper, and why: the pointer and the version. A single small znode holding "the current configuration is version 4711, at this location, with this checksum". That is a few hundred bytes, everyone must agree on it, it changes rarely, and clients can watch it to be told the instant it changes. The heavy bytes live where bytes belong; the one fact everyone must agree on lives where agreement lives.

This is the general pattern and it is worth naming: coordination services store the pointer, not the payload.

8.4 Adding linearizable reads

Mechanism 1 — route reads through the leader. Every read becomes a request to the leader, which answers from its own delivered state. Cost: destroys the read scalability that is ZooKeeper's whole performance story, and makes the leader the bottleneck for reads as well as writes. Not enough on its own either: a leader that has been partitioned away but has not yet noticed will happily serve a stale read, so this needs a quorum check or a lease as well.

Mechanism 2 — a quorum round per read (Raft's ReadIndex). The server asks the leader for its current commit position, the leader confirms with a quorum that it is still the leader, and the reader waits until it has delivered up to that position. Cost: one round trip per read, but it can still be served locally afterwards, and correctness does not depend on any clock.

Mechanism 3 — a leader lease. The leader holds a time-bounded lease and serves reads locally without coordinating. Cost: correctness now depends on bounded clock drift between machines. Your raft lab already recorded this trade — a lease is a bet on bounded clock drift — and google-spanner is the lab where somebody bought special hardware to make that bet safe.

What ZooKeeper actually offers: mechanism 2, as sync(). A sync() is queued to the leader and returns down the same ordered connection, so once it returns the follower has everything the leader had committed when the call was issued. sync() followed by getData() is a linearizable read.

Why it is opt-in rather than default. Because most ZooKeeper reads do not need it, and making everyone pay a round trip for the few that do would remove the reason to choose ZooKeeper at all. The design decision is deliberate: the fast, weak thing is the default, and the strong thing has a name you have to type. Whether that is the right default is a genuine question — etcd made the opposite choice — but it is a choice, not an oversight.
