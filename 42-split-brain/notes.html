Lab 42 · Copies and Agreement

Split Brain — when two machines both believe they are in charge

Tell a friend: Asha and Omar run the two signal boxes at the ends of one railway track, and a single telephone line joins them. One afternoon the line goes dead, and each of them must guess whether the other box is empty or only unreachable. Wait, and no train runs all day; go, and two trains may meet head-on. Computers that share a job face the same silence, and "split brain" is what engineers call it when both sides decide "I am in charge". You cannot stop a machine believing that, so the railway's fix is the one that works: make the track itself check a token, and a wrong belief can never become a crash.

The simulator for this lab is visualization.html, also published as Two Signal Boxes. What the lab is made of, where it sits among your other labs, and how its numbers were produced is in section 17.

1. Why does this problem exist at all?

In one breath: You run a job on two machines so that one of them can die. But now the two must agree which of them is doing the job right now. If both think it is them, the result is not slow. It is wrong.

Meera runs a service on more than one machine so that it survives one machine dying. For that to work, the machines must agree on which of them is currently doing the job. Otherwise two of them do it at once.

Two machines doing the job at once is not merely wasteful. It is the specific way distributed systems produce results that are wrong rather than merely late. Two machines both convinced they are in charge of an account balance will both debit it. Two machines both convinced they own a payment batch will both submit it. Two database servers both accepting writes will build two different versions of history. When they are reconnected, somebody has to decide which version is real. That means somebody has to decide whose data to throw away.

That last sentence is the whole cost. Almost every other distributed-systems failure is recoverable by waiting: the slow node catches up, the queue drains, the retry succeeds. This one is not. When two machines have both acted, no amount of later agreement un-acts either of them.

2. What happens when Asha's telephone goes dead?

In one breath: One track, two signal boxes, one telephone. When the line goes silent, Asha cannot tell "Omar's box burned down" from "the wire is cut". Those two need opposite answers, and she has to pick one.

A single stretch of railway track runs between two towns. One track, both directions. There is a signal box at each end. Asha keeps the London box. Omar keeps the Manchester box. The two of them are connected by one telephone line.

The rule is simple and absolute: only one train may be on the section at a time. So before letting a train in, Asha telephones Omar and asks. If Omar says the section is clear, the train goes. That is the entire protocol, and for decades it worked.

One afternoon Asha picks up the telephone and hears nothing.

She now has to answer a question she has no way of answering. Two things produce exactly this silence:

  1. The Manchester box has burned down. Nobody is there. If she waits for permission she will never get it, and she will stop every train on the line for no reason.
  2. The telephone line is down. The Manchester box is fine, Omar is at his desk, and at this moment he is picking up his own dead telephone and having exactly this same thought.

From where Asha is standing, these are the same event. Silence is the only evidence she has, and it has two meanings that demand opposite responses. The table is her whole dilemma.

Asha's choice If the box burned down If only the wire is cut
Wait for Omar costs the whole day's service, for no reason safe: Omar may have a train on the section
Let the train go fine: the section really is empty two trains on one track, travelling towards each other

🔮 Predict: Asha waits five minutes and rings again. Still nothing. Does the second silence tell her anything the first one did not?

Reveal No. If you said "it is twice as much evidence", you have counted the same silence twice. A cut wire is silent for as long as you care to wait, and so is an empty box. Waiting changes only one thing: how much it costs to have waited. Engineers will meet this again in stage 3 as the *timeout*. It decides how long you wait. It never decides what the silence means.

You have met this exact undecidability before. The gossip-protocols lab put it in one line: death and slowness are indistinguishable from outside. This is the same fact wearing a different hat. This lab is about what it costs when the thing you get wrong is who is in charge, rather than merely who is alive.

The railway industry learned this the expensive way, in Britain, in the nineteenth century. The usual example is the Thorpe collision of 1874, on a single-track line near Norwich. It was caused by a confusion at one end about whether an order had actually been sent, and around two dozen people died. Those details come from background reading, not from a source checked while writing this lab. Treat the date and the details as approximate and look them up before you repeat them anywhere that matters; section 17 lists every claim of that kind. The part that is load-bearing here, and which is certain, is the fix the industry adopted. We come back to it in stage 6, and it turns out to be the same idea your distributed database uses.

3. Why not just take over when the other side goes quiet?

In one breath: Turn Asha and Omar into servers that say "still here" on a timer. If the "still here" stops for too long, the other side takes over. It is the obvious rule, real systems ship it, and it is Asha's wrong guess from stage 2 made automatic.

Meera turns the signalmen into servers. Every server sends a short "still here" message to the others on a timer. Engineers call that message a heartbeat, and its absence is the only failure signal anyone gets. If a server has heard nothing from the one in charge for longer than some limit, it concludes that server is gone and takes over.

This is not a strawman. It is roughly what a great many production systems actually do. It is the default behaviour you get from a naive high-availability setup: a virtual IP address that moves to whichever machine notices the other has stopped answering, a cron job that promotes a replica, a lock in a cache with an expiry.

It is also completely reasonable on its face. The only way a server can learn anything about another server is by receiving messages from it. Absence of messages is the only failure signal available. Acting on that signal is the only way to recover from a real failure at all.

🔮 Predict: Five servers, three in London and two in Manchester, running exactly this rule. The cable between the two cities is cut. Nothing else fails: every server is healthy, every disk is fine, both cities have power. What is the state of the system sixty seconds later?

Reveal

Two servers are in charge, and both are accepting writes to the same database.

Manchester's servers hear nothing from London. Their rule says: take over. So they take over. They are not malfunctioning. They are following the rule correctly, on the only evidence available to them. London, meanwhile, has noticed nothing at all, because its leader is still alive and still leading.

If you said "Manchester waits, because it is the smaller side", you made the guess most people make, and it points at the critical detail. Manchester has no way to know it is the smaller side. It can count the servers it can reach. It cannot count the servers that exist.

4. Where exactly does the obvious rule break?

In one breath: Walk the cut by hand, tick by tick. Every server follows its rule. The database follows its rule. Nobody misbehaves, and the data is still wrong. That condition has a name: split brain.

Let us do that failure slowly, by hand, because the shape of it is the whole subject.

Meera's five servers have numbers. The three in London are S1, S2 and S3. The two in Manchester are S4 and S5. S1 is in charge. Every server knows it. Dev's London app writes through S1. Priya's Manchester app writes through S5, which forwards to S1.

The simulator counts time in ticks. In the traces below, "tick 40" is written t=40.

t=0     S1 in charge.  Everyone agrees.  Database has served writes from S1.
t=40    The cable is cut.  Nothing else changes.
        London still sees: S1 leading, S2 and S3 following.  All fine.
        Manchester sees: silence.
t=80    S4 has now heard nothing for longer than its limit.
        S4 takes over.  It tells S5, which accepts.
        Manchester now has a leader.  London still has a leader.
t=85    The Manchester app writes.  S4 accepts it and sends it to the database.
        The London app writes.  S1 accepts it and sends it to the database.
        The database accepts both.  It has no idea anything is wrong.
t=90+   Both continue.  Every write from one erases the effect of the other.

Nothing in that trace is a bug. Every server applied its rules correctly to the evidence in front of it. The database applied its rule correctly too: somebody who was allowed to write, wrote.

Engineers call this condition split brain: two or more nodes simultaneously believing they hold an exclusive role, and acting on that belief. The name is unflattering and accurate. One system, one job, two independent minds issuing orders, neither aware of the other.

🔮 Predict: Which of these is the true cause of what you just watched: the cut cable, the database that accepted both writes, or S4's decision to take over?

Reveal `S4`'s decision, made under uncertainty. If you said the cable: stage 11 shows the same split brain with every cable intact and a perfectly healthy network. If you said the database: it did exactly what it was told, and the *fix* in stage 6 does live there, but the cause does not. The cable supplied the uncertainty. The decision supplied the damage.

Three things about split brain are worth fixing in your head straight away, because each one is a place where intuition is wrong.

It is not caused by the network. It is caused by a decision made under uncertainty. The network breaking into pieces that cannot reach each other is called a partition, and a partition merely supplies the uncertainty. In stage 11 you will watch split brain happen with a perfectly healthy network and every cable intact.

It is not a bug you can fix by being more careful. No amount of care lets a server distinguish a dead peer from an unreachable one, because there is no observation that distinguishes them. This is a limit, not an oversight. It is the same limit that produces the cap-theorem lab's choice. On a partition you either refuse to answer or you risk answering wrongly. Both of those are decisions you make in advance, not discoveries you make at the time.

The two leaders are not the damage. This is the one that matters most, and we will spend the rest of the lab on it.

5. What do we actually need to guarantee?

In one breath: "Only one server believes it is in charge" is impossible, because beliefs live inside machines you cannot reach. The goal that works is about what lands: only the newest authority's writes may ever take effect.

Here is the trap. The obvious goal is:

At most one server believes it is in charge.

This goal is impossible, and chasing it is why people build fragile systems.

🔮 Predict: Meera pulls S4's network cable out entirely. Is there anything she can do now, from her desk, that changes what S4 believes?

Reveal No. If you said "send it a message telling it to stand down": along which wire? The machines you need to correct are exactly the ones you cannot reach. That is what made them a problem in the first place. The only thing Meera can change from her desk is what the *database* does with `S4`'s writes, and that is stage 6.

You cannot control what a machine believes. A machine's belief is a variable in its own memory, formed from messages it has received. A machine that has been unplugged from the network still holds its old belief perfectly intact. A machine frozen mid-execution wakes up with its old belief unchanged. No message you send can reach a machine you cannot reach, and the machines you need to correct are exactly the ones you cannot reach.

So drop it. Here is the goal that is actually achievable:

At most one authority's actions may ever take effect on the shared resource, and once a newer authority has acted, nothing from an older one may act again.

Read that twice, because four things in it are load-bearing. The table takes them one word at a time.

Word in the goal What it is about What it is not about
effects whether your write lands beliefs; believe whatever you like
shared resource the database, the disk, the payment gateway the cluster's internal opinion of itself
ordered authorities form a sequence that only goes forward, so it is checkable with arithmetic real time
at most one sometimes the honest answer is that nobody may act, and the service is down; that is a correct outcome, not a failure of the design "exactly one"

The distributed-locking lab reached the same place from a different direction and named it: at-most-one-effect-lineage, not at-most-one-holder. If that phrase meant nothing at the time, it should mean something now. It is the same requirement, stated for locks instead of leaders.

6. What is the key idea?

In one breath: You cannot stop a machine believing it is in charge. You can stop its writes from counting. Put the check at the thing being protected, the way the railway put a brass token in the driver's hand.

You cannot stop a machine from believing it is in charge.

You can stop its writes from counting.

So move the decision out of the machines' heads and put it at the thing being protected. The shared resource keeps one number: the identity of the newest authority whose work it has accepted. Every write arrives carrying the identity of the authority that issued it. Anything older than the number is refused.

That is it. That is the entire idea. Everything in the rest of this lab is either a way to produce that number reliably, or a story about what happens to systems that do not have one.

And this is where Asha and Omar's railway comes back. The answer the railways settled on was not a better telephone and not a better rule. It was a physical brass token. One token per section of track. No driver may enter the section without holding it. It cannot be in two places, so two trains cannot both be authorised, no matter what Asha or Omar believes, and no matter what the telephone is doing. The signalmen's beliefs stopped being safety-critical the moment the authority became an object that the track itself could check.

Your distributed system cannot pass a brass object around. It passes a number instead, and the resource checks it. Same idea, one abstraction thinner.

7. What are the words for what you have just watched?

In one breath: Every word below names something that already happened to Asha, Omar, or Meera's servers. Read each one as "the thing from stage N", not as a definition to memorise.

Each of these has been demonstrated above before being named. Minor terms are defined where they first appear later in the document; these are the load-bearing ones. They come in two families: the words for the condition and its causes, and the words for the tools that tame it.

The condition: how do split brain, partition, failure detector and divergence differ?

Split brain is Asha and Omar both letting a train through. In plain words, two parts of one system both think they are the one in charge, and both act on it. Technically, two or more nodes simultaneously hold a role the system's design says is exclusive (leader, primary, lock holder, coordinator) and issue side effects under it. The term exists to name the state of belief, separately from its cause and separately from its damage. Those are three different things, and people use one word for all three. That is why conversations about it go badly. Concretely: at t=85 in the stage 4 trace, S1 and S4 are both leaders. That is split brain. Whether it has hurt anything yet is a different question. The raft lab's blunt correction is the same point: "Raft guarantees one leader" is false; "one leader per term, and only the newest can commit" is true.

A partition is the cut telephone wire. In plain words, the network has broken into pieces that cannot talk to each other. Technically, a set of nodes is partitioned when messages between two subsets are not delivered, while nodes within each subset can still communicate. The word names a cause, and this lab's whole discipline is keeping cause, belief, divergence and loss in separate boxes. At t=40 in stage 4, London's {S1,S2,S3} and Manchester's {S4,S5} become two partitions. It is the P in cap-theorem. Note that a partition is neither necessary nor sufficient for split brain: stage 11 has split brain with no partition, and stage 11 also has a partition with no split brain.

The failure detector is the part of Asha that decides "Omar's box is gone". In plain words, it is the part of the system that decides whether another machine is dead. Technically, it is a component that converts observed silence into a verdict about liveness. It is unreliable by construction: it can report a live node as dead (a false positive) and a dead node as live (a slow detection). The term exists to make that fallibility explicit and give it a name you can argue about, instead of burying a timeout in a config file and calling the result truth. Concretely: "I have heard nothing from S1 for 40 ticks, therefore S1 is dead." A false positive of exactly this kind is the trigger for most real split brains.

The gossip-protocols lab turned this component's one dial all the way in both directions. There is no setting that is good at both columns.

That lab's headline finding is worth re-reading here: at 90% packet loss with zero crashed processes, a 100-node cluster wrote off 100% of its own members. The spreading is robust; the judging is fragile.

Divergence is two versions of the day's train register, each written by a signal box that thought it was the only one writing. In plain words, two copies of the data that disagree, and neither is obviously wrong. Technically, two histories that share a prefix and then differ, both containing writes that were acknowledged to a client. The term exists because divergence is the damage, as distinct from split brain, which is the belief. Split brain with no divergence costs you nothing. Divergence is what somebody has to clean up, by hand, choosing whose data to discard. The pacelc lab's PA/EC corner is exactly this: a database accepts writes on both sides of a partition and then discards the losing branch at heal, MongoDB writing it to a rollback file and moving on.

The tools: what does each of epoch, quorum, lease, fencing and witness buy?

The epoch is the brass token with a number stamped on it. In plain words, a number that goes up by one every time leadership changes, so you can tell whose orders are newer. Technically, a monotonically increasing integer attached to a period of authority. It is a logical clock: it says nothing about real time and everything about order. The term exists because "who is the leader" is not answerable from inside a partition, but "is this order newer than the last one I obeyed" is answerable by one integer comparison, with no timing assumptions whatsoever. Concretely: S1 leads in epoch 1. The cut happens. S4 wins an election and leads in epoch 2. A write arrives at the database carrying epoch 1 after the database has already served epoch 2. Refused, because 1 < 2.

You have now met this object under six names across five labs. It is Raft's term, Paxos's proposal number, ZooKeeper's zxid, etcd's mod_revision, Chubby's sequencer, gossip's incarnation number, and the distributed-locking lab's fencing token. The optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking lab's unification applies here too: a fencing check is a conditional write, with the version column minted by whoever grants leadership. Other names you will meet for the same thing: generation, revision, incarnation.

A quorum needs only a one-line refresher, since you have a whole lab on it. It is a majority, and the only property that matters is that any two majorities of the same set share at least one member. So two majorities cannot both act without at least one node knowing about both. The new fact this lab adds, and it is a big one: a quorum rule governs winning an election. It says nothing whatsoever about keeping the job. A leader elected legitimately last week, now stranded alone on the wrong side of a cut cable, still believes it is the leader. Nothing in the quorum rule tells it otherwise. You will watch this in stage 10, and it surprises most people.

A lease is authority with an expiry date on it. Technically, a grant of a role valid until a wall-clock deadline, which must be renewed against a majority to be extended. A holder that cannot renew must stand itself down when the deadline passes. The term exists to convert "I have not heard that I lost my job" into "my job has expired", which is a statement a stranded node can evaluate alone. Concretely: S4's lease runs to tick 180, written t=180. At t=140 the cable is cut. S4 cannot renew. At t=180 it stands down without anyone telling it to. The catch is the same one as always: a lease is a bet on clocks. It assumes the holder's clock advances at roughly the same rate as everyone else's, and that the holder is actually executing to notice the deadline. A process frozen for longer than its lease notices nothing.

Fencing is two different things, both called fencing, and this causes real confusion between communities, so learn both. A fencing token, in distributed-systems usage, is the epoch check described above: the resource refuses stale work, and nothing is killed. Fencing / STONITH, in high-availability-cluster usage, comes from Shoot The Other Node In The Head. It means physically powering off or isolating the suspected-dead machine: cutting its power via a management controller, or telling the storage array to stop accepting its writes. It converts "I believe you are dead" into "you are dead". Both exist because they solve the same problem for different resources. If the resource can check a number, use a token. If it cannot (a raw shared disk, a virtual IP, an industrial controller) you have no option but to make the other machine stop existing.

A witness, also called an arbiter or tiebreaker, is a small extra voter that stores no data and can never be in charge. Its only job is to make the total number of voters odd. It exists because two sites split evenly cannot produce a majority on either side. A third voter, in a third place, guarantees that at most one side can. The trap: the placement is the whole trick. A witness in one of the two main sites is not a tiebreaker. It is a rule that says that site always wins.

8. Which picture should you carry, and where does it lie to you?

In one breath: The nice picture is the railway with its brass token. The engineer's picture is three separate objects: what nodes believe, the true sequence of authorities, and the gate at the resource. Safety lives only in the gate.

The simple model: what makes Asha and Omar's railway safe?

One track. Two signal boxes. One telephone. One brass token, and no train may enter the section without physically holding it.

The token, not the telephone, is what makes the system safe. The telephone is how Asha and Omar coordinate; the token is what makes miscoordination harmless. If the telephone fails, the worst outcome is that no train runs, because the token is sitting in one box and the other box cannot conjure one up.

The engineering model: which three objects do people merge into one?

Three separate objects that people habitually merge into one:

  1. The belief set. Which nodes currently think they hold the role. This is distributed, unsynchronised, and frequently wrong. You cannot control it.
  2. The authority lineage. A totally ordered sequence of epochs, each with at most one holder. Produced by an election rule (usually a majority vote). It exists as a fact about the system even though no single node can observe it.
  3. The effect gate. A check at the resource that admits work only from the newest authority it has seen. This is the only place where correctness is actually enforced.

Safety is a property of (3). Availability is a property of (2). (1) is noise.

Where the story lies to you

Three places, and each one is the reason a real system needs something the railway does not.

The token is physical there and a number here. A brass token cannot be forged, duplicated, or replayed; the laws of physics enforce the invariant for free. A number enforces nothing by itself. It only works if the resource actively checks it on every operation. Send a perfectly correct token to a resource that does not check, and you have exactly as much protection as sending nothing. This is not a quibble. It is the single most important practical caveat in this lab, and stage 11 shows it in numbers.

The railway has out-of-band evidence. Asha can walk down the line, look out of the window, send a runner, or telephone a third station. She has senses beyond the one channel. A server has exactly one way to learn anything about another server, and when that fails it is blind. This is why real systems add indirect probes, meaning "can anyone else reach S4?". The gossip-protocols lab put numbers on that trick.

The railway has two parties and one resource. That means it has no notion of a majority; with two parties there is no majority to be had. Real clusters have N parties. That is what makes voting available as a tool at all. It is also why the answer to "how do I make a two-node cluster safe?" is very often "add a third machine". That sounds like a dodge and is in fact the entire answer.

🔮 Predict: Meera has exactly two servers, one in London and one in Manchester. Which single change makes them safe against a cut cable: a faster heartbeat, a longer timeout, or a third machine in Bristol?

Reveal The third machine. A faster heartbeat only makes Asha's silence arrive sooner. A longer timeout only makes her wait longer before making the same unanswerable guess. Neither gives the two sides any way to know which of them is the larger half, because with two there is no larger half. A third voter creates one. If you said "longer timeout", you have chosen where on the availability curve to sit, which is a real choice (stage 13 makes it), but it is not safety.

Real systems ship them in every combination. etcd's mod_revision is a fencing token with no lease anywhere near it.

✅ Checkpoint: you now have the BEGINNER MODEL

You can explain what split brain is, why it cannot be prevented by trying harder, and why the interesting question is about effects rather than beliefs. You could hold your end of a conversation about it.

What the next layer adds: the actual mechanism, built one defence at a time, with each defence introduced after you have watched the previous one fail. This is where you find out that the defence most people reach for first, majority quorum, is not sufficient on its own, and exactly what it is missing.

The cost first. There is a mandatory dead window on every failover: the new leader is elected and then deliberately does nothing while the old lease drains. You have bought safety with unavailability, and the bigger the lease, the more you buy of each.

Now the assumption, which is the dangerous part. A lease is a bet that the holder's clock advances at the same rate as everyone else's, and that the holder is actually executing to notice the deadline. Both halves of that can fail. A virtual machine suspended and resumed comes back with a clock that jumped. A process in a long garbage-collection pause is not executing at all, so it cannot notice anything. And here is the sharp bit: it cannot heartbeat either, which is precisely why its lease expired. The mechanism that is supposed to stop it is disabled by the same event that makes stopping it necessary.

Two things to take from that trace, and the second is more important than the first.

There was no partition. Not a single cable was cut. Split brain is not a network phenomenon. It is a decision-under-uncertainty phenomenon, and a frozen process supplies exactly the same uncertainty as a broken cable.

And be honest about this: what happened between t=100 and t=107 was a race. S1's stale write and S2's heartbeat were both in flight, and the write won by seven ticks. Run it with different timing and sometimes the heartbeat wins and nothing bad happens at all.

That is not reassuring. It is the problem. A defence that works most of the time is a defence you cannot reason about. You cannot write down what your system guarantees. You cannot test it, because the bad case does not show up on demand. You find out which way the race went from a customer. In production this race is decided in milliseconds you do not control, on a network you do not own.

We need a rule with no timing in it at all.

v4: what if the database checks the token?

Every write carries the epoch of the authority that issued it. The database keeps one number: the highest epoch it has served. Anything lower is refused. This is the brass token from stage 6, as a number, checked by the track.

Look carefully at that table, because it is the thesis of the lab in four rows.

Fencing did not prevent split brain. Two servers still believed they were in charge. The left number went to two, and it always will. What fencing did was make the belief inert. S1's write arrived carrying epoch 1 at a database that had already served epoch 2, and bounced. S1 went on believing it was the leader right up until its next heartbeat. The lease buys safety by making everybody wait. Fencing buys safety by letting everybody proceed and refusing the ones that turn out to be stale.

The reason is worth stating precisely, and it is the sentence the distributed-locking lab arrived at too:

Safety by arithmetic, liveness by timeout — never the reverse.

The token check has zero timing assumptions. It does not care about clock drift, GC pauses, VM suspension, network delay, or how long a packet sat in a queue. It is one integer comparison, and it is correct under every possible schedule. The timeout is still there. You still need one to decide when to try a takeover, because slow-versus-dead is undecidable and something has to break the tie. But the timeout is now only deciding how fast you recover, not whether you are correct.

Both are still worth having together, for different reasons. Fencing is what makes it safe. A lease is what stops a stranded leader from serving stale reads and burning capacity forever.

Reveal None. If you said "some, because the tokens are still right", you have placed fencing in the sender. It lives in the receiver. A correct token that nobody reads is a brass token left on the platform.
Counter Fencing, checking resource Fencing, non-checking resource What it means

All the protection is gone. Every bit of it.

This is not a contrived setting. It is the everyday situation for an enormous proportion of real side effects:

  • a payment gateway that takes a charge request and charges the card,
  • an SMTP server that takes an email and sends it,
  • a third-party API with no conditional-update support,
  • a raw block device shared between two machines,
  • a physical actuator.

None of these vote. None of these have any idea a cluster exists. And that leads to the sentence that generalises everything above:

A quorum protects the voters. Anything that is not a voter gets no protection from consensus at all, unless it independently refuses stale work.

Your five servers can agree perfectly about who is in charge. The payment gateway was not in the room, was not asked, and will cheerfully charge the card twice.

For those resources you have exactly three options, and you will use all three:

  1. Make the effect idempotent. Attach a client-generated key to the operation, and have the receiver deduplicate on it. Then a duplicate from a superseded leader still acting, a zombie, costs nothing, because the second attempt is recognised as the first. This is the idempotency lab's entire subject, and this is why it matters here.
  2. Make the machine stop existing. STONITH: power it off through a management controller, or have the storage array refuse its writes. This is what high-availability clusters do when the resource is a raw disk, because there is nothing else available. It has its own failure mode, which is stage 11.
  3. Reconcile afterwards. Accept that duplicates happen, detect them later, and correct. Slow, human, and often the only honest answer.
Option Works for What it costs
Idempotency key any receiver that can remember keys the receiver must keep and check them
STONITH a raw disk, a virtual IP, an actuator a new failure mode, the fence race of stage 11.9
Reconcile afterwards anything at all slow, human, and after the fact

11.8 What about the write that was already in flight?

This one is hard to stage deliberately; it is the general case of 11.7.

A leader validates that it is still the leader, and then sends a write. Between those two instructions the world can move. The write can sit in a queue, the leader can be paused, the lease can expire. The write lands after the leader has lost authority, carrying credentials that were valid when it left. It is Omar's train, already on the section, when the token changes hands.

The distributed-locking lab hit exactly this, and it was the most invisible failure in that lab. A pause shorter than the TTL still lost an update, because the hold includes the write's travel time. Its fix generalises here: fence reads too. A read presents its token and raises the resource's floor, so older in-flight writes bounce. Chubby checks sequencers on every request, not just writes, for this reason.

The general principle: a defensive check before acting cannot protect you, because the gap between the check and the act is unbounded. The check must be at the point of effect, performed by the thing being affected, atomically with the effect.

11.9 What happens when both machines shoot each other?

Stage it by turning on "shoot the loser" with no quorum requirement, then cut the link.

🔮 Predict: STONITH is on, with no quorum gate. The cable is cut. Asha's box and Omar's box each have a button that cuts the other's power. What is the best case?

Reveal A total outage. If you said "one side wins", you assumed the buttons are pressed one at a time. In a genuine two-way race both are pressed. And if the wire that carries the power-off command is the same broken wire, neither press lands, and you get split brain *plus* two machines certain they have just killed each other.

Both sides conclude the other is dead. Both issue a power-off. If both succeed, the entire cluster is down: a total outage manufactured by the safety mechanism. If the fencing path itself runs over the broken network, neither succeeds and you have split brain plus two machines convinced they have just killed each other.

There is a worse variant. Each machine reboots the other; the rebooted one comes up, concludes the first is dead, and shoots back. A pair of servers can ping-pong indefinitely.

In production this is a well-known problem in Pacemaker/Corosync clusters, and the standard mitigations are exactly what you would now predict. Gate fencing behind quorum, so only a majority side is permitted to shoot. And add a randomised or asymmetric delay before firing, so that in a genuine two-way race one side reliably wins. If that sounds familiar, it is the same trick as Raft's randomised election timeouts, which the raft lab put numbers on.

11.10 What happens when Ben presses the override?

Counter Value What it means

Ben is on call. Every runbook has this button. It exists for a real reason: when a site genuinely burns down, the survivors cannot reach a majority and someone must manually grant them the right to proceed. Without it, a real disaster is unrecoverable.

The problem is that the button cannot tell whether the other site is dead or merely unreachable. Neither can Ben, because he is looking at the same silence Asha was. The button does not talk to London; it cannot, that is the whole situation. It only changes what Manchester requires of itself. London carries on with a perfectly valid majority, having noticed nothing.

No consensus protocol defends against this, because it is not a bug in the protocol. It is a human removing the protocol.

In production, treat this as a physically dangerous operation.

  • Require the operator to state, in the runbook and in writing, how they know the other site is down: a specific out-of-band observation, not an inference from the same silence the cluster is looking at.
  • Prefer procedures that fence the other side first and only then grant quorum: revoke its storage access, drop its BGP session, shut its VMs down through the hypervisor API. Make the assumption true before you rely on it.
  • Instrument it. An override should page somebody, not appear in a log.

11.11 What happens when the cable comes back?

Stage it by cutting the link with no defences, running, then setting the cut back to "nothing cut". The simulator lets you do this mid-run.

🔮 Predict: The cable is restored. How long until only one server believes it is in charge: the rest of the lease, or a few ticks?

Reveal A few ticks. If you said "until the lease expires", you expected a timer. None is involved: one server simply answered another, and the lower epoch stood down. Then ask the harder question: what did *not* heal?

Two leaders become one within a few ticks. The moment messages flow, the leader with the lower epoch hears from a higher one and stands down at once. Notice that no timer was involved. One server simply answered another. Most split brains end in milliseconds, not at the end of a lease.

But healing the belief is trivial and healing the data is not. The writes that both leaders accepted during the split are still there, interleaved, and one branch has to be discarded. The cluster returns to health while the damage remains. Asha and Omar are back on the telephone, and there are still two trains on the section.

In production this is where MongoDB writes a rollback file: the losing branch's documents, dumped to disk as BSON under the data directory, and then the node moves on. Those writes were acknowledged to clients. Almost nobody ever reads those files. The pacelc lab put this system in the PA/EC corner for exactly this reason. It pays the worst everyday latency bill and discards acknowledged writes at heal.

12. What is guaranteed, what is not, and what did it cost?

In one breath: Three promises hold. Only one server can win any one election. The database never goes backwards. And a healthy majority eventually gets work done. Everything else you might have hoped for is not promised.

Meera wants the guarantees written down. Not the version for the architecture diagram. The version she could defend in a design review, or in an incident report at two in the morning.

There are exactly three, and each is smaller than it sounds.

Which three promises actually hold?

The first promise is one winner per election. Watch Omar's S4 in Manchester campaigning through stage 10. Its epoch climbs to 8 over the run, and it never once wins. It cannot. Any two majorities of five share at least one member, and a server votes at most once per epoch. So two winners in the same epoch would need one server to have voted twice, and no server does. Engineers call this election safety.

Now the part people skip. That promise says nothing about how many servers are acting as leader right now. Leaders from different epochs coexist happily, and in stage 11.2 two of them did exactly that. This gap is the reason everything else in the lab exists.

The second promise is that the database never goes backwards. Once it has served a write from epoch 2, no write from epoch 1 will ever land again. The reason is dull and that is its virtue: the resource keeps one number and compares. Pure arithmetic. No clocks, no timeouts, no assumption about how long a packet sat in a queue.

It rests on one condition, and the condition is about the resource, not about your cluster. The check must actually happen, atomically with the write. That is Priya's payment gateway from v5, and it is the assumption most often false.

The third promise is that a majority eventually gets work done. If a majority of servers can reach each other and the database, and the network holds still for long enough, somebody is elected and progress happens.

"Long enough" is carrying real weight in that sentence. Under a continuous partition, or the endless election churn of 11.5, nothing at all is promised. That is not a defect in the design. FLP says no deterministic protocol can guarantee both safety and liveness in an asynchronous system with faults. So you keep safety unconditionally, and you buy liveness with timing assumptions.

Here are the three side by side. The last column is the one to read twice.

The promise Its real name Why it holds What it does not say
One winner per election election safety majorities intersect; one vote per server per epoch anything about how many are acting as leader now
The database never goes backwards effect ordering a monotonic floor, compared with arithmetic anything, if the resource does not perform the check
A majority eventually makes progress liveness under a majority enough servers can reach each other and the resource anything under continuous partition or election churn

What is nobody promising you?

Five things, and each one is a promise somebody has assumed they had.

  • That only one server believes it is the leader. Never. Not achievable. Stop designing as though it were. Asha cannot reach into Omar's head, and Meera cannot reach into S4's memory.
  • That an acknowledged write survives. A write acknowledged by a leader that is later superseded may be discarded. "The server said OK" and "this is durable" are different statements, separated by the replication policy. The quorums-and-replication lab's acks=all plus min.insync.replicas finding is this same point, seen from the storage side.
  • That the minority side gets an error. It may get a confident, cheerful, completely stale success. The pacelc lab's whole else-clause is this: someone waits — the writer, the reader, or nobody; and if nobody waits, somebody gets stale.
  • Protection for a resource that does not check tokens. Zero. Not reduced. Zero.
  • Anything at all under Byzantine faults. Everything here assumes servers crash, freeze, or become unreachable. A server that lies about its epoch defeats all of it.

What did the guarantees cost?

Nothing above was free. Each defence bought one property and charged for it in a different currency.

Bought Paid with
No two leaders elected in one epoch Progress requires a majority: below that, the service is down by design
A stranded leader stops on its own (lease) A mandatory dead window on every failover; a bet on clocks; fewer writes served, 68 against 79
Stale effects refused (fencing) The resource must be able to check, and every operation costs one comparison
A tiebreaker for two sites (witness) One more machine, and a placement constraint that is easy to get wrong
Certainty that a peer is dead (STONITH) A new failure mode where both sides shoot, and a dependency on the fencing path

Which did you lose — safety or liveness?

These two are independent here, and keeping them apart is the single most useful diagnostic habit in this lab.

The raft lab pushed both directions with numbers. Safety never degraded at any message-loss rate it tried, including 90%. Liveness degraded smoothly and then fell off a cliff.

Message loss Commits, out of 30 attempts What it means
30% 30/30 liveness essentially intact
50% 7/30 liveness degrading fast
70% 0/30 liveness gone, and safety still perfect

So when something goes wrong, ask first which of the two you lost. The answer usually inverts your instinct.

A cluster that has stopped serving is usually working correctly. A cluster that is serving happily may be the one destroying your data.

What do the named systems actually do?

Ten systems, in the order you are most likely to meet them. Read each one looking for the same three things: what mints the epoch, whether the resource checks it, and where the sharp edge is.

etcd. Raft underneath. The piece to know is that mod_revision is a fencing token you already have and may not have noticed. A transaction of the form "compare mod_revision of key K to R, and if equal, put" is a fenced write with no extra machinery bolted on. Leader election runs through Campaign on a lease. Turn pre-vote on, for the reason 11.5 gave you.

ZooKeeper. ZAB underneath, and zxid is the epoch. The classic recipe is an ephemeral sequential node: the lowest sequence number is the leader, and the session's ephemeral node disappears when the session expires. Now the critical part. The znode version is what you present to the resource. The ephemeral node vanishing is not, on its own, fencing. Your side effects still have to carry the version and something still has to check it.

Consul. Raft plus sessions. A session invalidates when a health check fails, and that releases the lock key. Same caveat as ZooKeeper: releasing the lock does not stop work already in flight, which is exactly 11.8.

Redis Sentinel and Redis Cluster. Replication is asynchronous. Writes the old master acknowledged, and had not yet replicated, are lost when it is demoted. The redis lab put a number on that: 500 acknowledged writes, 500 lost. The settings min-replicas-to-write and min-replicas-max-lag narrow the window and cannot close it. The distributed-locking lab supplies the other half of the warning. SET k v NX PX is an efficiency lock, not a correctness one. A pause longer than the TTL puts two holders in the critical section. Redlock's five servers do not help, because the client's pause did not ask them anything. Redisson's RLock has a TTL and watchdog renewal and no fencing token.

MongoDB. Replica sets with a majority-elected primary, plus arbiters as the witnesses of 11.4. Writes not replicated before a failover are rolled back into files under the data directory: Asha's discarded train register, from 11.11. Since 5.0 the default write concern is majority, and that default is what makes an acknowledged write actually survive a failover. If your deployment is older, or somebody overrode it, go and look.

Elasticsearch. The canonical cautionary tale, and the one to tell in interviews. Before 7.0 you had to set discovery.zen.minimum_master_nodes to (master_eligible / 2) + 1 by hand. Set it wrong and you got split brain and real data loss. Forget to update it when you added nodes and you got the same. Enough people got it wrong that 7.0 replaced the whole discovery layer with a coordination subsystem that computes quorums itself, and removed the setting. The lesson generalises well beyond search: a safety parameter a human must compute correctly is a safety parameter that will be wrong.

Kafka. The controller has an epoch. Partition leaders have a leader epoch too, added by KIP-101, specifically because followers could otherwise diverge from a leader's log after an unclean failover. The setting unclean.leader.election.enable=false is the default, and it means: do not promote a replica that was out of the in-sync set, even if the alternative is unavailability. That is the CP choice made explicit in a config flag. And the transactional producer fences zombies with a producer epoch. That is the brass token appearing three times inside one product.

Kubernetes. Controllers elect through a Lease object in coordination.k8s.io, using optimistic concurrency on resourceVersion. Note carefully what that gives you and what it does not. It gives you a lease. It does not give you fencing on your controller's side effects. If your controller calls an external API, a paused leader can still make that call after losing the lease. That is Priya's payment gateway from v5 wearing a Kubernetes badge. Your side effects need to be idempotent regardless.

Patroni, for PostgreSQL. The leader key lives in etcd, Consul or ZooKeeper with a TTL, and this one gets the hard part right. It offers an optional hardware watchdog on the leader machine, so a node that fails to renew is rebooted by its own watchdog timer. That is self-fencing. It is the correct answer for a resource — a Postgres data directory — that cannot check tokens itself.

Pacemaker and Corosync. The high-availability-cluster world, where "fencing" means STONITH. You will meet quorum-gated fencing, no-quorum-policy, two_node with wait_for_all, and pcmk_delay_max to avoid the fence race of 11.9. If you ever wondered why HA-cluster people are so insistent about configuring STONITH before anything else, here is the answer. Their shared resource is usually a raw disk, a raw disk cannot check a token, and shooting the other node is the only mechanism they have.

What did forty-three seconds cost GitHub?

GitHub's outage of October 2018 is the canonical published example of the healing problem from 11.11.

From their public post-mortem: roughly 43 seconds of lost connectivity between the US East Coast network hub and the primary East Coast data centre. Their MySQL orchestration failed the cluster over to the West Coast. Writes had been accepted on the East Coast during those seconds and had not replicated west. Once the West Coast cluster began taking writes, the two histories had diverged. Neither branch could be discarded without losing real customer data, so they could not simply fail back. The site ran degraded for over 24 hours while the two sets of writes were reconciled.

Forty-three seconds of network trouble. More than a day of consequences. That ratio is the thing to carry away.

The partition is brief and the divergence is permanent.

It is Asha and Omar's afternoon at the scale of a company. The telephone line came back quickly. The two train registers did not merge themselves.

How tight should the timeout be?

There is one inequality behind every design in this lab, and the raft lab put numbers on it.

broadcastTime  ≪  electionTimeout  ≪  MTBF

In words: a heartbeat must cross the cluster far faster than a leader is presumed dead, and a leader must be presumed dead far faster than machines actually fail. Squeeze the middle term towards the left and the cluster starts electing on noise.

What was tried What happened What it means
heartbeat every 140 ticks, election timeout 150–300 stable comfortably inside the inequality
heartbeat every 200 ticks, same timeout 48 terms per 10,000 ticks the middle term collapsed into the left one
etcd's shipped defaults roughly a 10× ratio the same inequality, chosen for you by people who tested it

Too tight and you get false failovers on every garbage-collection pause. Too loose and real failures take minutes to notice. The honest framing is that you are choosing where on the availability curve to sit. You are not searching for a correct value, because there is not one.

What did this lab deliberately leave out?

Six things, each of which has a lab of its own or deserves one.

  • Log replication and repair. The simulator's election has no log-completeness check, so it cannot show how a new leader recovers entries. That is the raft lab, which has the numbers: a follower six entries behind costs six rejections, and a follower with five wrong entries costs zero.
  • Persistence of the epoch. Ours lives in memory. In reality it must be on disk before use. The distributed-locking lab planted exactly this bug on purpose: a restart reset the counter to zero, and legitimate writers were fenced off until it climbed back. Safety kept, availability lost.
  • Membership changes. Adding or removing a voter changes what "majority" means. Doing it naively creates a window in which two different majorities exist. Real systems use joint consensus or single-server changes.
  • Byzantine faults. A node that lies about its epoch defeats everything here.
  • Client-side behaviour in full. Our apps do one redirect hop. Real clients cache the leader's identity, and a client holding a stale leader address is its own source of writes to a zombie.
  • Message loss and reordering. The simulator delivers reliably, and only the faults you press exist. Real networks supply their own.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Split brain is when two machines that are supposed to take turns being in charge both think it is their turn, at the same time, and both act on it.

You cannot fix it with a more careful failure detector because the detector is being asked an unanswerable question. The only evidence one machine has about another is messages arriving. When they stop arriving, two completely different situations produce identical evidence: the other machine died, or the path between you broke. There is no observation that separates them — not a longer timeout, not more retries, not a smarter algorithm. A longer timeout makes false positives rarer and real failures slower to detect; it does not make the question answerable. This is a limit on what is knowable, not a gap in the implementation.

1.2 Wrong because you cannot control what a machine believes: belief is a variable in its own memory, it survives being unplugged, it survives being frozen, and the machines you would need to correct are precisely the ones you cannot reach.

The goal that replaces it: at most one authority's actions may ever take effect on the shared resource, and once a newer authority has acted, nothing from an older one may act again. Effects, not beliefs; ordered, so it is checkable by comparing two integers; and at most one, because "nobody may act" is sometimes the correct answer.

1.3

  • Partition — the network has broken so that two groups of machines cannot reach each other. A cause.
  • Split brain — two machines simultaneously believe they hold a role that is supposed to be exclusive, and act on it. A state of belief.
  • Divergence — two versions of the data that share a prefix and then differ, both containing writes that were acknowledged to somebody. The damage.
  • Data loss — somebody has discarded one of those branches. A later, separate, usually manual event.

They are independent: you can have a partition with no split brain (a well-designed cluster), split brain with no divergence (fencing working), and divergence with no partition (a frozen process).

1.4 Prevention would require stopping a machine from holding a belief, which is impossible, so any design aiming at it is aiming at something it cannot reach and will fail in ways it did not plan for. Harmlessness is achievable with one integer comparison performed by the resource, and — the part people miss — it is cheaper in availability as well. The measured comparison in stage 10: a lease reaches one leader by making everyone wait and serves 118 writes; fencing leaves two leaders in existence and serves 133, with the same zero damage. Prevention costs you availability to buy something you cannot have; harmlessness gives you correctness and keeps more of your service running.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 Two servers believe they are in charge: S1 in London, still in epoch 1, and a Manchester server in epoch 2. The database reports it will accept anything — "nothing" as its floor — because fencing is switched off in this scenario, so writes carry no token for it to compare. That is exactly why the damage counter climbs: the database has no basis on which to refuse anybody.

2.2 The split brain begins when the new leader wins its election, roughly 40 ticks after the freeze starts (one silence limit). It ends when the new leader's next heartbeat reaches S1 after S1 wakes — typically within about 7 ticks of waking.

It was ended by a message arriving, not a timer. S1 saw an epoch higher than its own and stood down immediately. This is worth internalising: most split brains in real systems end in milliseconds, as soon as any packet gets through. The dangerous window is not "until the lease expires" — it is "until the first message crosses", which is usually much shorter and occasionally much longer.

2.3 A hollow square is a refusal. It was sent by S1 carrying epoch 1, and the database had already served epoch 2. The comparison: 1 < 2 → refuse. That single comparison is the entire safety mechanism, and note what is not in it: no clock, no timeout, no assumption about how long anything took.

2.4 S1's inspector still says role: leader, epoch: 1, and "believes: that it is in charge" — right up until the heartbeat reaches it. That is the whole point of the two counters. The left counter is reading S1's belief, which is wrong and which nothing could have prevented. The right counter is reading the database's behaviour, which is correct. Belief and authority are separate things, and only one of them is worth defending.

2.5 In the non-transitive run the epochs climb to around 6 within 400 ticks; on a healthy cluster the epoch stays at 1 for the entire run. The difference is election churn: nobody can hold the job, because the witness that both sides can reach keeps voting for whoever bids higher. It is a symptom of a partition that is not a clean split — and the reason to alarm on election rate rather than on partitions, since nothing here reports itself as broken.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 Manchester's application gets errors, not stale successes. Its servers cannot form a majority, so no Manchester server is ever elected, and a follower with no reachable leader has nothing to forward a write to. This is the CP behaviour: the minority refuses rather than guesses.

Worth noting what would change the answer. If the system served reads from local replicas without a leader check, Manchester would get stale successes on reads while erroring on writes — which is the far more common real-world shape, and why a "read from any replica" setting is a consistency decision disguised as a performance one.

3.2 Manchester's servers receive nothing from London, so their failure detectors fire and they campaign. Their vote requests do reach London, so London's servers see a higher epoch, step down, and vote — but their votes never come back. The result: Manchester never wins, and London's leader has been repeatedly unseated by vote requests it can hear but cannot answer.

This is the nastiest case in the whole lab: a node that cannot receive is invisible to itself and disruptive to everyone else. London absolutely notices — it loses its leader repeatedly — while the cause is entirely in Manchester and shows up nowhere in Manchester's own metrics. This is what pre-vote exists to stop.

3.3 London wins, always, in every partition. That arrangement is not a tiebreaker. It is a configuration that says "London is the primary site and Manchester is a warm standby that can never take over", which may well be what you want — but you should know that is what you have built, because it means a London outage is a total outage that no automatic failover will rescue.

The witness must be able to fail independently of both main sites for it to be a tiebreaker. That is a physical constraint, not a config value.

3.4 A 30-tick freeze with a 40-tick limit: zero damage — nobody times out, so no election happens, so there is no second authority. A 70-tick freeze: damage, measured at 2 writes.

The rule separating them is "is the freeze shorter than the silence limit". You should trust it about as far as you can throw it. It requires you to know your worst-case process stall in advance, and the distributed-locking lab put a number on how well that goes: "longer than my longest pause" is a number nobody has. Your longest pause is set by the garbage collector, the hypervisor, the disk and your noisiest neighbour, none of which consulted you. This is a timing bet, and stage 10's point is that you want a rule with no timing in it at all.

3.5 Two servers believing they are in charge, and zero damage. The stranded incumbent keeps its belief until its lease expires, and meanwhile its writes bounce off the database's epoch floor. That combination — belief duplicated, authority not — is the healthy shape of split brain and the target you should be designing for.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 The damage disappears, because the old leader's lease has not expired by the time it wakes, so its authority is arguably still valid and the new leader has not yet started acting.

You paid for it with a longer mandatory dead window on every single failover — the new leader must wait out the whole lease before touching anything, on every leadership change, forever, including the ordinary ones. And it is not a real fix, for two reasons. First, you have set a timing parameter based on a freeze length you happened to choose; a freeze one tick longer defeats it. Second — and worse — a longer lease means a longer period during which a genuinely dead leader has not yet been replaced. You have made the rare bad case less likely and the common case slower. That is the lease's whole trade, and it is why fencing is the better instrument: fencing has no parameter to get wrong.

4.2 Better: far fewer false failovers. A GC pause or a slow disk no longer triggers an election, so the cluster is much more stable, epochs stay flat, and you stop generating split brains for no reason.

Worse: a genuinely dead leader is now unnoticed for up to 90 ticks, so every real failure costs a much longer outage.

You traded liveness for liveness, not safety for liveness — and that is the subtlety worth naming. Safety was never on the table here: with fencing on, no setting of this dial can corrupt anything. What you are choosing between is false-failover churn and real-failure detection latency. Both are availability. This is exactly why the raft lab's finding matters — election churn is always a timing problem, never a correctness one.

4.3 Routing changes how much damage occurs and, in some configurations, whether it occurs at all — but it never changes whether the system is safe. If both apps talk to servers in the same city, only one authority is receiving writes and no divergence appears, even though split brain exists. Point them at different cities and both authorities are fed.

So client routing is a liveness and blast-radius matter. It determines who gets served and how bad a split brain becomes; it cannot make an unsafe design safe. Which is why it must never be load-bearing: a design that is only safe because of where the load balancer happens to point is a design that breaks when someone edits the load balancer.

4.4 Two servers believe they are in charge (quorum is off, so both sides take over), and zero damage (fencing refuses the older one). Writes served will be noticeably lower than the healthy baseline, because one side's writes are all bouncing.

This is the cleanest demonstration in the lab of the division of labour:

  • Fencing does the safety work. It alone reduces the damage to zero, with no quorum anywhere in the system.
  • Quorum does the availability work. It stops the losing side from taking over in the first place, so that side's clients get a clean error instead of a stream of refused writes, and so the cluster does not thrash.

Neither substitutes for the other. If you can only have one, and correctness is what you care about, fencing is the one that protects your data.

4.5 Writes served drop sharply. A 10-tick lease must be renewed constantly, and every renewal round-trip that is even slightly delayed causes the leader to stand itself down, triggering a fresh election and a fresh mandatory quiet period before the new leader may act. You spend the cluster's time on leadership rather than work.

The general shape: lease too long, real failures take too long to recover; lease too short, you fail over constantly and pay the quiet period every time. There is no setting that is good at both, which is the same conclusion the gossip lab reached about its detection dial.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 The pre-write check dies in its own gap.

A concrete run: worker A holds the lock, TTL 30 s, renewing every 10 s. At t=0 A checks the lock and sees it holds it. At t=0.001 A's JVM enters a 35-second stop-the-world pause. Its renewal thread is also stopped — it cannot heartbeat, because being stopped is exactly what makes the lease expire. At t=30 the lock expires. At t=31 worker B acquires it and starts writing. At t=35 A resumes, completes the instruction after its check, and writes.

Both wrote. The check was true when performed and false when it mattered.

Violated: mutual exclusion, and through it whatever invariant the lock protected.

The general principle: a check before acting cannot protect you, because the gap between the check and the act is unbounded. The check has to be performed by the thing being affected, atomically with the effect. This is the distributed-locking lab's measured result, and adding renewal does not move the cliff — the pause that expires your lease is the same pause that prevents you renewing it.

6.2 The counter is not durable.

A concrete run: the election service issues epochs 1 through 12 over some months. The store's floor is 12. The election-service process restarts — a deploy, a crash, a pod reschedule. Its in-memory counter starts at 0. The next election issues epoch 1. Every write from the new, entirely legitimate leader carries epoch 1, and the store refuses all of them because 1 < 12. The system is down, with a correct leader that cannot do anything, until the counter climbs back past 12 — which takes twelve more elections.

Violated: liveness. Note carefully that safety was preserved — nothing was corrupted. That is the signature of this bug class and why it is so confusing in practice: a total outage with a perfectly healthy cluster and no data damage.

There is a worse variant. If the store's floor is also reset or the store is replaced, the reissued epoch 1 collides with the original epoch 1 and two different authorities become indistinguishable — and then you lose safety too.

The rule: the epoch must be persisted before it is used, which is precisely why real systems derive it from something already durable — a Raft term written to disk, a ZooKeeper zxid, an etcd revision — instead of minting their own.

6.3 Fencing writes but not reads loses updates via read-modify-write.

A concrete run, which is the distributed-locking lab's planted bug #4 and the most invisible failure in that lab: leader A (epoch 5) reads balance = 100, intending to write 110. A pauses. Its lease expires. B is elected in epoch 6. B reads balance = 100 — the read is not fenced, so nothing about B's read raises the floor. B writes 120 with epoch 6; accepted, floor becomes 6. A wakes and writes 110 with epoch 5 — refused, correctly.

So far so good. Now the variant that actually bites: A's write was already in flight when its lease expired, so it arrives carrying epoch 5 at a time when the floor is still 5, and is accepted. Then B's read (unfenced, so it raised nothing) had already returned the pre-A value. Both writes carry valid, increasing tokens; zero refusals; one update silently lost.

Violated: the effect-ordering invariant, in substance if not in letter — the database's state no longer reflects a single serial authority lineage.

The fix: fence reads too. A read presents its token and raises the floor, so older in-flight writes bounce. Chubby checks its sequencer on every request for exactly this reason.

6.4 Six servers tolerate the same number of failures as five and offer more things that can break.

A majority of six is four, so six tolerates two failures — identical to five, which also tolerates two. You have added a machine that can fail, a machine to patch, and a machine to pay for, in exchange for nothing.

Worse in this specific layout: three in each of two data centres means a site link failure gives 3 and 3, and neither side reaches four. Total outage on a single link failure. Five servers as 3|2 would have kept one side serving. The correct arrangement is an odd number of voters with no site holding a majority-blocking share — in practice, three sites.

Violated: nothing formal. This is a pure availability own-goal, and the general rule is: always an odd number of voters, spread over an odd number of failure domains.

6.5 Two nodes, symmetric fencing, and a partition means both shoot.

A concrete run: the link between them fails. Node A concludes B is dead and sends a power-off to B's management controller. Node B concludes A is dead and sends a power-off to A's management controller. If the management network is separate from the cluster network, both succeed: total outage, caused entirely by the safety mechanism. If the management network shares the broken path, neither succeeds and you now have split brain plus two nodes each convinced it has just killed the other.

The ping-pong variant: A shoots B, B reboots, B concludes A is dead, B shoots A, A reboots, and so on indefinitely.

Violated: liveness, catastrophically, by the mechanism installed to protect safety.

The mitigations, all standard in Pacemaker: gate fencing behind quorum so only a majority side may shoot (which a two-node cluster does not have, hence a quorum device or wait_for_all); add an asymmetric or randomised delay before firing so one side reliably wins a genuine race; and use a fencing path that does not share fate with the cluster network.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 — The unfenceable payment API.

Start by naming the situation precisely: your consensus protects the voters, and the payment API is not a voter. It was not asked, it has no idea your cluster exists, and it will do what it is told by whoever asks. No amount of correctness inside your five nodes reaches it.

What you can actually build, in order of how much it buys you:

  1. Idempotency at the operation, not the caller. Generate a stable key derived from the business fact — payment-{orderId}-{attemptNumber}, not a random UUID per request — and send it as the idempotency header. Where the provider honours it, a duplicate from a zombie leader is recognised and discarded. This is the only mechanism that protects you at the resource, and everything else is a way of making it less likely you need it.

  2. A local fenced intent log. Before calling the API, write an intent row to your own database with a conditional write fenced on the epoch. If that write is refused, you are stale — do not call. This does not close the gap (you can be fenced between writing the intent and making the call) but it narrows it from "the whole lease" to "one network call", and it gives you a record to reconcile against.

  3. Short leases plus a watchdog. Reduce the window in which a zombie can act. If you can, use a hardware or process watchdog that reboots a node that fails to renew — self-fencing, the way Patroni does. This turns "I might be stale" into "I am no longer running".

  4. Reconciliation. Poll the provider for the authoritative list of transactions and diff it against your intent log, continuously, not just after incidents.

The residual risk, stated honestly: if the provider's idempotency is not reliable, you have a window — bounded by (freeze duration) + (call duration) — in which a zombie leader can submit a duplicate that nothing will refuse. You cannot eliminate it. You can size it: instrument your process-stall duration and your API call latency, and the product of (stalls longer than the lease per month) × (probability a submission is in flight during one) is your expected duplicate rate. If that number is not acceptable, the answer is not a better lock — it is to get the provider to honour idempotency keys, or to move the invariant somewhere you control.

The distributed-locking lab called this "box G" and reached the same conclusion: a resource that cannot check conditions is safe under no lock.

7.2 — Harmless retries.

The server must make the operation idempotent: accept a client-supplied key with the request, and on receiving a key it has already processed, return the original result rather than performing the work again. Crucially, the key and the effect must be recorded atomically — same transaction, same commit — or the server can perform the work and then die before recording the key, which is the exact failure you were trying to fix.

The client must generate the key before the first attempt and reuse the same key for every retry of that logical operation. A client that generates a fresh key per retry has defeated the whole mechanism.

Which is load-bearing: the server. The client's contribution is a discipline that is easy to state and easy to get right; the server's is a real invariant that has to survive crashes, and it is where the design work is. A useful sanity check: if the server is correct and the client is sloppy you get duplicates; if the client is correct and the server is sloppy you also get duplicates. But only the server can make the guarantee, so that is where it must live.

The framing to carry: a timeout is not a failure. An operation that timed out may still have committed. This is the transactional-outbox and idempotency labs' central point, and it is why retries must be harmless rather than merely rare.

7.3 — The break-glass procedure.

The design constraint is unusual: it must be usable under pressure at 3 a.m. and not usable by mistake. Those pull in opposite directions, so the resolution is to make the dangerous step easy but make it require a fact that is hard to fabricate.

BREAK-GLASS: force quorum onto a surviving site

STOP. This procedure can destroy data. It is safe only if the other site is
genuinely down. It is NOT safe if the other site is merely unreachable, and
the cluster cannot tell the difference — that is why you are here.

1. ESTABLISH DEATH BY OUT-OF-BAND EVIDENCE.
   You must confirm the other site is down using a channel that does NOT
   depend on the link that failed. Acceptable evidence, at least two of:
     - the cloud provider's console shows the instances stopped or the
       region impaired;
     - the hypervisor/BMC reports the hosts powered off;
     - a human physically present reports it;
     - the provider's status page reports a regional outage.
   NOT acceptable: "we cannot reach it", "it is not responding",
   "monitoring is red". That is the same silence the cluster is looking at.

   Record the evidence in the incident channel BEFORE step 2. Name it.

2. FENCE IT. Make the assumption true rather than relying on it.
   Do at least one, ideally more:
     - revoke the other site's credentials to the shared datastore;
     - stop its instances through the provider API;
     - withdraw its routes / drop the BGP session;
     - revoke its storage access.
   If you cannot fence it, escalate. Do not proceed on hope.

3. SNAPSHOT FIRST. Take a backup of the surviving site's data now, before
   forcing anything. Reconciliation later will need the pre-override state.

4. FORCE QUORUM on the survivor.  <exact command for your system>

5. VERIFY: one leader, epoch advanced, writes flowing, and the fenced site
   is confirmed still fenced.

6. PAGE the service owner. This is not a silent operation.

7. BEFORE RECONNECTING the other site: it must be wiped and rebuilt from the
   survivor, never rejoined. A node holding a divergent history that rejoins
   is how a recovered incident becomes a second incident.

The two steps people skip are 2 and 7. Step 2 is what converts a guess into a fact. Step 7 is what prevents the healing problem (11.11) from turning a resolved outage into a data-reconciliation project.

7.4 — The JVM with 4-second pauses.

Currently guaranteed: nothing useful. Your observed worst-case pause of 4 s sits inside your 5 s detector limit with only 1 s of margin, which means an ordinary bad garbage collection is roughly one bad afternoon away from triggering a failover. And the 10 s lease means that when it does, the woken leader may still consider its lease valid for up to 6 s after a replacement has been elected — a 6-second window of two believed leaders. If nothing fences the resource, that is a 6-second window of possible corruption.

What is not guaranteed: that a stale leader's writes are refused. Nothing in this setup refuses anything.

What I would change, in order:

  1. Add fencing at the resource. This is first because it removes the timing question entirely. With a fenced resource, no combination of pause length, lease length and detector limit can corrupt data — the worst case becomes wasted work and a refused write. Everything else on this list is then a tuning problem rather than a correctness one.
  2. Then measure the pause distribution properly — a pause monitor, not an inference from GC logs — and set the detector limit to something like 3× the observed p99.9. Not to make it safe (fencing did that) but to stop needless failovers.
  3. Then attack the pauses: heap sizing, a low-pause collector, or moving the coordination role off the JVM that is doing the heavy allocation.
  4. Consider a watchdog so a node that stalls past its lease is restarted rather than resumed.

The ordering is the answer. Every instinct says "tune the timeouts first"; the lab's point is that timeout tuning buys you fewer incidents and fencing buys you harmless ones. Do the one that changes the category.

Rung 8 — Design

These are discussions, not answers. What follows is one defensible design each; yours may differ and still be right if it survives the same questions.

8.1 — The payments control plane

The requirement, restated in this lab's terms: the invariant is "a batch is submitted at most once". Late is fine; twice is not. So this is a correctness lock, not an efficiency lock, and by the distributed-locking lab's rule the guarantee must live at the resource — which here means at the banking partner, or at the closest thing to it you control.

Coordination state. A three-region etcd or Consul cluster, one voter per region, five voters if you want to survive a voter loss during a region outage (2/2/1 across three regions). Never an even number, and never a layout where one region holds a blocking share. At 50 ms between regions, expect roughly 100 ms per consensus write — fine for a control plane that makes a decision every few minutes, and a good argument for keeping the data plane out of it.

How a submitter proves authority. It campaigns for a leadership key with a lease. On winning, it gets a revision number — etcd's mod_revision — which is its epoch. That number is durable and monotonic because etcd made it so; do not mint your own (rung 6.2).

What the banking partner must do. Ideally: honour an idempotency key on batch submission, scoped to the batch, so a duplicate is recognised and discarded. Ask for this explicitly during integration; it is a normal request and most partners support it.

If it will not — and this is common — then you build the check yourself as close to the partner as you can get:

  • A submission-record table in a database you control, with a unique constraint on batch_id, written in the same transaction as the record of the submission.
  • The write is conditional on the epoch: ... WHERE current_epoch <= :myEpoch, which fences it.
  • Order of operations: claim the batch (fenced, atomic) → submit → record the outcome. A crash between claim and submit leaves a claimed-but-unsubmitted batch, which is a reconciliation case, not a duplicate.
  • Reconcile continuously against the partner's own transaction list. This is the only thing that catches a genuine duplicate, and it must run always, not only after incidents.

One region isolated. It cannot reach a majority, so it never wins leadership, so it never submits. Its application tier should fail fast with a clear error rather than queueing silently. Explicitly: do not let the isolated region "queue up batches to submit when it reconnects" without re-checking authority — that is a zombie with a backlog.

The coordination cluster unavailable. Everything stops submitting. This is correct and you should say so in the design document, because someone will propose a fallback and the fallback is how you get double submissions. If the business cannot tolerate that, the answer is more coordination-cluster availability, not less coordination.

Monitoring. Leadership changes per hour (should be near zero); the epoch/revision over time (flat with steps, never a ramp); fenced-write rejections (each one is a prevented duplicate); reconciliation diffs against the partner (should be zero, and this is the metric that actually proves the design works); time since last successful lease renewal per instance; and coordination-cluster quorum health.

Runbook. The break-glass procedure from 7.3, plus one specific to this domain: what to do when reconciliation finds a duplicate. That path must exist before you need it.

What survives from this lab, and what changes. Survives: everything — quorum for election, lease for expiry, fencing for effects, odd voters across odd failure domains, monitoring on election rate. Changes: the resource is now a third party you do not control, so the effect gate has to be relocated into a database you do control, sitting immediately in front of the call, and backed by continuous reconciliation because that gate is not actually at the point of effect. That relocation is the design's weakest joint and you should say so out loud.

8.2 — The retail edge

The first move is to notice that this is not a consensus problem. Two machines, no third, a link that fails several times a week, and a hard requirement to keep operating while offline. Every tool in this lab requires a majority, and two machines cannot produce one. If you try to force a majority-based design onto this, you will end up with the two-node trap of stage 11.3 — a total outage every time the WAN blinks — and then somebody will add an override, and then you will have split brain by policy.

So change the frame. The shop must keep taking payments while partitioned, which means you have chosen AP — availability over consistency — and the honest thing is to say so and design for it deliberately rather than discover it during an incident. That immediately tells you what you need: not a lock, but a data model where concurrent activity in two places cannot conflict.

What that looks like:

  • Make the shop's writes append-only and independently identified. A card payment is an event, not an update to a shared counter. Two terminals recording two different payments is not a conflict; it is two facts. Give each transaction a globally unique id generated at the terminal (store id + terminal id + local sequence), so nothing needs coordination to be unique.
  • Give the two shop machines disjoint id space, so they cannot collide even with no communication at all. This is the CRDT insight from the crdts lab applied at the smallest possible scale: design the data so that merging is trivial, and you never need to decide anything.
  • Queue locally and forward on reconnect. The shop's durable local queue is the guarantee; the WAN is an optimisation.
  • Head office deduplicates on the transaction id at ingest. Idempotent ingestion is what makes replaying the queue safe, and it is what makes the reconnection safe.

What you give up: any invariant that requires a global view at the time of the payment. You cannot enforce a real-time global credit limit, a global fraud counter, or "this card has already been used at another store in the last minute". Those require coordination and coordination is exactly what is unavailable. You handle them after the fact — detection and reversal rather than prevention — and you accept a bounded exposure, sized by (offline duration) × (transaction rate).

What you keep: every payment is durably recorded before the customer is told it succeeded, and every payment reaches head office exactly once. Those are the two that matter commercially.

Where a lock still belongs. If the two shop machines do share something exclusive — a cash drawer, a printer, a till-reconciliation job — use a lease between them for efficiency, and make the effect idempotent so that a split brain costs a duplicate receipt rather than a duplicate refund. That is the efficiency-versus-correctness distinction, applied per resource rather than per system.

8.3 — Reviewing a home-grown leader election

Ask these in order. Each one's bad answer tells you something specific about their data.

  1. "What decides that the current leader is dead?" Bad answer: "if we can't ping it" / "if the file lock is gone". Means the failure detector is a bare timeout with no analysis of what stalls the process, and nobody has thought about false positives.

  2. "How long can your process stall without being dead?" Bad answer: "it doesn't stall" or a number they are guessing. If they cannot quote a measured p99.9 pause, their timeout is unrelated to reality and their system will split-brain on the first bad afternoon.

  3. "What must a candidate collect before it may lead?" Bad answer: "it takes over when the other one stops". No quorum: this is v1 from stage 10, and it produces split brain on every partition.

  4. "Does an existing leader ever stand down on its own?" Bad answer: "it doesn't need to, it has the lock". No lease: this is stage 11.2, which produces two leaders even with a quorum. This is the most commonly missing piece and the one reviewers skip.

  5. "What number does a write carry, and who checks it?" Bad answer: silence, or "the lock guarantees it". No fencing. Everything upstream is now a timing bet, and their data has no protection at all against a stale leader.

  6. "Where does that number come from, and does it survive a restart?" Bad answer: "we increment a counter in the service". Rung 6.2 — an outage waiting for a deploy, and possibly worse.

  7. "Can the shared filesystem refuse a write from a stale owner?" For their specific case — renaming files on a network filesystem — this is the decisive question, and the answer is almost certainly no. A POSIX-ish network filesystem will happily let two machines rename the same file. That means there is no effect gate available, which means their invariant cannot be enforced by any lock they build.

    What follows from that is the useful part of the review: they need either (a) an idempotent design where a duplicate rename is harmless — for example naming the target deterministically from the source content so two workers doing the same work produce the same result, or (b) a real fencing point, such as staging the operation through a database row with a conditional update that records the intent before the filesystem is touched, or (c) NFSv4 with server-enforced locks if their storage supports it, or (d) self-fencing via a watchdog.

  8. "How many voters, and across how many failure domains?" Bad answer: an even number, or all in one rack. Stage 11.3 and 6.4.

  9. "What does your override do, and who can press it?" Bad answer: "there's a flag". Stage 11.10 is the most expensive failure in the lab and it is always a human one.

  10. "What is your alarm on election rate?" Bad answer: "we don't have one". They will not find out about churn until it becomes corruption.

The shape of a good review here: questions 1–2 establish how wrong the detector can be, 3–4 establish whether authority is bounded, 5–7 establish whether anything actually enforces the invariant, and 8–10 establish whether they will notice. If question 7 comes back "no" — which for a network filesystem it will — then questions 1 through 6 are all secondary, because no amount of careful election protects a resource that cannot refuse.
