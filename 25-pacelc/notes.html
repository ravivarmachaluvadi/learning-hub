1. WHY — the meeting that goes wrong every week

Somewhere right now, a team is designing a service whose data lives in three datacenters. Someone asks "why is the user in Virginia seeing yesterday's price?" and someone else answers "CAP theorem — you can't have consistency and availability, so we accept stale reads."

That answer is wrong, and the wrongness matters. The network between those datacenters is healthy today — no partition anywhere — and CAP, as you learned in the cap-theorem lab, only speaks about what happens when the network breaks. On a healthy network CAP demands nothing and forbids nothing. So something else is making that Virginia read stale, and because the team has no name for it, they can't reason about it, tune it, or price it. They just wave the CAP flag at it.

This lab gives the missing thing a name. It is the tradeoff you pay on every single request, every day, on a perfectly healthy network — and it was hiding in plain sight in three labs you have already done.

2. THE PROBLEM — one price book, two shops

Meera owns a jewellery business with two branches: one in Mumbai, one in Delhi. Both branches sell from the same price book — gold moves daily, so the book changes often. Head office is in Mumbai; when the gold price moves, Asha at head office writes the new price into Mumbai's book.

Delhi has a copy of the book. The only way to move information between the branches is the phone. A call takes three minutes — someone has to stop serving customers, dial, and read out numbers. The phone line works perfectly; nobody is cutting any wires in this story.

Now a customer walks into the Delhi branch and asks the price of a gold necklace. Ravi, the Delhi clerk, has exactly three possible policies:

  1. Quote from the local book immediately. Fast — but if Asha changed the price ten minutes ago and the update call hasn't happened yet, the customer gets the old price.
  2. Call Mumbai before every quote. Always correct — and every customer waits three minutes to hear a number.
  3. Make Asha call Delhi before her price change "counts". Then Ravi's local book is always current and he can quote instantly — but now every price change takes three minutes, and if Ravi's line is busy, the gold price is stuck.

Notice what just happened: the phone line is perfect, and there is still a problem. Somebody has to wait — the customer, or Asha — or nobody waits and somebody gets a stale price. The three minutes don't disappear under any policy; the only question is who absorbs them.

3. THE NAIVE SOLUTION — "no partition, so we can have everything"

A reasonable engineer who just learned CAP reasons like this:

"CAP says the C-vs-A choice only bites during a partition. Our network is healthy 99.99% of the time. So 99.99% of the time we can have both: replicate the data, answer from the nearest copy, everyone is fast AND consistent. Done."

Concretely, for a product catalog replicated in three regions — Mumbai, Frankfurt, Virginia — the plan is:

  • A write goes to the nearest replica, which applies it and immediately acknowledges. The replica then forwards the update to the other two.
  • A read is answered by the nearest replica, from its local copy.

Fast writes, fast reads, three copies, healthy network. What could go wrong?

4. THE FAILURE — stale on a perfect network

Let's run it by hand with real numbers. Round trips between the regions (these are realistic public-internet figures, used throughout this lab and in the visualization):

Link Round trip (RTT) One-way
Mumbai ↔ Frankfurt 110 ms 55 ms
Frankfurt ↔ Virginia 90 ms 45 ms
Mumbai ↔ Virginia 190 ms 95 ms

The trace — watch the clock column (this same trace is Scenario 1 in the visualization):

Time Event
t = 0 ms Asha (Mumbai) writes: necklace ₹50,000 → ₹54,000. Mumbai replica applies it.
t = 1 ms Asha gets her acknowledgment: "saved". Update messages depart for Frankfurt and Virginia.
t = 40 ms Omar (Virginia) asks the price. Virginia replica answers from its local copy: ₹50,000.
t = 55 ms Frankfurt replica receives the update, applies ₹54,000.
t = 95 ms Virginia replica receives the update, applies ₹54,000.

At t = 40 ms, Omar was told a price that had been officially replaced — write acknowledged, done, confirmed — 39 milliseconds earlier. No partition. No failure. No misconfiguration. The network did everything right. The staleness came from a design choice: nobody waited.

Could we make Omar's read correct? Sure — but look at what each fix costs:

  • Make the writer wait until all three replicas have applied the update before acknowledging Asha. Now no acknowledged price can be missing anywhere… and Asha's every write takes ≥ 190 ms (the Mumbai↔Virginia round trip), because light itself needs that long. Bombay to Virginia is ~13,000 km of fiber; no amount of engineering removes that.
  • Make the reader check with other replicas before answering. Now Omar's every read pays a cross-ocean round trip instead.

This is exactly Meera's shop. Consistency between distant copies has a price in the happy case, and the currency is milliseconds — not availability. CAP never mentions this, because CAP's premise (Gilbert & Lynch's proof, which you met in the cap-theorem lab) is specifically about a network that has stopped delivering messages. Ours is delivering perfectly. The cost is that delivery takes time.

🔮 Predict: Your team "fixes" the staleness by having the writer wait for all three replicas (fix #1 above). A month later, the Virginia replica's machine dies. What happens to Asha's writes in Mumbai — a region that is perfectly healthy and nowhere near Virginia?

Reveal

Every write in the world now hangs. The acknowledgment rule was "wait for all three", and one of the three will never answer. A single machine's death in Virginia has taken away write availability in Mumbai. (You measured this exact trap in the quorums-and-replication lab: acks=all with no minimum is either a total write outage or a silent degrade to acks=1.) The fix's fix — wait for a majority, 2 of 3 — is where the next section's ladder goes. The point to keep: every consistency mechanism is simultaneously a latency policy and a failure policy, which is why the two questions PACELC asks are separable but both always present.

5. THE REAL REQUIREMENT

We need a design vocabulary that names both regimes a replicated system lives in:

  1. When the network breaks (rare, forced on you): do you keep answering with possibly-wrong data, or refuse to answer? — This is CAP's question, and it is still a real question.
  2. When the network is healthy (~99.99% of the time, chosen by you): do you make someone wait for coordination, or answer immediately and accept staleness? — This is the question CAP cannot even express.

A vocabulary that only covers regime 1 produces engineers who justify regime-2 choices with regime-1 arguments — "we're eventually consistent because CAP" — which is how a decade of design discussions went sideways.

6. THE KEY IDEA

In 2010, Daniel Abadi wrote down the two-part question, and in 2012 he formalized it in an IEEE Computer article ("Consistency Tradeoffs in Modern Distributed Database System Design: CAP is Only Part of the Story"). It reads as a single mnemonic:

PACELC — if there is a Partition, choose Availability or Consistency; Else, choose Latency or Consistency.

Two questions, each with two answers, giving four possible system characters. That's the whole idea. The rest of this lab is what each answer's machinery looks like, why all four combinations genuinely exist in production systems, and what each one costs — in numbers you have already measured.

7. TERMINOLOGY — the words, now that you've felt the problem

PACELC (pronounced "pass-elk"). Plain meaning: a checklist of two questions for any replicated system. Technical meaning: a classification scheme — every system gets a four-letter tag like PA/EL meaning "under Partition it chooses Availability; Else it chooses Latency". It exists because CAP's single question let the everyday tradeoff hide unnamed. Example: the naive design of §3 is PA/EL. Connection: the P-half is CAP; the E-half is new.

Else clause / baseline mode. Plain: "normal operation" — the network is delivering messages. Technical: the regime in which coordination is possible and therefore has a measurable price, rather than impossible. The term exists to break the habit of only discussing the partition case. Example: everything in the §4 trace happens in the else clause. Connection: your eventual-consistency lab's staleness measurements (t-visibility, lag) are all else-clause measurements.

EL — latency over consistency (else clause). Plain: nobody waits; answers are immediate and may be stale. Technical: acknowledgments and reads do not block on cross-replica coordination; recency is unpromised (or only measured, never contracted). Why the term exists: to name what "eventually consistent" systems are actually buying — it was never about partitions. Example: the §4 trace — 1 ms writes, 1 ms reads, 39 ms-stale answer. Connection: this is the eventual-consistency lab's whole subject, now placed on the map.

EC — consistency over latency (else clause). Plain: someone waits so that nobody gets a stale answer. Technical: reads and/or writes block on enough coordination that recency holds (in this lab's judge: a read never returns a value older than the newest write acknowledged before the read began — the real-time rule from the linearizability lab). Example: waiting for a write quorum at 110 ms instead of acking at 1 ms. Connection: every "bill" you measured before — ABD read write-back taking mean read latency 11.7 → 24.4 ticks, Spanner-style commit wait taking write acks 6.7 → 24.8 ticks — is an EC bill.

PA / PC — availability or consistency under partition: exactly the CAP choice from the cap-theorem lab; PA keeps answering on both sides of the break (divergence risk), PC refuses on the side that can't coordinate (the minority, in quorum systems).

The four classes. PA/EL (fast and loose everywhere: Dynamo-family), PC/EC (strict everywhere: Spanner-family), PC/EL and PA/EC (the two "paradoxical" corners — §10 shows real machinery that lands in each; neither is a philosophy, both fall out of a mechanism).

Staleness window. Plain: how long a replica can lag behind an acknowledged write. Technical: in the async design it is the one-way network delay plus queueing — 55 ms to Frankfurt, 95 ms to Virginia in our matrix. Exists because "eventually" needed a measurable everyday size. Connection: eventual-consistency's t-visibility, and PBS's "tens of milliseconds at 99.9%" — same object.

Per-operation dial. Plain: the EL/EC choice need not be system-wide; many systems let each request choose. Technical: Cassandra's per-query consistency level, DynamoDB's ConsistentRead=true flag — the R/W dial you built in quorums-and-replication is literally a per-request PACELC selector. This term exists to stop you asking "is this database EL or EC?" when the honest answer is "per request, at a price."

Minor terms defined at first use later: timeline consistency, per-record master, degraded ack, ack policy.

8. MENTAL MODELS

The simple model (carry this one around): Meera's two-branch shop. The phone that takes three minutes is the network; the price book is the replica; Asha's price change is a write; a customer's question is a read. The three policies of §2 are the three places the delay can be put: on the customer (coordinated reads), on Asha (coordinated writes), or on nobody — in which case some customer, somewhere, hears an old price. A dead phone line is a partition; then the question changes from who waits to who gets refused.

The engineering model (what's actually there): N replicas with a value and a version each; an RTT matrix between them; an ack policy (how many replicas must have applied a write before the client is told "saved") and a read policy (how many replicas a read consults). Coordination = messages crossing the RTT matrix before someone is answered. The partition-mode behavior (PA or PC) and the else-mode behavior (EL or EC) are both consequences of those two policies plus a timeout.

Shop System
Price book in a branch Replica
Asha's new gold price Write
Customer asking a price Read
The 3-minute phone call One-way network delay (55–95 ms)
"It counts once Delhi confirms" Ack policy (W)
"Call Mumbai before quoting" Read policy (R)
Dead phone line Partition
Busy line, call takes 9 minutes Elevated RTT — note this is not cleanly either

Where the analogy breaks (read this — the shop lies to you in four ways):

  1. The shop picks one policy for the whole shop. Real systems choose per operation, per key, even per request (the per-operation dial). PACELC tags are defaults, not destinies.
  2. A stale quote costs the shop one discount. In a system, a stale read that feeds a read-modify-write compounds — you proved this as the eventual-consistency lab's F7 rule ("EC reads are for display, never decisions") and measured it as the linearizability lab's 87%-lost increments.
  3. The shopkeeper can see the phone line is dead. A real node cannot distinguish a dead peer from a slow one — silence is the only evidence (distributed-locking lab). "Partition" in practice means "my timeout fired", which is why the P-vs-E boundary is defined by a config value, not by physics. §16 makes this precise.
  4. The phone call is a conversation. Replication messages are one-way, can reorder, duplicate, and arrive after the line "died". The shop hides every ordering problem — which is fine here, because ordering had its own labs (causal-consistency, hybrid-logical-clocks).

10. THE ACTUAL MECHANISM — one ladder, four landing spots

There is really only one machine here — N replicas and an ack policy — and the four PACELC classes are four settings of it. We'll build it the way you'd build it, breaking it three times. All timings use the §4 RTT matrix.

v1 — ack locally, spread later (lands on PA/EL)

The §3 design. Write applied at the nearest replica, acked in ~1 ms, update messages fanned out. Reads are local, ~1 ms.

What you saw in the trace: staleness window = one-way delay (Omar stale for up to 95 ms per write). And there's a second, worse problem you already measured elsewhere: two regions writing concurrently diverge, and when the dust settles a conflict rule picks a survivor — last-write-wins silently discarded 1000 of 1000 genuinely-newer updates in the quorums-and-replication lab when one clock ran fast. Under partition, v1 doesn't even notice: both sides keep answering and diverge freely (that's the PA), then the heal hands the mess to the conflict rule. Fast always; correct by luck.

v2 — the writer waits for everyone (attempt at EC)

Flip one rule: don't ack Asha until all three replicas confirm they applied the write. Now no read anywhere can miss an acknowledged write — the write isn't acknowledged until it is everywhere. Reads stay local and fast. Write ack latency: the slowest round trip, 190 ms from Mumbai.

You already predicted the break (the 🔮 in §4): one dead replica = global write outage. "All" is not a policy that survives the real world.

v3 — majority, both sides (lands on PC/EC)

The fix you built in quorums-and-replication: wait for a majority (2 of 3) on writes, and — because a write acked by {Mumbai, Frankfurt} is invisible to a local read at Virginia — make reads consult a majority too, taking the newest version they see. R + W > N; the two sets must overlap; no acknowledged write can be missed.

The bills, from our RTT matrix (client next to its coordinator):

Operation v1 (local) v3 (majority)
Write from Mumbai ~1 ms 110 ms (nearest 2nd ack: Frankfurt)
Write from Virginia ~1 ms 90 ms (Frankfurt)
Read from anywhere ~1 ms 90–110 ms (nearest 2nd answer)

Under partition: the isolated region can't assemble a majority for anything and refuses (that's the PC); the majority side continues correctly. You measured the flavor of this refusal in causal-consistency: the linearizable store answered 0 of 600 operations on the minority side.

Honesty flag: full linearizability needs one more ingredient on reads — the ABD write-back (push the value you're about to return to a quorum, so no later reader can un-see it), which took mean read latency from 11.7 to 24.4 ticks in the linearizability lab. The visualization's majority design skips write-back and says so on the page; its judge is "never return a value older than the newest acked write", which majority overlap alone does satisfy in the sim's single-writer scenarios.

🔮 Predict: Can a system reasonably choose C under partition but L in the else clause — strict when the network breaks, cheap and stale when it's healthy? What machinery would even produce that combination?

Reveal

Yes — and nobody designs it as a philosophy; it falls out of a mechanism: give every record a single home master (next rung). Writes must reach the master (so a partition that hides the master refuses writes — PC); reads are served locally from possibly-lagging copies (so everyday reads are 1 ms and stale — EL). Yahoo!'s PNUTS, the system Abadi had in mind, is exactly this. The class looks paradoxical only if you imagine someone choosing letters off a menu; the letters are shadows of the machinery.

v4 — one home master per record (lands on PC/EL)

Every record has a per-record master — one replica that all writes for that record must go through (say Mumbai for our necklace, because most of its writes originate there). The master applies writes in a single sequence and streams updates out asynchronously. Reads are local everywhere.

  • Write from Mumbai: ~1 ms (master is local). Write from Virginia: 190 ms (forwarded to Mumbai and back).
  • Read anywhere: ~1 ms, stale up to the one-way delay — EL.
  • What the master's single sequence buys over v1: no divergence, ever. All replicas apply the same writes in the same order; a lagging replica is behind, never different. PNUTS called this timeline consistency — every copy moves along one timeline, some copies further back. (Contrast v1, where two timelines can genuinely fork and something must die at merge.)
  • Partition hiding the master: writes for that record are refused — PC. (Reads continue from the frozen local copy. Abadi classifies this as PC because availability is reduced — you lose writes — rather than consistency being abandoned. "PC" does not require refusing everything.)

And the fourth corner — PA/EC, waiting until it hurts

Take v2 (wait for everyone) and patch its fatal flaw with a timeout: wait for all replicas; if one stops answering for ~1 s, ack with whoever responded and carry on. This is a degraded ack — the ack policy itself downgrades under failure.

Every day this system pays the full EC bill (190 ms writes from Mumbai — the worst latency of any design here). But the moment a partition isolates a region, both sides keep accepting writes — PA — and diverge exactly like v1, with the heal handing survivors to the conflict rule. It pays for consistency daily and abandons it the moment it's tested.

Why would anyone build this? Because "keep taking orders" is a business requirement and the everyday consistency is real and valuable — this is roughly classic MongoDB's character (Abadi's own classification: PA/EC), and the failure signature is famous: writes accepted during the bad minutes that are thrown away at heal (MongoDB literally wrote them to "rollback files" for a human to look at). The class isn't stupid; it's a specific bet that partitions are short and rare. The honest version documents what degrade does; the dangerous version calls itself "strongly consistent" unqualified.

One sentence per class, the ones worth memorizing:

  • PA/EL — nobody waits, ever; conflicts are resolved after the fact.
  • PC/EC — someone always waits; the system would rather refuse than be wrong.
  • PC/EL — reads never wait, writes follow one master; lose the master, lose writes.
  • PA/EC — everyone waits while waiting works; when it stops working, stop waiting.

And the single most compressible statement of the whole else-clause: someone waits — the writer, the reader, or nobody; and if nobody waits, somebody gets stale. Every design above is just a choice of who.

11. THE MIDDLE OF THE DIAL — two designs that are not in the 2×2

§10 ended on a sentence worth re-reading: someone waits — the writer, the reader, or nobody. Hidden inside it is an assumption nobody stated: that the waiting applies to everybody, for every operation, forever.

Two mechanisms break that assumption, and between them they cover the way most production systems actually run. Neither is a fifth PACELC class — both live inside the else clause, between EL and EC. This is where the binary you just learned starts to bend.

v5 — make only the person who wrote wait (session guarantees)

Start from the complaint that actually reaches your team. Not "region B lagged 39 ms behind region A" — nobody files that ticket. The one that arrives is:

"I changed the price, hit refresh, and my change was gone."

Watch it happen with the §4 numbers, design v1 (local ack):

Time Event
t = 0 ms Asha writes ₹54,000 in Mumbai.
t = 1 ms Asha is told "saved". Updates depart for Frankfurt and Virginia.
t = 10 ms Asha hits refresh. Her connection is routed through Frankfurt this time — a load balancer decision, a mobile network hop, or an app that simply sends reads to a read replica.
t = 10 ms Frankfurt answers ₹50,000 — the price Asha just replaced.
t = 55 ms Frankfurt receives the update. Too late; the page is already wrong.

Nothing here is new physics — it is the same one-way delay as F1. But it is a different kind of wrong. F1's stale read broke recency against the clock: some other person saw an old value. This breaks Asha's view of her own action, and no amount of "it's eventually consistent" comforts someone staring at their own missing edit. It is also the single most frequent consistency complaint in real systems.

🔮 Predict: You want to fix only this — everyone should see their own writes; strangers may still see stale prices for 55 ms. What is the cheapest mechanism, and who ends up paying for it?

Reveal

The client carries a token: "the newest write I was personally told was saved" — a version number, a log position, a timestamp. Every read the client sends carries the token, and the replica serving it must already hold that write, or wait until it does (or hand the read to one that does). In the trace above, Asha's t = 10 ms read is a parked read — held at Frankfurt (⏳ on that city's card in the visualization) and completed at t = 55 ms: 45 ms, once, paid only by the person who just wrote. Compare with EC, where every read by everybody pays 90–110 ms forever. The waiting didn't disappear; it was aimed at exactly the one operation that needed it.

That mechanism is a session guarantee, and it is old and well-defined: Terry et al. named four of them in 1994 (the Bayou system).

Guarantee Plain meaning What it costs
Read-your-writes You never fail to see a write you made Token + occasional wait, as traced above
Monotonic reads Your successive reads never go backwards in time The token also tracks the newest value you have seen, not just written
Monotonic writes Your own writes are applied in the order you issued them Sequence per client at the replica
Writes-follow-reads A write you make after reading X lands after X everywhere Attach the read's version to the write

Monotonic reads is the one people forget. Same design, no writes by you at all: you read Frankfurt at t = 60 ms and see the new ₹54,000; your next read is routed to Virginia at t = 70 ms, which is still on ₹50,000. The price went backwards on your screen — a value un-happening is far more alarming to a user than a value being old. The token fixes this too, because it records the newest version you were shown.

Now the honest part — what a session guarantee does NOT buy. In the trace, Priya reading in Frankfurt at t = 20 ms still gets ₹50,000, and the lab's recency judge still calls it stale, correctly. Session guarantees make the system consistent with respect to one client's own history, never with respect to real time. You can see both facts at once in the visualization: with the session design selected, the "your own change vanished" counter stays at 0 while the "stale reads" counter keeps climbing. Those two numbers are measuring different promises.

Where this sits in the labs you already have: session guarantees are exactly causal consistency restricted to one client's own thread of interaction. That restriction is why they're cheap — a single client's history is a straight line, so there are no concurrent branches to merge and no dependency graph to ship. Causal consistency gives you all four guarantees plus everyone else's causality; session guarantees give you only your own, for the price of one token.

v6 — contract the staleness instead of removing it (bounded staleness)

EL's contract is "stale by some unknown amount". That is unusable for a decision — the eventual-consistency lab's F7 rule ("EC reads are for display, never decisions") exists because of it. EC removes staleness entirely at 90–110 ms per operation. The middle option is to promise a ceiling: this answer is at most Δ old, guaranteed.

The interesting part is why a replica cannot do this alone. A lagging replica has no way to know it is lagging — no incoming updates looks exactly like nobody is writing. Silence carries no information (the distributed-locking lab's rule, in a new costume). So the promise has to come from the write path: the master (or the quorum leader) publishes a closed timestamp — "you now hold every write stamped at or before T" — and replicas heartbeat that value forward.

Hand trace, home-office design, master = Mumbai, beacons every 100 ms:

Time Event
t = 0 ms Master applies ₹54,000 (stamped t = 0).
t = 95 ms Virginia receives and applies it.
t = 200 ms Master publishes a beacon: "closed through t = 100 ms" — every write with a stamp ≤ 100 is already sent to everyone.
t = 295 ms Virginia receives the beacon. It now knows it holds everything stamped ≤ 100 ms.
t = 300 ms Omar reads. Virginia answers as of t = 100 ms — locally, in ~1 ms, with a proof that nothing older than 200 ms is hidden from it.

Omar's answer is 200 ms old and provably not older. He waited for nothing; no cross-ocean round trip happened on his read path at all. The ceiling is (beacon interval + one-way delay + replication lag), and every term is a number you can monitor. What he gave up is the present: this is a consistent snapshot of a past instant, not a linearizable read of now.

This is real machinery, not a teaching device: CockroachDB's closed timestamps and follower reads (AS OF SYSTEM TIME follower_read_timestamp() — documented as roughly 4.8 s behind by default), Spanner's read-only transactions with exact or bounded staleness, and Azure Cosmos DB's Bounded Staleness level, where you configure the bound in both versions and seconds. Honesty flag: those defaults are quoted from vendor documentation; nothing in this section is measured by this lab, and the visualization implements v5 (session tokens) but not v6 — the beacon trace above is done by hand.

"The current value, no exceptions" Quorum or leader read (v3), plus write-back for full linearizability One RTT, every operation, forever

Read rows 3 and 4 together, because they contain the sharpest cost insight in this lab: the moment your freshness requirement is larger than the network's one-way delay, coordination becomes free. A fraud score that may be five minutes old and a price display that may be two seconds old are not weak versions of a linearizable read — they are requirements that asynchronous replication already satisfies, at 1 ms, with a provable ceiling. Most flows in most systems are in row 1, 2 or 3. Paying row 5's bill for them is the most common over-engineering in distributed systems, and the most common cause of a global outage in a system that had no business being globally coupled in the first place.

12. THE PRICE IS A DESIGN VARIABLE — latency arithmetic and placement

§10 quoted EC at "110 ms" as if that were physics. Part of it is physics. The rest is a decision you made — usually without noticing — about where the coordinating replicas sit. This section is the arithmetic, because an engineer who can compute the bill in their head stops arguing about philosophies.

Step 1 — decompose one EC operation. Three terms, and only one of them is consensus:

total = (client → coordinator) + (coordinator → quorum → coordinator) + (coordinator → client)

Worked with our matrix, for a majority write of one record:

  • Omar (Virginia) writes a record whose leader is Mumbai: 95 + 110 + 95 = 300 ms. (Cross-ocean, then a European round trip.)
  • Omar writes a record whose leader is Virginia: ~0 + 90 + ~0 = 90 ms.

Same database, same consistency level, same code — 3.3× apart, decided entirely by which replica holds the leadership for that record. The consensus tax was 90–110 ms in both cases; everything else was the trip to the coordinator.

Step 2 — quorum arithmetic. With N replicas and majority writes, the coordinator's ack lands when the ⌈(N−1)/2⌉-th nearest peer answers:

Deployment Majority What the writer waits for Mumbai's bill
3 regions (BOM, FRA, IAD) 2 of 3 1 nearest peer 110 ms (Frankfurt)
5 regions (+ Tokyo, São Paulo) 3 of 5 2 nearest peers 130 ms (Frankfurt, then Tokyo)

Adding replicas for durability raises everyday write latency and, worse, raises the tail faster than the median, because the ack is a maximum over the members you wait for — the p99 of a max, not the max of p99s. You measured that shape in quorums-and-replication: waiting on 3-of-3 vs 2-of-3 turned 2.85% slow requests into 27.31%.

13. THE BILL COMPOUNDS — coordination is a capacity decision

Every number so far — 110 ms, 190 ms, 300 ms — was the price of one operation, alone, on an idle system. That is the number a benchmark prints and the number an architecture document quotes. It is also the number that misleads people, because a production system does not run one operation; it runs two thousand a second, and latency at that scale stops being a latency problem.

Step 1 — the identity that turns milliseconds into machines. From the queueing-theory lab, Little's Law: L = λW. It is an identity, not a model — no assumption about arrival patterns or service distributions, only steady state. Read it in the direction that matters here:

How many operations are in flight at once = arrival rate × how long each one stays inside.

Take one catalog read endpoint at λ = 2,000 reads/s, and switch it from a local read to a majority read:

Design W (per §12's arithmetic) L = λW — operations in flight
EL — local read 2 ms 4
EC — majority read via Frankfurt 90 ms 180

Same traffic, same code, same hardware. 45× the concurrent work in the building. And "concurrent work" is not an abstraction: it is 45× the threads, 45× the sockets, 45× the database sessions, 45× the request buffers — every resource that is held for the duration of an operation is multiplied by exactly the factor the latency grew.

Step 2 — the ceiling that appears the moment you count pool slots. Your Java service holds a connection for the duration of the call, and the pool has 50 of them (a Hikari default nobody revisited). Capacity is c / W:

Design Pool Per-op latency Throughput ceiling c/W
EL 50 2 ms 25,000 ops/s
EC 50 90 ms 556 ops/s

🔮 Predict: The endpoint takes 2,000 reads/s. Someone flips it from eventual to strongly-consistent reads — a one-line config change, no deploy of application code. p99 read latency was 4 ms. What does the graph look like ten minutes later?

Reveal

Not "reads got slower". The service stops. 2,000 arrivals per second against a 556/s ceiling is ρ = 3.6 — the queueing lab's F2, where the queue grows linearly and forever: 5% over capacity already produced 1,619 waiting requests at 30 minutes and still climbing, with throughput pinned at exactly capacity and every utilization dashboard looking completely healthy. Here you are 260% over. Latency does not settle at 90 ms; it climbs until something times out, and the thing that times out is the caller, who then retries (step 5). The pool is the visible symptom — "connection timeout" from Hikari, which is the queueing lab's F6 unbounded queue with a 30-second multiplier — but the pool is not the cause. The cause is that a consistency setting is a capacity setting and nobody re-derived the capacity.

Little's Law read backwards is the sizing rule: c ≥ λW / ρ_target, so this endpoint needs 2000 × 0.09 / 0.8 = 225 connections to run the EC path at 80% busy — 4.5× the pool, and 225 concurrent sessions is now a question for the database team rather than a line in a properties file.

Step 3 — and 80% busy is not 90 ms. The queueing lab's central measurement was that total time inside a busy system is service time multiplied by congestion: at ρ = 0.8, 5.1× (measured, S = 80 ms). So the EC path sized "correctly" at 80% utilization delivers roughly 450 ms, not the 90 ms of §12's arithmetic. The RTT is the floor of the EC bill, never the bill.

Step 4 — contention: the per-record ceiling no hardware can lift. The numbers above assume operations are independent. Conflicting operations on the same record are not: they must serialize, and under EC each one holds its place across a coordination round. If a row's lock is held for one majority round trip (110 ms), that row's ceiling is roughly 9 conflicting writes per second — and buying more machines does not move it, because the constraint is the round trip, not the CPU. Honesty flag: this is arithmetic from this lab's model, not a measurement; real systems pipeline non-conflicting work aggressively, so the ceiling applies per contended record, not to the system.

Two independent confirmations from labs you have already done:

  • google-spanner measured the tradeoff directly: going from 1 to 3 to 5 replicas took snapshot-read throughput 13.5 → 38.5 → 50.0 K ops/s while write throughput fell 4.1 → 2.2 → 2.8 K ops/s. Replication adds read capacity and subtracts write capacity — the else-clause tradeoff appearing as a throughput curve rather than a latency number. Its 2PC latency by participant count (17.0 · 24.5 · 42.7 · 71.4 · 150.5 ms for 1/2/50/100/200) is the same story: coordination width is a price.
  • cockroachdb shows what contention does when the system refuses to block: conflicting transactions are pushed and then must re-verify, and the loser is thrown away as a 40001 retry — so under contention an EC system does not merely slow down, it starts discarding completed work, which is offered load being paid for twice. Worse, the refused transaction is the one that committed second in real time, so the slower client starves rather than queues fairly.

Step 5 — the loop that converts a slow EC path into an outage. An EC operation that exceeds its deadline produces a timeout; a timeout produces a retry; a retry is new offered load arriving at the component that is already saturated. The queueing lab measured exactly this shape: a one-instant 400-job burst into a healthy 85%-busy system, with clients retrying twice on a 600 ms timeout, reached 37,115 queued at 20 minutes and still growing, while the identical burst without retries was absorbed in four minutes. 99% of server time went to clients that had already given up. And the property that makes it an incident rather than a blip: removing the trigger does not restore service — which is why the fix is a traffic shut-off and a cold start.

EL has no such loop. Nothing waits, so nothing accumulates; when an EL system is overloaded it serves stale data faster, which is ugly but stable. That is the real asymmetry between the two columns: EL degrades, EC collapses. The four classes differ in latency by a factor; they differ in failure shape by a category.

What follows for design, in four lines:

  1. A consistency-level change is a capacity change. Re-derive λW before shipping it, and treat "turn on strong reads" as a resize, not a flag.
  2. Prefer a concurrency limit to a rate limit on EC paths — it re-derives itself when W moves, and W moves every time leadership migrates (F9).
  3. Bulkhead the EC path into its own pool. Otherwise the 45× in-flight multiplier eats the connections that the EL flows — the 95% of your traffic that never needed coordination — were using.
  4. Give every EC call a deadline shorter than the caller's, and a retry budget. An unbudgeted retry on a coordinated path is the metastable loop above, pre-installed.

14. THE QUESTION BEFORE PACELC'S — when nobody has to wait

Meera's shop has been selling information — a price. Information can be stale and then corrected: the customer sees ₹50,000, the page refreshes, now it says ₹54,000, nobody died. Every mechanism in this lab so far has been about how fresh that information is and who pays for the freshness.

Now Meera starts selling necklaces, and she has exactly ten.

The rule is no longer a preference, it is an invariant: never sell the eleventh necklace. And an invariant changes the nature of a stale read.

The trace, design v1 (local ack), during a partition:

Time Event
t = 0 Both branches believe: 10 in stock.
t = 1 s The link goes down. Mumbai and Virginia can still each serve customers.
t = 1–5 min Mumbai sells 6. Every sale acked locally in ~1 ms — the design working perfectly.
t = 1–5 min Virginia sells 5. Same.
t = 6 min Link heals. The two counts meet. Last-write-wins picks one.

The data converges — that is what "eventually consistent" promised, and it delivered. Eleven customers are holding receipts for ten necklaces. No conflict-resolution rule can fix that, because the thing that must be un-done is not a value in a database; five of those necklaces are in vans.

That is the distinction this section turns on, and it is sharper than the eventual-consistency lab's "reads are for display, never decisions":

A stale read that informs can be repaired by a later read. A stale read that authorizes an irreversible act cannot be repaired at all — the repair has to happen in the world, and the world does not support rollback.

The conclusion everyone jumps to, and why it is only half right. "Invariants need coordination, so this flow is EC — 110 ms per sale, and refuse in the minority during a partition." That works. It is also, very often, unnecessary.

🔮 Predict: You want both branches to keep selling during a partition — at 1 ms, with no messages between them — and you want it to be impossible to sell the eleventh necklace. Not unlikely. Impossible. What would you change?

Reveal

Change the invariant, not the machinery. Give each branch its own allowance: Mumbai may sell 4, Frankfurt 3, Virginia 3. Each branch now enforces a purely local rule — "I have sold no more than my allowance" — that needs no messages at all, and because 4 + 3 + 3 = 10, the local rules together imply the global invariant. The eleventh sale is not unlikely; it is arithmetically unavailable. Sales stay at 1 ms, both sides keep selling through the partition, and the count is never wrong. This is escrow (O'Neil, 1986), also called the reservation pattern.

You have already built this once. In the distributed-rate-limiting lab, the design that beat per-request coordination for hot keys was: hand each node a lease — a block of the limit it may spend locally — and re-synchronize periodically. Per-request coordination costs a round trip per request; leases cost a round trip per key per node per interval. That lab even recorded the failure mode by name: stranded quota. Escrow is the same mechanism, and the same accident, applied to a stock count instead of a rate limit. Two more places it is already in your stack: Hibernate's HiLo identifier allocator (each JVM takes a block of IDs, so no coordination per insert), and every ticketing system's reserved seat pool.

Branch Allowance Demand Sold Refused

Frankfurt 3 2 2 0
Virginia 3 2 2 0
Total 10 10 8 2 — with 2 necklaces unsold in the warehouse

This is a new kind of refusal, and it is worth naming precisely because it is not any of the ones you have met. PC refuses because it cannot coordinate. Escrow refuses while healthy, with stock available, on a perfect network — it has traded some availability for locality, and the loss shows up as stranded inventory rather than as errors during an incident. Four ways to recover it, in the order you should reach for them:

  1. Split by demand, not equally. 6/2/2 would have sold all ten.
  2. Rebalance in the background. A branch that is running low asks for more allowance. This is coordination — but it happens once per several sales, off the critical path, and can be slow without anyone noticing. Moving coordination off the request path is almost always worth more than removing it.
  3. Fall back to coordinating when the local allowance hits zero. Now you pay §12's bill on the last few percent of sales instead of on all of them. This is the shape most production systems should have: cheap local path, coordinated tail.
  4. Hold a central reserve — split 3/2/2 and keep 3 unallocated for whoever needs them.

And escrow's transfer must be fenced. Moving allowance from Virginia to Mumbai during a partition is exactly the handover problem from distributed-locking and partitioning-and-rebalancing: if Virginia is merely slow rather than dead and its allowance is reassigned, both regions can sell the same three necklaces — a split-brain with physical consequences. Allowance transfer needs an ownership epoch, or a lease with an expiry that the recipient waits out.

The general rule underneath escrow

Escrow is a trick that worked here. Is there a rule for when a trick like that exists at all? Yes, and it has a name.

Invariant confluence (I-confluence) — Bailis et al., Coordination Avoidance in Database Systems, VLDB 2014. Informally: an invariant can be maintained without any coordination exactly when merging two independently-reached valid states always produces a valid state. If two replicas can each do something legal, and the merge of their results is still legal, nobody ever had to ask permission. If the merge can be illegal, some coordination is provably required — no amount of cleverness removes it.

Invariant I-confluent? Why
Add an item to a set / append to a log Yes Union of two legal sets is legal
Counter that only increases Yes Order does not matter, no state is illegal
Per-row CHECK (age >= 0) Yes Each row is validated where it is written
Foreign-key insert Yes (with care) Adding a child to an existing parent stays legal on merge
Uniqueness — one account per email No Two replicas each legally claim asha@…; the merge is illegal
SUM(stock) ≥ 0 with decrements No Both sides legally sell; the merge is negative — §14's opening trace
"At most one row is active" No Same shape as uniqueness

Now the reframing that makes escrow generalizable: escrow does not make SUM ≥ 0 I-confluent. It replaces that invariant with a stronger, local one — "each branch's sales ≤ its allowance" — which is I-confluent, and which implies the original. That is the transferable move:

When a global constraint forces coordination, look for a partition of it into local constraints whose conjunction implies it. You do not weaken the invariant; you divide it.

Every instance of the pattern is that same move: sequence-number blocks, per-shard rate limits, regional credit limits, seat pools, per-region inventory allocation. And the reason it cannot always be done is worth holding onto: you can divide a quantity; you cannot divide an identity. There is no way to give Mumbai "40% of the username asha".

One more anchor, stated honestly as a result whose shape I am quoting rather than proving here: the CALM theorem (Hellerstein, 2010; proved by Ameloot et al.) says a program has a coordination-free implementation if and only if it is monotonic — roughly, if adding more information never retracts a conclusion. Uniqueness is non-monotonic because it rests on a claim about absence ("nobody else has taken this"), and absence is the one thing a message cannot carry — the same rule that gave the eventual-consistency lab its tombstones and the partitioning-and-rebalancing lab its resurrected deletes.

The three questions, in order, for any flow

This is the decision procedure the last four sections add up to. Ask them in this order, and stop at the first one that answers:

  1. Does this read merely inform, or does it authorize something irreversible? If it informs — a price, a feed, a count, a dashboard — you are in §11's table row 1 or 2, and the whole coordination conversation is over. Most flows end here.
  2. If it authorizes: can the invariant be divided into local allowances? If yes — a quantity, a budget, a stock level, a rate — use escrow, keep 1 ms local writes, keep selling under partition, and pay coordination only on rebalance and on the zero-allowance tail.
  3. If it cannot be divided — uniqueness, identity, "exactly one winner", a global ordering — then coordination is required, not chosen. Now, and only now, go and pay §12's bill, size the pool by §13's arithmetic, and pick the cheapest coordinated form from §11's table.

The mistake this lab exists to prevent is answering question 3's price before asking questions 1 and 2.

F7 — "My own change vanished" (the session failure). Create: any EL design, write in one region, then have that client's next read served by a different replica. Watch: Scenario 7 — Asha writes at t = 0, is told "saved" at t = 1 ms, and at t = 10 ms her refresh is routed through Frankfurt, which answers with the price she just replaced. Why: the ack came from replica A and the read was served by replica B, which had not received the update yet — no failure of any kind is involved. Guarantee kept: everything the system ever promised (recency was never promised). Lost: read-your-writes — a session guarantee that nobody wrote down and every user assumes. Production face, and this one is probably in your Java service today: a write to the primary followed, inside the same user request, by a read through a read replica (a @Transactional(readOnly = true) method, a routing DataSource, a reporting connection); a load balancer without sticky routing; a mobile client reconnecting through a different point of presence; or a CQRS read model whose projection has not caught up — the same bug promoted to an architecture. Detection is the hard part: server-side it is completely invisible — no error, no refusal, and replication lag may be well inside its normal band. You find it with a canary that writes and then immediately reads through the read path, and counts how often its own write is missing. Fixes, cheapest first: send that user's reads to the primary for a few seconds after their write, make routing sticky, or do it properly — carry a token and wait (§11's v5).

F8 — The staleness promise cannot be kept (bounded staleness's cliff). Create: run a v6 design (§11) and let a replica fall behind its promised bound Δ — a slow disk, a long GC pause, a re-indexing job, a burst of writes. Not simulated: the visualization implements v5, not v6, so this one is reasoned from the mechanism rather than watched. What happens: the replica knows it is past the bound (that is the whole point of the closed timestamp), and now has only two honest moves, both of which hurt. Refuse the read — and an EL design has just acquired PC-shaped behavior with no partition anywhere. Or fall back to the leader — which converts thousands of local 1 ms reads into cross-region coordinated reads at the exact moment the system is already struggling, so the fallback stampedes the component that is already behind. Azure documents a third option at the Bounded Staleness level: throttle the writers to hold the lag inside the bound — pushing back on the write path instead of the read path (vendor documentation, not measured here). Kept: the bound is never violated silently — the promise is honest to the end. Lost: read availability or read latency, in a correlated burst rather than a trickle. The mitigations are the ones you built in caching: single-flight, jitter, a shed valve — plus enough leader headroom to absorb the fallback you designed. And one design rule that follows directly: pick Δ larger than your one-way delay (the §11 table's row 3), or you have quietly built an EC system with extra steps.

F9 — The hidden cross-region hop (the placement failure). Create: move the leadership for a hot record to another region — a failover, a rolling restart, or an automatic rebalance does it for you. Watch: §12's decomposition, client → coordinator term. What happens: every write for those records silently pays one extra cross-region trip; correctness is untouched, latency doubles. Why: your code contains no mention of geography, and the first term of that decomposition appears in no profiler you own — the database still reports the same consistency level and the same internal timings. Kept: consistency, availability, everything the consistency model promises. Lost: your latency SLO, quietly and indefinitely. The signature is unmistakable once you know it: a bimodal latency histogram with the two peaks separated by exactly one RTT from your matrix, with error rates flat and nothing unhealthy. Production: a CockroachDB leaseholder that migrated, Kafka partition leaders sitting in the wrong AZ or region (its acks path is this lab's ack policy under another name), a MySQL primary failed over to the DR region and never failed back, a Cosmos DB write region that no longer matches where the traffic is. The dashboard that catches it is leaders per region, and it is the cheapest chart in this lab.

F10 — The consistency change that ate the capacity (the load failure). Create: take a busy endpoint and flip it from local reads to coordinated reads — ConsistentRead=true, readConcern: majority, LOCAL_QUORUM → QUORUM, synchronous_commit=remote_apply. Watch: §13's arithmetic; the simulator has no load model, so this one is computed, not watched. What happens: per-operation latency rises by the RTT (visible, expected, in the change ticket) and in-flight work rises by exactly the same factor (invisible, unbudgeted). If λW now exceeds the pool, ρ ≥ 1 and the queue grows without bound — the queueing-theory lab's F2, where throughput sits pinned at capacity looking perfectly healthy while the backlog climbs linearly forever. Then timeouts fire, callers retry, and offered load rises again: its F3, which reached 37,115 queued at 20 minutes from a single burst and did not recover when the trigger was removed. Why it fools people: every component is behaving exactly as specified. The database is answering at its documented consistency level and its documented latency; nothing is broken; the service is down. Guarantee kept: consistency — perfectly. Lost: the service. On-call sees connection-pool timeouts (Hikari's 30 s connectionTimeout), a thread pool at 100%, and a database that looks idle, because 200 of your threads are waiting on the network rather than on it. Dashboard: split latency into queue time vs service time, alarm on in-flight count against the pool size, and treat every consistency-level change as a resize. Cheapest structural defence: a separate pool for coordinated calls (bulkhead), so the 5% of traffic that needs EC cannot starve the 95% that does not.

F11 — The oversell (the invariant that reconciliation cannot repair). Create: any PA/EL design, a stock count, a partition, sales on both sides. Watch: Scenario 10 in Tab 2 — Mumbai sells 6 and Virginia sells 5 out of ten necklaces, both sides acknowledging every sale in ~1 ms. What happens at heal: the data converges exactly as designed — LWW keeps one count — and eleven customers hold receipts. Why it is a different failure from F4: F4 loses an acknowledged write, which is bad but bounded by the data. F11 has already moved goods, taken money, and sent emails on the authority of a stale read, and there is no merge function for a van. What survives: availability, latency, and eventual convergence — every promise the system made. What is lost: an invariant the system never knew it had. Response, in the order §14 lays them out: divide the invariant into allowances (escrow) if it is a quantity; coordinate if it is an identity; and if you deliberately keep accepting under partition, design the compensation before the incident — reservations that expire, a queue of provisional orders, idempotency keys, an explicit "confirmed" state distinct from "accepted" (saga, idempotency). Production face: double-booked seats, oversold flash-sale inventory, two accounts on one email address, a promo code redeemed twice, a limit checked on a read replica.

F12 — The stranded allowance (escrow's own accident). Create: split ten necklaces 4/3/3 and send six customers to Mumbai. Watch: Scenario 12. Mumbai refuses its fifth and sixth customers while two necklaces sit unsold in Frankfurt and Virginia — a refusal on a perfectly healthy network, with inventory in the building. Why: escrow bought locality by dividing the constraint, and a division is a bet on where demand will land. What is kept: the invariant, absolutely, and 1 ms sales. What is lost: utilization — and it is lost silently, since a refusal for "out of stock" looks identical whether the warehouse is empty or merely elsewhere. The metric that separates them is the one nobody builds by default: refusals while global stock > 0. Second, sharper form of the same accident: the background rebalancer moves allowance away from a region that is slow rather than dead, and now both regions may sell the same units — the distributed-locking stale-holder problem with physical goods attached, which is why allowance transfer must carry an ownership epoch or a lease the recipient waits out (partitioning-and-rebalancing). Also note the shape it shares with F8: both are mechanisms that keep their promise by refusing, so both need headroom monitoring — how close is each region to exhausting its allowance — rather than error-rate alarms that only fire after the customer has left.

F13 — The slow partition (the boundary dissolves). Create: any EC design; don't cut anything — just drag the RTT slider from 90 ms toward 2000 ms. Watch: latency climbs smoothly until the 1 s timeout starts firing, at which point v3 begins refusing (F3 behavior) and the degraded-ack design begins degrading (F6 behavior) — with no partition anywhere. Why: a node cannot tell "dead" from "slow" (distributed-locking, and now with its own lab: flp-impossibility, where a slow process and a dead one produce literally identical evidence); the only partition detector that exists is a timeout. What this teaches: the P in PACELC is not a physical event, it's a threshold you configured. Which is the door into §16's unification.

🔮 Predict: Given F13 — if a partition is just "latency above my timeout", is CAP's A-vs-C choice and PACELC's L-vs-C choice really two different tradeoffs, or one tradeoff at two points on a dial?

Reveal

One tradeoff. Unavailability is unbounded latency; a partition is the far end of the latency axis. CAP's choice is what you do when the coordination price goes to infinity; PACELC's else-clause is what you pay when it's finite. Abadi's own framing points here, and it's the deepest thing in this lab: the fundamental tradeoff of replicated systems is consistency versus response time; a partition just moves the price. The four classes are really answers to "how much will you pay before you stop paying?" — PC/EC says "any price", PA/EL says "nothing, ever", and the other two say "it depends which operation".

16. CORRECTNESS — what PACELC is, and honestly is not

CAP is a theorem; PACELC is not. Gilbert & Lynch proved CAP (2002), with precise definitions: C = linearizability, A = every request to a non-failed node eventually gets a response, P = arbitrary message loss. Under those definitions the impossibility is real math. PACELC's else-half has no impossibility proof of that shape — it is a design-space taxonomy: an organized, honest description of a tradeoff, not a statement that some combination is impossible.

But the else-half is not merely vibes either. Two hard facts anchor it:

  1. A lower bound exists. Attiya & Welch (1994) proved that in a network with message-delay uncertainty, a linearizable shared register cannot have both its reads and its writes answer faster than a constant fraction of that delay — coordination latency is provably unavoidable for linearizability, not an implementation artifact. (Stated here from memory of the result's shape; the constants and exact model are in the paper — "Sequential consistency versus linearizability", ACM TOCS 1994 — and the lesson survives any constant: the speed of light is in the contract.)
  2. The bills are measurable and you measured them. 11.7 → 24.4 ticks (ABD write-back), 6.7 → 24.8 (commit wait), 2× (DynamoDB strong reads), 27.31% vs 2.85% (tail at 3-of-3 vs 2-of-3). The else-clause tradeoff has receipts.
  3. And there is a second lower bound, of a different kind — one that tells you when the bill is due at all. Attiya–Welch says if you want linearizability you must pay delay. I-confluence (§14; Bailis et al., VLDB 2014) says whether your application needs to pay: an invariant is maintainable with no coordination exactly when merging independently-valid states is always valid. Together they bracket the subject — one bounds the price of coordination, the other bounds the need for it. This is why §14's three questions come before PACELC's two.

What "C" means here — and the ladder underneath it. This lab's judge is recency (linearizability's real-time rule, single key). But the C in the else-clause is really a dial, and every rung you've climbed has a different price: full linearizability (the prices above) → bounded staleness (a promised ceiling — Cosmos DB contracts it; costs enforcement) → causal (the strongest model that stays fully available under partition — causal-consistency, 600/600 minority-side answers — its everyday price being dependency metadata and the slowdown cascade, not round trips) → session guarantees (nearly free with stickiness) → plain EC (free, un-promised). PACELC's binary EL/EC is a teaching simplification of this dial, the same way CAP's binary was — and Abadi says as much in the paper.

Two axes, not one. §11's session guarantees do not sit anywhere on the EL↔EC line, and trying to place them there is the most common confusion in this whole subject. The line measures staleness against real time, for everybody. Session guarantees measure something else entirely:

What is being promised Who it is promised to
EL … bounded staleness … EC Freshness measured against the clock Every reader, including one who has never touched the data
Session guarantees Consistency with a history One client, about its own writes and its own past reads

A system can be at the far EL end of the first axis and fully strict on the second — that is exactly §11's v5, and Scenario 8 shows both facts on screen at once (stale reads climbing, own-change-vanished stuck at zero). The formal relationship, which lets you re-use the causal-consistency lab whole: causal consistency implies all four session guarantees, because a client's own history is a special case of causal order — the special case with no concurrent branches, which is precisely why it is cheap enough to buy with one token instead of a dependency graph.

PACELC letters belong to a flow, not to a system — per-path classification. The read path and the write path can, and usually do, land in different cells — and a system described by one four-letter tag is being described inaccurately:

This is why "what class is Postgres?" has no answer and "what does this flow wait for?" always does.

PACELC classifies replicas, not invariants — the category error worth being loud about. The letters describe what copies of the data promise each other. They say nothing about whether your business rule can break. Both directions of the mistake are common and expensive:

  • A PA/EL system with escrow (§14's v7) can hold "never sell the eleventh necklace" absolutely, through partitions, at 1 ms per sale. Its letters have not changed; its replicas are still stale.
  • A PC/EC system oversells happily the moment application code reads a row, thinks, and writes another without a transaction, a conditional write, or a lock. Linearizable storage is not a linearizable application — you also measured this as the linearizability lab's lost increments (read-modify-write over a store that was behaving correctly).

So the honest sentence is: PACELC tells you what a read costs and what it may be missing; it does not tell you whether your invariant holds. For that, §14's three questions.

What the on-call engineer watches, per letter:

  • EL: staleness AGE (replication lag age, t-visibility probes under injected stress — the eventual-consistency lab's machinery-heartbeat rule). Nothing errors when EL fails you; only lag age tells the truth.
  • EC: write/read p99 vs the RTT floor. An EC system's latency SLO is a physics commitment — a Mumbai↔Virginia quorum cannot beat ~90 ms, and promising 50 ms is promising to break the SLO.
  • PC: refusal rates per region during incidents (they should be loud, scoped, and alarmed — a quiet PC incident means clients aren't retrying elsewhere).
  • PA: divergence artifacts at heal — sibling counts, rollback-file growth, conflict-resolution discard counts. Every discard is an acknowledged promise broken; count them like dropped payments.
  • Session guarantees: unobservable from the server — no error, no lag alarm — so you need a canary that writes and then immediately reads through the read path, counting how often its own write is missing, plus the sticky-routing hit rate. If nobody is running that canary, F7 is happening and you will hear about it from a user, not a dashboard.
  • Bounded staleness: headroom, not lag — how close is the worst replica to the promised bound, as a percentage. An alarm at 80% of Δ is the only warning you get before F8's cliff, where the choice narrows to refusing reads or stampeding the leader.
  • Placement: leaders (or leaseholders, or primaries) per region, and a latency histogram you actually look at rather than a p99 you glance at — F9 shows up as a second peak exactly one RTT away, and as nothing else at all.
  • The load axis (§13): in-flight operations against the pool size (that is λW, and it is the number that moves when anyone changes a consistency level), and latency split into queue time vs service time — if you add one metric to a coordinated path, add time_in_queue. Alarm on spare capacity, not utilization; measure goodput, not throughput, because throughput stays flat right through the collapse (queueing-theory).
  • Divided invariants (§14): refusals while the resource still exists elsewhere — the single metric that separates "genuinely sold out" from "stranded in the wrong region" (F12) — plus each region's allowance headroom, the rebalance rate, and an oversell counter that should be identically zero and must be alarmed as a correctness signal rather than reconciled in a nightly job.

What this lab's toy omits (and where each omission lives in reality): multiple keys and transactions (cross-key ordering — your distributed-transactions and causal-consistency labs); real clocks and skew (the sim has one perfect global clock — a luxury no real system has; hybrid-logical-clocks); partial/gray failures and asymmetric partitions (the sim's partition is a clean box around one region); node recovery and catch-up (anti-entropy — gossip-protocols, quorums-and-replication); the ABD read write-back on the majority design (flagged in §10); bounded staleness — the sim implements v5's session tokens but not v6's closed-timestamp beacons, so §11's beacon trace and F8 are reasoned, not watched; more than three regions, so §12's five-region arithmetic is done on paper; load and queueing — §13 is entirely arithmetic, because neither simulator has an arrival rate, a service time or a pool (RTT here is constant and every operation runs alone; real p99 is RTT plus queues, and the queueing-theory lab's simulator is where that half was measured); and, in Tab 2, money, settlement, expiring reservations and the rebalancer itself — allowance is moved by hand there so that the transfer's fencing problem (F12) stays visible rather than being automated away.

Commonly confused, one sentence each:

  • PACELC vs CAP: CAP is PACELC's first clause; PACELC adds the question that applies the other 99.99% of the time.
  • EL vs "eventually consistent": EL is the choice (don't wait); eventual consistency is the resulting contract (the weakest promise — its own lab).
  • EC (else-consistency) vs EC (eventual consistency): the acronym collision is real and unfortunate — in PACELC writing, EC always means else-Consistency; this lab never uses it the other way.
  • PC vs "down": PC systems refuse the side that can't coordinate; the majority side stays up — PC is a scoped, honest outage, not a global one.
  • Latency vs availability: the same axis at two scales — unavailability is unbounded latency (F13/§16), which is why the two clauses rhyme.
  • Session consistency vs strong consistency: session guarantees promise one client its own history; strong consistency promises every client the present. A system can give the first perfectly while every stranger reads stale data (Scenario 8 shows both at once).
  • Bounded staleness vs eventual consistency: both serve stale data; only one of them tells you the maximum age and can be alarmed on. "Eventually" is a direction, Δ is a number.
  • Consistency vs an invariant: consistency is a promise replicas make to each other about a value; an invariant is a promise the application makes about the world. Escrow keeps the second while breaking the first, and a read-modify-write over linearizable storage does the reverse (§16).
  • Coordination-free vs consistent: I-confluence asks whether coordination is needed; PACELC asks what it costs when it is. A flow can be coordination-free and stale (escrow), or coordinated and still wrong (an unguarded read-modify-write).
  • Latency vs capacity: the same coordination shows up twice — as milliseconds on one operation (§12) and as a multiplier on everything in flight (§13). Only the first one is in the benchmark.

invariant A rule the application must never break (never sell the eleventh necklace) — a different object from replica consistency
informing vs authorizing read A stale read that informs can be repaired by a later read; one that authorizes an irreversible act cannot be repaired at all
escrow / reservation Divide a quantity into per-region allowances whose local rules imply the global invariant; local writes, no coordination, no violation (O'Neil 1986)
stranded allowance Escrow's accident: a refusal while the resource still exists in another region — measured as refusals while global stock > 0 (F12)
I-confluence An invariant needs no coordination exactly when merging independently-valid states is always valid (Bailis et al., VLDB 2014)
CALM A program has a coordination-free implementation iff it is monotonic; negation — a claim about absence — is what forces coordination
divide the constraint The transferable move: replace a global invariant with local ones whose conjunction implies it. Quantities divide; identities do not
per-path classification Read path and write path get their own PACELC letters; a one-tag system description is inaccurate
Attiya–Welch bound Proof that linearizable reads+writes can't both beat a fraction of message delay
unification Unavailability = unbounded latency; a partition is the far end of the latency dial
rollback files MongoDB's artifact of F6: partition-era acked writes set aside at heal for a human

PACELC — Solutions

Rungs 7–8 are worked design discussions, not single correct answers.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 CAP only speaks about the moment the network stops delivering messages: then you must pick between refusing to answer and answering possibly-wrong. PACELC adds: and when the network is healthy — which is almost always — do you make requests wait for cross-replica coordination, or answer immediately and accept that some answers are stale? It mattered because teams justified everyday staleness (a latency choice they were making) with a partition argument (a constraint that wasn't operating), which made the real tradeoff untunable and undiscussable.

1.2 "CAP constrains us only while a partition is happening, and we haven't had one; our reads are stale because we chose not to make writers or readers wait for replication — a latency-versus-consistency choice. That choice may well be right, but it's ours, it has a measurable window, and we should defend it as a latency decision, not blame a theorem that's off duty."

1.3 Someone waits — the writer, the reader, or nobody; and if nobody waits, somebody gets stale. "Someone" = the writer (coordinated acks: majority/all/home-forwarded writes), the reader (quorum or leader reads), or nobody (local everything — EL).

1.4 The first user lost read-your-writes: they were served by a copy that had not yet received their own change. The second lost monotonic reads: successive answers came from copies at different points in the same timeline, so a value they had already been shown un-happened. Neither is answered by "eventually consistent", because that sentence is a promise about the system converging, made to nobody in particular — it says nothing about one person's own history, which is the only thing either user is complaining about. Both are fixable for roughly nothing (a token and, at worst, one catch-up wait); neither requires paying the coordination bill on every operation.

1.5 A stale read that informs tells you something you can be told again — a price, a count on a dashboard — so the next refresh repairs it. A stale read that authorizes is used to justify an act that happens outside the database: goods ship, money moves, a seat is given away. The data can be corrected afterwards; the act cannot. Refreshing repairs the first because the only wrong thing was a copy; it cannot repair the second because the wrong thing is now in the world.

1.6 Because it names a requirement (never sell the eleventh) and then jumps to one particular mechanism (coordinate every sale) without checking whether cheaper ones satisfy the same requirement. The question to ask first is: can this invariant be divided? A stock count, a spend limit, a rate, a budget — anything that is a quantity — can be split into per-region allowances whose local rules imply the global one, which keeps 1 ms local writes and survives partitions. Coordination becomes genuinely necessary only when the invariant is about an identity ("this username is mine", "exactly one winner"), because you cannot give a region 40% of a username.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 Big colored dots are value-carrying messages (the repl copies of the price, colored by the writing region); small hollow dots are confirmations and read queries (ack, rreq, rrep). The write becomes official at the arrival of the first ack back from another region — that's when acks reach 2 of 3 (the coordinator's own counts as the first).

2.2 P → C and E → C are lit. Evidence for E→C: the write-ack latency readout (~110 ms from Mumbai — the coordination bill) and zero stale reads. Evidence for P→C: cut a region off and its operations end in REFUSED (the refused counter), while the other two continue.

2.3 Home region's writes are ~1 ms (master is local); the other two pay their full RTT to home (Frankfurt 110 ms, Virginia 190 ms with home=Mumbai). The number is in each client box's "last write ack" readout.

2.4 (a) Asha's client box shows session token: write #N (t = 0 ms) — the stamp of the newest write she was told was saved. (b) The ⏳ marker sits on Frankfurt, the city serving her read, because that copy is behind the token. (c) The wait is however long remains of Frankfurt's 55 ms one-way delay — 45 ms if she reads 10 ms after writing. Meanwhile the stale reads counter keeps climbing on other clients' reads while "your own change vanished" stays 0: the system is still returning out-of-date answers to everyone else, and it is still an EL system. What was fixed was one client's view of its own history, not the system's freshness.

2.5 Sell-now-tell-later: nowhere. There is no place in the design that could refuse the eleventh sale, which is why no failure is needed to break the rule — that is the point of running it once. Head-office approval: the head office's book — one machine's counter, consulted before every sale, and the single thing whose failure or unreachability stops sales entirely. Split-the-stock: each branch's own allowance check — three independent places, none of which needs the other two to be reachable, correct, or even alive.

2.6 Nobody. Frankfurt decrements the moment it sends, Mumbai increments only on arrival, and for the 55 ms in between the two necklaces are unsellable. That is the only safe direction: if both ends counted the allowance as theirs during the flight, the same two necklaces could be sold twice — the transfer would have created stock. Losing 55 ms of sellability is a cost paid in utilization; the alternative is paid in oversells, which are not recoverable. (Same asymmetry as a lease handover: always leave a gap, never an overlap.)

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 At 10×, the Mumbai↔Frankfurt round trip becomes 1100 ms — above the 1000 ms timeout — so the majority write is refused with no partition anywhere. Identical in kind to a partition because the system's only partition detector is the timeout: "partition" operationally means "latency above my configured threshold". The boundary is the 1000 ms constant, a config value, not a physical event. (This is F13 and §16's unification: a partition is the far end of the latency dial.)

4.2 Home = Frankfurt: Frankfurt writes ~1 ms; Mumbai 110 ms; Virginia 90 ms. A per-record master should live where that record's writes originate — write locality. (PNUTS migrated a record's master toward its observed writers for exactly this reason.)

4.3 Up. Majority of 5 is 3, so a write needs two remote acks — you wait for your second-nearest neighbor, not your nearest, and the tail gets worse because more independent slow-downs can catch you (the quorums lab's tail measurement: the more replicas you wait on, the more often one of them is having a bad moment). More regions buy durability and read locality, and raise coordinated-write latency.

4.4 Her read parks at Virginia, Virginia can never receive Mumbai's update while it is cut off, and at 1000 ms the read is refused. The rule: every guarantee you add is also an availability liability. A PA/EL system that promises nothing can always answer; the moment it promises a client something ("you will never see your own past"), there exist situations where the only honest move is to refuse — a promise that cannot be kept has to be declined, not quietly broken. This is the same shape as F13 at a smaller scale: the refusal appears when a wait crosses the configured timeout.

4.5 The ack falls to 12 ms (each one-way hop is 55 × 0.1 ≈ 6 ms). Re-answer: "strong consistency is too slow" is mostly a claim about how far apart you put the voting replicas, not about consistency — the consensus tax is one round trip between them, and if they are 6 ms apart it is 12 ms. What you gave up to make it cheap: independence of failures (three replicas within one continent share power grids, network backbones, provider region-groups and weather), and the fact that distant users now pay the trip to reach that quorum — the first term of the decomposition, which no consistency setting can remove.

4.6 Leader in Mumbai: 95 (Virginia → Mumbai) + 110 (Mumbai's quorum round trip to Frankfurt) + 95 (back to Omar) = 300 ms. Leader in Virginia: ~0 + 90 (Virginia's quorum round trip to Frankfurt) + ~0 = 90 ms. The consensus tax barely moved (110 → 90); what changed is the first and third terms — 190 ms of pure travel to reach the coordinator, a cost that appears in no consistency setting, no query plan, and no profiler. Placement, not consistency level, is where that latency lived.

4.7 Sales coordinated: zero — every sale is local, including the two after the top-up. Transfers coordinated: one. At 200 sales/day with blocks of 20, that is 10 coordinated operations per 200 sales, a ratio of 1:20, and none of them is on a customer's critical path — a slow transfer delays a refill, not a sale. Head-office approval coordinates 200 times per 200 sales, 1:1, every one of them in front of a waiting customer. That ratio — coordination per key per node per interval versus coordination per request — is exactly the economics your distributed-rate-limiting lab measured, and it is why the two designs are cheap in opposite regimes: escrow wins when a key is hot (blocks amortize), per-request coordination wins for the cold long tail (a block would sit stranded).

4.8 (a) L = λW: before, 1,500 × 0.003 = 4.5 in flight; after, 1,500 × 0.090 = 135 in flight. A 30× multiplication of every per-request resource, in a change that mentions none of them. (b) c / W: before, 60 / 0.003 = 20,000 req/s; after, 60 / 0.090 = 667 req/s. (c) ρ = 1,500 / 667 = 2.25. The queue does not settle at a higher latency — it grows without bound, linearly, for as long as the traffic lasts; throughput pins at 667/s and every request beyond that waits, then times out. (d) c ≥ λW / ρ_target = 1,500 × 0.09 / 0.8 = 169 connections (and now 169 concurrent database sessions is a conversation with the DBA, not a properties file). (e) In-flight count against pool capacity (equivalently, pool utilization / queue depth). p99 latency is not that metric because it is a lagging one: it looks fine until saturation, then goes vertical — it tells you the collapse has already started. In-flight count moves the instant W moves, which is at deploy time.

Only the write path knows what it has sent, so only the write path can certify what is missing.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

Concrete run: at t=0 Virginia's inbound link is congested (or cut — the sim's partition control). Asha writes ₹54,000 at t=0; Frankfurt's repl lands at 55 and its ack completes the majority at t=110: write acknowledged — {Mumbai, Frankfurt} have it; Virginia does not, and nothing in the protocol waits for it. At t=200 Omar reads locally in Virginia: returns ₹50,000, while a write acknowledged 90 ms before his read began says ₹54,000. Recency rule violated.

The false step is "since writes are majority-committed, reads are consistent": a majority write guarantees two replicas have the value — it says nothing about the third, and a local read has no way to know whether it's standing on one of the two. Consistency needs the read to intersect the write set (R+W>N — the overlap argument), or to be routed to a leader. As written, the system's read path is EL (with an unbounded window under partition): it is PC/E(mixed) at best, and its marketing is writing checks its reads don't cash. This is exactly why the sim's majority design quorum-reads, and why ZooKeeper — majority writes, local reads — documents that its reads are stale unless you sync() (linearizability lab).

6.2 Wrong thing one: "consistent" with no answer to "for whom?". Session guarantees are promises to one client about its own history; two users looking at the same account can still see different values, and a client that has never written gets nothing beyond not moving backwards. The document silently upgrades a per-client promise into a global one.

Wrong thing two, which is the dangerous one: the fraud engine's decision depends on somebody else's write. Concrete run — the customer's other device authorizes ₹40,000 in Mumbai at t = 0, acknowledged at t = 1 ms. At t = 40 ms the checkout in Virginia asks the fraud engine, which reads the local Virginia replica; the debit lands there at t = 95 ms, so the engine sees the pre-debit balance and approves. Both payments are approved against the same money. The session token was never violated — the checkout session made no writes — and that is precisely why it protects nothing here.

Smallest change to the requirement: stop asking for "the balance" and ask for "a balance at most Δ old, with Δ larger than the one-way delay", accepting the bounded exposure that implies (a soft limit, reconciled after the fact). Then a local bounded-stale read is legitimate and cheap. The real invariant stays where it belongs — a conditional write at the ledger, which rejects the second debit whatever the fraud engine believed (the optimistic-locking lab's rule: the guarantee lives at the resource).

6.3 First half — the CRDT claim. A CRDT guarantees convergence: every replica that has seen the same set of operations computes the same value, with no lost updates and no arbitrary conflict-resolution casualties. It guarantees nothing whatsoever about an invariant, because each replica applies its own operation locally before it knows about the others'. Smallest run: stock is 1; Mumbai and Virginia each apply −1 at the same instant; both are legal against every local view; the merge is a perfectly converged, perfectly agreed −1. The design document has confused "no updates are lost" with "no rule is broken" — and it is precisely because nothing is lost that the oversell is undeniable. (Escrow is the fix, and note what it really does: it stops the decision being local while keeping the write local.)

Second half — the rebalancer. "Quiet for 30 seconds" is a timeout, and a timeout cannot distinguish a region with no customers from a region that is slow, garbage-collecting, or partitioned from the job (F13, and the flp-impossibility lab's whole point). Run: Virginia holds an allowance of 3 and is partitioned from the rebalancer but not from its own customers; at 30 s the job reassigns Virginia's 3 to Mumbai; Mumbai sells them; Virginia sells them too. Three necklaces sold twice, no error anywhere. The missing mechanism is fencing: an ownership epoch on each allowance, so a transfer bumps the epoch and any sale stamped with the old epoch is rejected — distributed-locking's fencing token and partitioning-and-rebalancing's ownership epoch, both of which exist for exactly this run. The weaker, cheaper alternative: make allowances leases with an expiry, so the recipient waits out the old holder's lease before selling — safety from the clock instead of from a token, at the cost of the wait.

Rung 7 — Handle failure (worked design)

The root defect of the stock PA/EC degrade is that partition-era writes enter the same last-write-wins register as everything else, so heal-time merge silently kills acknowledged promises. Redesign:

  1. The degraded ack changes meaning and shape. On timeout, the coordinator does not pretend the write is globally committed. It durably appends the order to a local, partition-scoped queue (an outbox — the transactional-outbox lab's shape) with a client idempotency key (idempotency lab), and acks as "accepted", a documented weaker state. Orders are appends with fresh identities, never overwrites of a shared register — concurrent adds on two sides then have no conflict to lose (the eventual-consistency lab's cart lesson: model it as a set/log, and two sides' additions union instead of fighting).
  2. What the isolated side may promise: "your order is accepted and will be confirmed" — acceptance, not fulfillment. Anything that requires the global invariant (inventory guarantees, payment capture) is deferred or done at-most-once with reconciliation.
  3. At heal, no LWW. The queue is replayed through the normal ordered path; idempotency keys make replay harmless; a replay that now violates a business rule (out of stock) becomes a surfaced compensation (saga lab), not a silent discard. Discard count target: zero, structurally.
  4. Two paging signals: (a) degraded-ack rate > 0 — the degrade must be an alarmed event, never a silent if; (b) oldest unreconciled partition-era order AGE — the reaper-style age alarm (idempotency lab): a queue that isn't draining after heal is the real emergency.

What this redesign concedes: during the partition you are no longer PA/EC for the invariant — you are PA with an explicit, bounded reconciliation debt. That is the honest version of "we never stop taking orders."

7.2 The shape is a metastable failure: a fallback that transfers load onto the very component whose slowness triggered the fallback, so the system stays broken after the original trigger is gone. It is the caching lab's thundering herd with a feedback loop — and structurally the same runaway as the gossip lab's failure-detector meltdown, with reads as the payload.

Three mechanisms, all already yours: (1) single-flight at the primary — one in-flight fetch per key, everyone else waits on it, so N replicas' worth of duplicate reads collapse to one; (2) a fallback budget with jittered backoff — only a capped fraction of reads may promote to the primary, the rest get a stale answer with a visible "as of" marker or an honest refusal (load shedding beats collapse, and admission control should prioritise the replication traffic, because that is what ends the incident); (3) rate-limit the batch job itself — it is the actual input, and the distributed-rate-limiting lab is the tool.

Δ should have been set from the measured p99.9 replication lag under peak load, plus headroom — not from a round number. If the batch pushes lag to 30 s every Monday, a 2 s bound guarantees a weekly cliff; either raise Δ above the real peak or fix the peak, but do not discover it at 09:00. The dashboard that warns days earlier is lag as a percentage of Δ (headroom), alarmed at 80% — a number that would have been visibly creeping up for weeks. Raw lag in milliseconds hides this; the ratio is what matters.

7.3 (a) At 09:14, W for that endpoint goes from a few ms to ~90 ms. λ is unchanged, so L = λW rises by the same factor — every in-flight request now holds its connection ~30× longer. Within seconds the pool is fully occupied; new requests queue for a connection rather than for the database. Queue time (invisible in the database's metrics, because those requests have not reached it) becomes most of the response time. Callers hit their 600 ms timeout, retry twice, and the offered load triples on a component already at ρ > 1. (b) Because flat throughput with a rising error rate is the signature of saturation, not failure. A broken dependency makes throughput fall; a capacity ceiling holds it exactly level while the backlog grows behind it — which is why the graph looks reassuring and is the worst news on the page. Goodput, meanwhile, is collapsing: most of the work being completed is for callers who have already given up. (c) Because the system is now metastable (the queueing-theory lab's F3): the retry backlog is self-sustaining, so removing the original trigger leaves the loop running. What restores it is breaking the loop — shed load hard (to ~0.85 of capacity, not 0.95: a timid shed took 18 minutes where a hard one took 6), or drain traffic entirely and restart, which is why "turn it off and on again" genuinely works here. (d) Guardrails: (1) treat the consistency flag as a capacity change — compute λW and resize the pool (or a concurrency limiter) in the same change, and roll it out to a fraction of traffic first; (2) bulkhead the coordinated path into its own pool so it cannot consume the connections the other 95% of traffic needs. And the one that makes the rollback work: a retry budget plus deadline propagation, so that when the path is slow the system stops manufacturing its own load and a rollback is enough.

Reads of the ledger for display stay EL; reads that decide (available balance before authorizing) go through the same home master — the eventual-consistency lab's F7 rule closing the loop.

8.2 — placement (worked discussion).

(a) With the ledger's leader for every account in Mumbai, using the three-term decomposition: a Mumbai customer pays 0 + 110 + 0 = 110 ms; a Frankfurt customer 55 + 110 + 55 = 220 ms; a Virginia customer 95 + 110 + 95 = 300 ms. Same code, same consistency level, 2.7× spread — and the spread is invisible in the database's own metrics.

(b) Rule: each account's leader lives in the account holder's home region (v4's per-record master, applied as a placement policy — this is CockroachDB's REGIONAL BY ROW and Spanner's leader placement). The common case collapses to 0 + nearest-peer RTT + 0: 110 ms for Mumbai-led, 90 ms for Frankfurt- and Virginia-led accounts. What gets worse: the traveller (a Mumbai account holder transacting in Virginia still pays the full 300 ms), and — more seriously — a transfer between two accounts in different regions now spans two leaders, which is a distributed transaction, priced in the two-phase-commit and distributed-transactions labs, not a single ledger write.

(c) Under data residency, an account's replicas may not leave its region, so a three-region majority for that account is simply illegal. You move to three replicas inside the region (or region-group): EC gets cheaper (single-digit ms — Rung 4.5's lesson arriving as a compliance requirement), and you lose cross-region durability for that account, which is now a business decision rather than an engineering one. What becomes impossible to promise: a globally atomic, linearizable cross-region transfer. The best available promise is atomic-per-side with an idempotent, reconciled, visibly in-flight transfer — the saga shape, with the in-flight state exposed to the customer rather than hidden.

8.4 — the three questions, applied (worked discussion).

Promotional stock, 10,000 units sold globally. Q1: it authorizes — a unit ships. Q2: it is a quantity, so it divides. Stop at question 2. Mechanism: allocate blocks per region sized to about 10–20 minutes of that region's expected sales (say 3,000 / 4,000 / 3,000, refilled in blocks of 500 from a central pool), each region enforcing "units sold from my block ≤ my block"; the sum of the blocks plus the unallocated pool never exceeds 10,000, which is the argument that the local rules imply the global one. At zero, the region does not silently fail: it requests a refill (fast path) and, if the pool is unreachable, refuses — this is the one moment coordination is on the critical path, and it should be a few percent of sales, not all of them. Failure it can still have: stranding — a region holding 800 units nobody wants while another refuses customers (F12); the detecting metric is refusals-while-global-pool

0, plus per-region block headroom. And if the requirement flips to never under-sell, the design inverts: stranding is now the primary risk rather than an inefficiency, so you shrink blocks (more frequent, smaller refills — coordination traded back in), add aggressive reclaim of idle blocks with fenced transfers, and near the end of the campaign switch to coordinated allocation from the central pool. Never-over-sell and never-under-sell pull the block size in opposite directions, and no single size satisfies both — which is the honest answer to give the business.

Per-customer daily spend limit. Q1: authorizes. Q2: also a quantity — but the natural division is not geographic, it is per customer, and a customer's spend limit divides badly across regions (giving Mumbai ₹40,000 of Asha's ₹100,000 limit strands most of it, because one customer is usually active in one place at a time). The better move is the one the distributed-rate-limiting lab reached: route the key to an owner. Give each customer a home region that holds the whole limit; authorization for that customer coordinates only by going to their home region — one trip, no quorum, and the invariant is enforced in a single place. That is v4's per-record master used as an invariant-enforcement strategy rather than a replication strategy: the flow becomes PC/EL (refuse when the home region is unreachable, and note that this is the correct answer for a spend limit — refusing to authorize is survivable, double-spending a limit is not). What makes it work is that spend limits are a cold, long-tail key space: almost every customer is inactive almost always, so blocks would strand and a per-request trip to one owner is cheap. The general lesson worth extracting: the division that matters is not always by region — divide by whatever axis the demand naturally partitions along, and if it does not partition at all, own it in one place.
