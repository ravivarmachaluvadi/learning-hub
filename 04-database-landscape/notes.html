Lab 4 · The Ground Floor

The Database Landscape — how to choose a data store, and when to say no

Tell a friend: Meera ran a bookshop with one big notebook and wrote every event into it in the order it happened. That worked until customers started asking four different kinds of question, and three of them meant reading the whole notebook. So she kept four books instead of one, each arranged the way one question wanted. Every database on earth is one of Meera's books: it arranges bytes so its favourite question is nearly free, and quietly makes the other questions expensive. This lab teaches you to name the question first, pick the book second, and know which book must never hold the truth.

The trainer is visualization.html: eight scenarios to diagnose before the answer shows. Three runnable proofs live in experiments/. What is in the folder, how the numbers were made, and where this lab sits among your others are all in section 19 at the end.

BEGINNER MODEL

1. Why are there fifty kinds of database?

In one breath: The cloud console offers you a bewildering menu, and the pick you make is the hardest thing in the whole system to undo. The menu is not fifty random products. It is about a dozen ideas, and each one exists for a reason you can learn in an afternoon.

Open any cloud console and read the storage menu. Relational. Key-value. Document. Wide-column. Graph. Time-series. Search. Warehouse. Lake. Streaming. Vector. Eleven words, and each one hides several products.

Teams routinely pick wrong. That would be forgivable if it were cheap. It is not, and the reason is specific.

A database choice is the hardest decision in a system to reverse. Code can be refactored in an afternoon. Migrating a terabyte of live data between stores with different data models is a multi-month project with downtime risk. So a team that picks a store because it is trendy, or because it is the one they already know, ends up in one of two places. They fight their database every day. Or they rebuild on a new one under pressure.

Now the good news. The menu is not fifty unrelated things. It is about a dozen categories, and each category exists for one clear reason. By the end of this document you will look at a workload and name the category it wants. Just as importantly, you will name the categories that would quietly ruin you.

We get there through one shop and one company. Meera runs the shop. Chaptr is what the shop became when it went online, and Priya, Dev, Asha, Omar and Ben are the people who keep it alive. Arjun is the customer they keep arguing about. Everything in this lab happens to those seven.

2. What breaks in Meera's one big notebook?

In one breath: Meera writes everything into one book, in date order. Four different questions arrive. Three of them force her to read the whole book, because the book is arranged for the fourth.

Meera runs a corner bookshop and keeps one big ledger book. Every event goes in it, one line after another. Sold two novels to Arjun. Received a box from the distributor. Priya asked for "that blue book about trains". Noted that Arjun likes mysteries.

One notebook. Perfectly fine. Then the shop grows, and four different questions start hitting the same notebook.

The first is her accountant's. "How much money did we make in March?" Meera flips through every page, picking out just the amounts, adding as she goes. One question, an hour of flipping.

The second is a customer at the counter. "Do we have 'The Blue Train' in stock?" Now she flips backwards, looking for the last time that title was bought or sold. Another whole flip-through.

The third is Priya's. "Which books mention trains?" The ledger is ordered by date, not by topic. There is no way to answer it without reading everything she has ever written.

The fourth is about Arjun. "He liked these mysteries — what should I recommend?" Meera would have to trace from Arjun, to the books he bought, to other customers who bought those books, to what they bought. Hopping like that through a date-ordered notebook is torture.

Notice what is actually happening here. The notebook is not "too small". It is shaped wrong for three of the four questions. Each question wants the information physically arranged a different way.

🔮 Predict: Meera buys a second, much bigger notebook and hires an assistant to help her flip. Which of the four questions gets better?

Reveal Only the second one, and only a little, because two people can flip in parallel. If you guessed "all of them, twice as fast", you are thinking about *how much* work there is. The problem is *what shape* the work is. Question 1 still reads every page to find the amounts. Question 3 still has no path from a word to a book. Question 4 still has no thread from a person to a person. More notebook does not create an arrangement that was never there.

3. Why is "one database, just make it bigger" the right first move?

In one breath: Meera's shop went online as Chaptr, and everything went into one relational database. That was not a mistake. It is the correct opening move, and it survives longer than most people expect.

Back to software. Meera's shop became Chaptr, an online bookstore, and Priya and Dev built it. Like almost every sensible startup, they put everything in one relational database: PostgreSQL. Users, books, orders, page views, search, everything. Tables, foreign keys, transactions.

To be clear, and this matters more than anything else in the section: this is the right starting move, not a mistake. One store. One backup. One thing to learn. Transactions keep money-related data correct for free.

Chaptr grows. Things get slow. Priya reaches for the plan every team tries first, in this order:

  • Slow query? Add an index.
  • Still slow? Buy a bigger server.
  • Still slow? Add read replicas, which are copies of the database that serve reads.

This works for a surprisingly long time. Years, sometimes. A single well-tuned PostgreSQL box comfortably handles tens of thousands of queries per second. Most apps never outgrow it, which is why "just use Postgres" is genuinely good default advice rather than laziness.

But Chaptr keeps growing. And growth does not send more of the same queries. It sends new shapes of query.

4. How does one database die four different ways?

In one breath: Chaptr's single database had four bad years in one year. Each incident is one of Meera's four questions arriving in software. No bigger server fixes any of them, because the problem is the arrangement of the data and not the amount of hardware under it.

Four incidents, four people, one diagnosis. Watch which of Meera's questions each one is.

Incident 1 — the dashboard that ate the database. This is Meera's question 1, the totals. Asha runs finance, and she wants a dashboard: revenue per day, per category, per region, over two years. That query touches 40 million order rows. PostgreSQL stores data row by row, so each order's id, user, book, price, address and timestamp sit together on disk. To sum just the price column, the database must read entire rows. It drags the addresses and timestamps through memory only to throw them away. Asha's dashboard takes 4 minutes, and while it runs, checkout queries queue behind it. Analytics is literally slowing down shopping. An index does not help. An index finds few rows fast; this query wants all rows.

Incident 2 — search that is not search. This is question 3, the topics. Omar owns the search box. Users type "blue train mystery" into it, and the first implementation is WHERE title LIKE '%blue%train%'. That has two problems, and only one of them is speed. It scans every row, because a LIKE pattern with a leading % cannot use a normal index. And it is wrong. It will not find "The Blue Locomotive". It will not rank the best match first. It will not tolerate the typo "mistery". Relational databases match values. Users want relevance over language. Different job entirely.

Incident 3 — the counter that melted. This one is a new shape, tiny hot writes, and it is Dev's. He ships "👀 231 people viewed this book today". Every page view is now an UPDATE to a counter row. Popular books take hundreds of updates a second, all fighting to lock the same row, each waiting for the previous transaction to finish. The database is doing everything correctly. It is applying full transactional ceremony to a number nobody needs to be exact. That is correctness Chaptr does not need, at a price it cannot pay.

Incident 4 — the recommendation query from hell. Question 4, the threads, and it is about Arjun. "Readers who bought this also bought…" is, in SQL, a join from orders to orders to orders. Three hops means joining the 40-million-row orders table to itself three times. Each hop multiplies the work. Priya measures six-second response times. The data is a web of connections, and tables answer web-shaped questions by rebuilding the web from scratch on every single query.

Read the four incidents side by side. The last column is the one that matters:

Incident Whose Meera's question Why the row layout loses
1. Dashboard Asha totals reads whole rows to sum one column
2. Search box Omar topics no path from a word to the books holding it
3. View counter Dev a new one, hot writes full transaction ceremony on one contended row
4. Recommendations Priya, about Arjun threads rebuilds the web of links on every query

One diagnosis covers all four: the query shapes changed, and the storage layout did not. PostgreSQL was never wrong. It is shaped for question 2, which is find or update one order by id, and it still does that beautifully. It was handed questions 1, 3 and 4.

🔮 Predict: the team's first instinct is "PostgreSQL can't handle our scale — let's migrate everything to something webscale." Is Chaptr's problem scale?

Reveal No — and this misdiagnosis is the most expensive mistake in the field. The order workload, which has question 2's shape, is fine; Postgres handles it easily. The problem is that *other-shaped* workloads landed on a store shaped for question 2's find-one-record-fast shape. Migrating *everything* to, say, Cassandra would fix incident 3, leave 1 and 4 broken, and *break checkout*, which needs transactions Cassandra doesn't offer. The fix is not one better database. It is one database *per dominant query shape*.

5. What does Chaptr actually need?

In one breath: Two promises. Arrange the data the way each question asks for it, and still keep exactly one copy that is definitely true.

Say it in plain words first, then read it twice:

For each workload, data must be physically arranged the way that workload asks for it — while keeping exactly one authoritative copy of the truth, so the many arrangements can disagree only briefly and can always be rebuilt.

Both halves are load-bearing, and they pull in opposite directions.

The first half says you will run more than one kind of store. Meera's four books, in software.

The second half says they cannot all be equally trusted. One of them owns the truth. The rest are copies, arranged for speed, and a copy that disagrees with the truth is simply wrong.

The whole discipline of choosing databases is the pursuit of those two guarantees at once.

6. What is the one idea underneath all of it?

In one breath: A database is not a general tool. It is a bet on one question, paid for by making the other questions cost more.

Here it is, and everything after this section is a footnote to it:

Every database is an opinionated bet on one access pattern. It arranges bytes on disk so that its favorite question is nearly free — and pays for that by making other questions expensive. Choosing a database is therefore not "which is best?" but "whose bet matches the question I ask most?"

There is a corollary, and it comes straight from the second half of section 5. Use several stores. Appoint exactly one of them the source of truth. Treat all the rest as disposable copies you could delete and rebuild tonight.

The industry name for running several stores this way is polyglot persistence, and the next section defines it properly.

7. What do professionals call what you just watched?

In one breath: You have now felt every problem these words name. Here are the words, each one attached to the moment in Chaptr's year where you met it.

Asha's dashboard and Priya's checkout are the two poles of this whole subject, and the trade has a name for each.

The checkout is OLTP, online transaction processing. In plain terms it is the shop-counter workload: many small, fast reads and writes of individual records, right now, correctly. Precisely, it is a high volume of short transactions, each touching few rows, needing low latency and strong correctness. Chaptr's checkout is the example. Read one cart, insert one order, decrement one stock count, 30 ms, and it must never double-charge. The term exists to separate this workload from analytics, because the two want opposite storage layouts. The transactions and locking you watched melt down in incident 3 are OLTP machinery, working exactly as designed.

Asha's dashboard is OLAP, online analytical processing. Plainly, it is the back-office workload: big questions over history. Precisely, it is a low volume of long queries, scanning millions of rows, aggregating a few columns, with latency in seconds and slightly stale data being fine. Revenue per category per month over two years is the example. Incident 1 is the OLTP and OLAP collision, because one store cannot be laid out for both. Section 10 shows you the row-versus-column layouts that make it physical.

The word for what distinguishes them is access pattern, also called query shape. Plainly, it is the kind of question you ask, and how often. Precisely, it is the distribution of operations across point lookup, range scan, full scan, traversal and aggregation, plus the read/write ratio, the latency budget and the consistency need. Meera's four questions are four access patterns. The term matters because it is the input to every database decision you will ever make. Compare "get order by id, 5000×/sec, under 10 ms, must be current" with "sum revenue, 10×/day, under 30 s, yesterday's data fine". Those two sentences choose two different products.

An index is the card box at the back of the textbook: a shortcut from a value to where it lives. Precisely, it is an auxiliary structure, usually a B-tree, mapping a key to a row location, making point lookups cost O(log n) instead of a full scan. Every index also slows writes slightly, because each write must update it too. It is the first tool everyone reaches for. Incidents 1 and 2 showed you its limits: indexes accelerate selective lookups, not scan-everything queries and not match-the-language queries.

Now the distinction the whole architecture hangs on. A source of truth is the master ledger. Derived data is every copy made from it. Precisely, the source of truth is the one store whose loss is unrecoverable and whose value is definitionally correct; every other store holds transformations of it that may lag and can be rebuilt from it. Chaptr's Postgres orders table is truth. The search index Omar builds and the analytics warehouse Asha queries are derived. The distinction exists because the moment you run two stores they will disagree, and this rule decides who wins. Your CQRS lab already taught you the same shape: read models are exactly derived data, and its "consistency window" is the lag between truth and its copies.

Denormalization is writing the same fact in several places so each reader finds it already arranged for them. Precisely, it is deliberately duplicating data to eliminate query-time joins and aggregation, trading write complexity and storage for read speed. Storing the author's name, not just the id, inside every order record is the small version. NoSQL stores do not do joins, so denormalization is how they answer join-shaped questions at all.

And polyglot persistence is the name of the destination this document walks toward: several kinds of store in one system, each doing what it is shaped for. Precisely, it is an architecture with one source-of-truth store per data domain, plus derived stores per access pattern, connected by a synchronization mechanism that section 15 builds.

INTERMEDIATE MODEL

10. What are the seven tricks, and who bets on which?

In one breath: Databases look like fifty products. Under the floorboards there are about seven ways to arrange bytes so that one kind of question becomes cheap. Learn the seven, and every product is just "which tricks, bundled how".

Priya's mistake, before this section, was shopping by product name. After it she shops by trick. Learn the tricks first; the catalogue that follows is then almost boring, which is the point.

10.1 What are the seven physical tricks?

Trick 1, the hash table. Take the key, scramble it into a number, and jump straight to that slot. In code that is hash("user:42") % number_of_slots. No walking, no comparing, no ordering. One jump to read, one jump to write, and engineers write that cost as O(1), meaning it does not grow as the data grows.

The price of trick 1 is that there is no ordering. Ask for "all users between 40 and 50", or for "all keys starting with user:", and the store has nothing better than checking everything. Redis, Memcached, and DynamoDB's partition routing all run on this trick.

Trick 2, the B-tree. Keep the rows sorted by key, in fixed-size pages of typically 8 KB. Arrange those pages as a tree so wide and so shallow that a billion rows sit three or four levels deep. A point lookup walks three or four pages and stops. A range scan, such as "orders from March", finds the start and then reads neighbours in order, which the hash table simply could not do. Writes update pages in place.

The price of trick 2 shows up under write pressure. A write to a random key touches a random page. At high write rates that becomes scattered disk I/O, and page locks start contending, which is exactly what melted Dev's counter in incident 3. PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQL Server and Oracle are B-tree stores, and so is almost every secondary index anywhere.

Trick 3, the LSM-tree, short for Log-Structured Merge tree. Never update in place. Always append. A write lands in an in-memory sorted buffer. When that buffer fills, it is flushed to disk as one immutable sorted file. A background process called compaction then merges those files, discarding values that were overwritten. Every byte written to disk is written sequentially, which is the fastest thing storage hardware does, so write throughput is enormous.

The price of trick 3 lands on reads. One read may have to consult the memory buffer plus several files on disk, so reads are slower and much less predictable than a B-tree's. Compaction also burns background I/O forever. The B-tree and LSM fork is the single most important internals fact in this document: B-tree is read-optimized, LSM is write-optimized. Cassandra, HBase, RocksDB, LevelDB and ScyllaDB run on it, as do the storage engines inside many newer systems.

Trick 4, the columnar layout. A row store keeps [order1: id,user,price,date][order2: id,user,price,date]… on disk. A column store keeps [all ids][all users][all prices][all dates] instead. Now SUM(price) reads only the price bytes. On a 40-column table that is roughly one fortieth of the data, and it is the direct cure for Asha's incident 1. There is a bonus. A column holds similar values, so it compresses brutally well. A million dates from the same year, run-length-encoded, almost vanish. Compression of 10–20× is ordinary, and it shrinks the scan again.

The price of trick 4 is the exact opposite bet from a B-tree. Fetching or updating one whole row now touches every column file. Columnar stores are therefore batch-loaded rather than transactionally updated. BigQuery, Snowflake, Redshift, ClickHouse, DuckDB and the Parquet file format all take this bet.

Trick 5, the inverted index. This is the index at the back of a textbook. Split every document into terms, so "The Blue Train" becomes blue and train, and for each term keep the list of documents containing it. The query "blue train" is then an intersection of two lists, which takes milliseconds over millions of documents. Then each hit is scored for relevance: rarer words weigh more, and how often a term appears matters. Analyzers add stemming, turning running into run, and fuzzy matching, turning "mistery" into "mystery". This is the cure for Omar's incident 2.

The price of trick 5 is freshness. The index is expensive to update in place, so search engines rebuild index segments in the background. Documents become searchable seconds after being written rather than instantly, which the trade calls near-real-time rather than real-time. It is also why search engines make poor primary stores, a point section 11 turns into a war story. Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Solr and Lucene, the library inside all three, are the names here.

Trick 6, adjacency. Store each record with direct references to its neighbours, a layout the trade calls index-free adjacency. Now "friends of friends of Arjun" is pointer-following, and the cost is proportional to the neighbourhood you touch rather than to the size of the table. In SQL every hop is a join that searches an index over ALL edges. In a graph store, hop three costs the same as hop one. This is the cure for Priya's incident 4.

The price of trick 6 is distribution. A pointer-chasing layout partitions poorly across machines, because a graph has no clean lines to cut along, so graph stores scale awkwardly. Queries that are not traversals, such as "all users named Priya", still need ordinary indexes. Neo4j, Amazon Neptune and Memgraph are the products.

Trick 7, the append-only log. Records are only ever appended. Each reader keeps its own position, called an offset, and replays forward at its own pace. Writes and reads are sequential, which is maximal disk throughput. Many independent readers do not disturb the writer or each other. The past is never destroyed, so any reader can start from zero and rebuild. You already know this trick: it is your event-sourcing lab's event store, industrialized.

The price of trick 7 is that a log answers "what happened, in order" and nothing else. There are no lookups and no queries. You derive queryable stores from it. Kafka, Pulsar and Kinesis are the products, and the write-ahead log hiding inside every database above is the same idea.

Half-trick 8, the vector index. An embedding is an array of floats that represents meaning, and similar meanings land near each other in that space. A vector index, chiefly HNSW, is a navigable graph of neighbours that finds approximate nearest neighbours without comparing against every vector. It trades exactness for speed: results are about 99% right, deliberately. pgvector, Pinecone, Milvus and Weaviate run on it, and it gets a proper entry in the catalogue below.

🔮 Predict: Cassandra (LSM, trick 3) advertises stunning write throughput. A teammate proposes it for Chaptr's order system "because writes matter most when checkout traffic spikes". What did they miss?

Reveal Two things. First, checkout isn't write-heavy in the relevant sense — thousands of writes/sec is nothing; Postgres does that easily. LSM's advantage matters at *hundreds of thousands* of writes/sec. Second, checkout's actual hard requirement is the one Cassandra sacrificed: multi-row ACID transactions (decrement stock + insert order + record payment, atomically). They'd trade a guarantee they need for throughput they don't. The lesson generalizes: **pick by the requirement that is hardest, not the benchmark that is flashiest.**

10.2 Which store makes which bet?

Thirteen store families follow, in eight clusters. Each cluster opens with the question its members are shaped to answer, because that is how you will actually choose. Every family carries a circled number, ① through ⑭, which the rest of the document uses as shorthand. Two comparison tables at the end of the section put all thirteen side by side.

Who answers "find or change one record, right now, correctly"?

This is Meera's question 2, and it is the largest cluster because it is the most common question in software. Five families answer it, and they differ in what they are willing to give up.

① Relational OLTP — PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQL Server, Oracle. No incident forced this one. It is the store Chaptr rightly started with, and it keeps the checkout, orders and users workload forever. The bet is rows together on disk, so fetching one order gets the whole order, plus B-trees for keys and indexes, plus ACID transactions across many rows and tables, plus schema-on-write, plus joins. Joins mean data lives in one place, which the trade calls normalized, and still answers combined questions. The usual deployment is one writer node with read replicas.

Priya reaches for ① when entities relate to each other and correctness matters: orders, payments, inventory, bookings, and anything where "read two things, decide, write two things" must be atomic. There is a second and less obvious reason. Reach for it when you don't know your query patterns yet. SQL's flexibility, any WHERE and any join and ad-hoc questions, is the best hedge against an unknown future. That is why "default to Postgres until a specific incident forces otherwise" is professional advice rather than laziness. Real users: basically every company on earth for the transactional core, with Stripe on MySQL, and Instagram and Robinhood on Postgres.

Priya walks away from ① in three situations. First, when a single machine's write capacity is genuinely exceeded, at roughly tens of thousands of sustained writes per second. Replicas scale reads only, and sharding relational data yourself is a famous tar pit, so that pushes her to ⑤ or ⑨. Second, when the workload is analytical scans, which is ⑥. Third, when the data is genuinely schema-free or the access is pure key to blob, which is ② or ③. Its consistency posture is CP-leaning single-writer ACID, and its replicas introduce the one eventual-consistency leak most teams meet first, replica lag.

② Key-value stores and caches — Redis, Memcached, DynamoDB. Dev's melted counter is the loud story here. There is a quieter one alongside it: every page load hits the database for the same book details, thousands of times a minute. The bet is trick 1, the hash table. The store knows nothing about your value, which is an opaque blob behind a key. In exchange you get sub-millisecond get and set, and, because keys hash independently, near-perfect horizontal scaling. Two families share that one bet. In-memory caches such as Redis and Memcached give microsecond reads, and data may be evicted or lost. Durable key-value stores such as DynamoDB and Riak are disk-backed and replicated, and still key-only.

Dev reaches for ② when access is by exact key, at high volume. Sessions, so session:abc123 maps to who is logged in. Caching query results in front of store ①, in the read-through shape: check the cache, and on a miss read the database and fill the cache. Counters and rate limiters, where Redis INCR is atomic and lock-free and is incident 3's actual cure. Leaderboards, on Redis sorted sets. Feature flags. DynamoDB specifically earns its place when you need key-value at any scale with SLAs and zero operations work, which is why Amazon's own cart runs on it.

Dev walks away from ② the moment a query wants anything other than the key. "All sessions for user 42" is unanswerable, because the store cannot see inside values. Multi-key transactions are another exit. And the third exit is the dangerous one: when you are tempted to make a cache the only copy of data you cannot lose. A restarted Redis is empty, and section 11 opens with that war story. DynamoDB has its own corollary: you must know your access patterns up front, because changing them later means re-modeling the table, which is precisely why "unknown future queries" was a reason to stay relational. On posture, a single-node Redis is trivially consistent, but only because it is one machine; DynamoDB is AP by default with eventually-consistent reads, and offers opt-in strongly-consistent reads at double cost.

③ Document stores — MongoDB, Couchbase, Firestore. Chaptr's product catalogue is the story. Books, e-books, audiobooks and box sets each have different fields. Does this one have a narrator? A file size? A number of volumes? In Postgres that becomes 60 nullable columns, or a dozen sparse side-tables, and every product page load joins six tables to assemble one product. The bet is to store each record as a self-contained JSON document, the whole product, nested, retrieved by key in one read, with no joins to reassemble it. This is denormalization institutionalized. Unlike ②, the store can see inside, so you may index and query any field. It is schema-on-read: two documents in one collection may differ in shape, and the writer decides, per document.

Omar reaches for ③ when records are naturally self-contained things that are read whole. Product catalogues are the canonical use, because each seller's item has different attributes. User profiles, CMS content, and IoT payloads that vary by device are the others. The trigger is that each record's shape varies enough that a fixed schema fights you. Real users: eBay's catalogue and Forbes' CMS, both on MongoDB.

Omar walks away from ③ in three cases. First, when the entities are many-to-many relational, such as orders referencing users referencing addresses. Documents then force him into one of two bad choices. Either he embeds copies, which must be updated everywhere, so "user changed address" now touches every order document. Or he simulates joins in application code, badly. Second, when cross-document ACID becomes routine. MongoDB does have multi-document transactions now, but if you use them constantly, your data is telling you it is relational. Third, when documents grow without bound. An orders: [...] array inside the user document eventually hits the 16 MB document cap, and it rewrites the whole document on every append. That is failure F5 in section 11. One honesty note belongs here: Postgres's JSONB gives you indexed documents inside a relational store, and for mixed workloads one store ① with JSONB columns often beats running ① and ③ together. Posture: single-document atomicity always, primary-replica with eventual reads if you read from secondaries, tunable per query.

④ Wide-column stores — Cassandra, ScyllaDB, HBase, Bigtable. Chaptr launches a reading app that reports each reader's page position every 10 seconds. A million readers means about 100 000 writes per second, forever, spread worldwide, and the only query is "positions for reader X, most recent first". Postgres's B-trees choke on that random-write firehose. The bet is trick 3, the LSM tree, for the firehose, plus aggressive partitioning. Rows are grouped by a partition key, so reader X's data lives together on one set of nodes, sorted by a clustering key such as timestamp. Partitions are spread across dozens of nodes in a hash ring, and every node accepts writes, so there is no single writer to bottleneck. Replication is masterless: writes go to N replicas, with read and write quorum knobs per query.

There is a catch that shocks relational people. You design tables from your queries, not your entities. One query shape per table, with data duplicated across tables at write time. There is no join and no ad-hoc query. The schema is the query plan.

Ben reaches for ④ when the workload is write-heavy, key-partitioned, with queries known in advance, at serious scale, with multi-datacenter and no-single-point-of-failure requirements. Messaging history is the flagship case, and Discord stores trillions of messages on ScyllaDB. Activity feeds and device telemetry follow. Netflix's viewing history runs on Cassandra, and Apple runs hundreds of thousands of Cassandra nodes.

Ben walks away from ④ when queries are ad-hoc or still evolving, because every new question means a new table and a backfill. He walks away when data volume is under a few terabytes, or writes are under about 10 000 per second. Postgres does that without the operational bill. Cassandra clusters need real care: compaction tuning, repair schedules, the lot. He walks away when transactions or strong consistency are the default requirement. And he walks away when the partition key would produce hot or unbounded partitions, which is section 11's celebrity problem. Posture: ④ is the textbook AP system straight from your CAP lab, available under partition, with tunable quorums where R plus W greater than N buys per-query strong reads, and last-write-wins conflict resolution by default.

⑤ Distributed SQL, also called NewSQL — Spanner, CockroachDB, YugabyteDB, TiDB, Vitess. Chaptr goes global. Orders must survive a region failure, and write volume finally, genuinely exceeds one Postgres box. But checkout still needs real transactions. Category ① cannot scale writes. Category ④ will not do transactions. For decades the answer was "pick your poison".

The bet is to keep SQL, schema and ACID, and to shard data automatically across nodes while running consensus per shard. That consensus is Raft or Paxos: your paxos lab, productized, where every write is a quorum decision, so any node can fail and the shard keeps a consistent leader. Cross-shard transactions layer two-phase commit on top of per-shard consensus. Your two-phase-commit lab's blocking problem is solved by making each participant itself a fault-tolerant quorum, which is exactly the fix that lab hinted at. Spanner adds globally synchronized clocks, from GPS and atomic clocks, to give lock-free consistent reads across continents.

Meera reaches for ⑤ when she needs relational semantics at a scale or availability level one machine cannot give. Global financial ledgers. Multi-region inventory. Any system where "Postgres, but it survives a region and scales writes" is the actual specification. Google's own AdWords billing runs on Spanner, which exists because sharded MySQL had become unbearable; DoorDash and Netflix keep metadata on CockroachDB.

Meera walks away from ⑤ when one region and one box's write volume suffice, which is most businesses, because the costs are real. Every write crosses the network for quorum, so the latency floor is milliseconds rather than microseconds. Cross-shard joins and transactions cost more than local ones. Operations, or bills, are heavier. This category is a destination you earn through measured limits, never a starting point. Posture: CP by construction, so under partition the minority side refuses writes, which is your CAP lab's "the majority keeps working" made into a product.

Who answers "add up a column across millions of rows"?

This is Meera's question 1, Asha's dashboard, and two families answer it at two very different price points.

⑥ Columnar warehouses, the OLAP shelf — BigQuery, Snowflake, Redshift, ClickHouse, DuckDB. Incident 1 is the story: the 4-minute dashboard that queued up checkouts. The bet is trick 4, columnar layout with compression, plus massively parallel execution that splits a big scan across many workers, plus one more thing the cloud generation added: storage and compute separation. Data lives in object storage. Compute spins up per query. You pay per scan on BigQuery, or per warehouse-second on Snowflake. Data is loaded in batches or streams from other systems, and queried with full SQL, big and read-only.

Asha reaches for ⑥ for analytics, full stop: dashboards, BI, "revenue by category by month over two years", machine-learning feature backfills, and anything scanning millions or billions of rows for aggregates. The pattern is always the same. The OLTP stores, ① through ④, hold the live truth. A pipeline, which section 15 builds, copies changes into the warehouse. Analysts then hammer the warehouse where they cannot hurt production. Two variants are worth naming. ClickHouse is the pick when you need sub-second analytics inside a product, which is how Cloudflare's dashboards work. DuckDB is the pick when the data fits one machine: it is "SQLite for analytics", a library rather than a server, and you will run it in this lab's experiments.

Asha walks away from ⑥ the moment the workload is operational: point lookups such as "order 12345", single-row updates, or thousands of small queries per second. Columnar layout makes each of those touch every column file. Warehouses also often charge per data scanned, so an app hammering BigQuery with point lookups is slow and ruinously expensive. A warehouse is never the source of truth, and never sits behind an end-user request path, with the deliberate ClickHouse-style exception noted above. Posture: consistency is not the interesting axis here, because data is minutes to hours stale by design. The honest contract is "answers about up to time T, fast", and consumers must know T.

⑪ Object storage, data lakes and lakehouses — S3, GCS or ADLS, plus Parquet, plus Iceberg, Delta or Hudi. Chaptr's machine-learning team wants everything, forever, raw: clickstreams, images, logs, six years of order snapshots. The warehouse ⑥ is structured and priced for curated tables, not for petabytes of maybe-useful-someday. The bet here is three layers that took the industry a decade to stack.

The bottom layer is object storage, meaning S3 or GCS: infinitely scalable key-to-blob-file storage at around $0.02 per GB-month, with eleven nines of durability and no compute attached. It is the cheapest durable bytes money buys. The middle layer is the data lake: files in object storage in an open columnar format, namely Parquet. Parquet is trick 4 as a file format, so any engine such as Spark, Trino or DuckDB can query those files in place. But bare files have no transactions. Two writers corrupt each other, and readers see half-written data, which is how the industry got the phrase "the data swamp". The top layer is the lakehouse, meaning Iceberg, Delta Lake or Hudi. It is a table format: a metadata layer over those files that adds ACID commits, schema evolution and time travel. It makes the lake behave like warehouse tables while every engine keeps direct file access and storage stays at $0.02 per GB. Industry consensus is converging here, and warehouses like Snowflake and BigQuery now read Iceberg natively.

Omar reaches for ⑪ when data is raw, varied and huge, and its future uses are unknown. Training corpora go here. So does the cheap long-term layer beneath the warehouse. So does sharing one copy of data across many engines instead of keeping N copies. Also reach for plain object storage for what it plainly is: the correct home for files, meaning images, PDFs and backups. Put the URL in your database, never megabyte blobs in Postgres rows.

Omar walks away from ⑪ when he needs low-latency serving, because lake queries take seconds to minutes and nothing user-facing reads a lake directly. He walks away for small structured data that a warehouse or Postgres handles, because a lakehouse's Spark-sized toolchain is a heavy tax. And he walks away from using it as a system of record for transactional workloads. Posture: object stores are now strongly consistent per object, which S3 has been since 2020, and table formats add snapshot-isolation commits per table.

Who answers "match the words a human typed"?

This is Meera's question 3, Omar's search box, and two families split it between matching words and matching meaning.

⑦ Search engines — Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, Solr, Algolia, Typesense, Meilisearch. Incident 2 is the story: LIKE '%blue%train%' is not search. The bet is trick 5, the inverted index, wrapped in a relevance-scoring pipeline that tokenizes, normalizes, stems, scores and ranks. Add typo tolerance. Add faceting, so the sidebar can say "Fiction (1 203) | Mystery (410)…". Add highlighting. Add aggregations fast enough that Elasticsearch doubles as a log-analytics engine, which is the ELK stack: logs in, Kibana dashboards out, and likely where you have already met it.

Omar reaches for ⑦ when humans type words and expect the best matches ranked: product search in the Amazon style, site search, log search that greps terabytes in milliseconds, and autocomplete. Real users include Wikipedia, the lineage behind GitHub code search, and Uber on Elasticsearch, with Stripe's dashboard search in the Typesense and Algolia class.

Omar walks away from ⑦ when matching is exact and structured. "Orders over ₹500 this week" is SQL's job, and a search engine adds operations for nothing. He also walks away, hard, from the temptation to treat it as a primary store. It is the canonical derived store: near-real-time rather than immediate visibility of writes, historically weak durability guarantees, and mappings that periodically need a full reindex. One rule keeps you safe here. You must always be able to delete the search index and rebuild it from the source of truth. If you cannot, you have mis-assigned truth, and section 11 shows what that costs. Posture: eventually consistent by design, with a refresh interval of about 1 s, which is your CQRS lab's consistency window made into a product.

⑫ Vector stores — pgvector, Pinecone, Milvus, Weaviate, Qdrant. Chaptr adds "books like this one", and semantic search, so that "cozy mystery on a train" finds The Blue Locomotive even though no word matches. A model turns each book description into an embedding, an array of about 1000 floats where similar meaning lands nearby in space. The query is now "nearest neighbours of this point", and neither B-trees nor inverted indexes nor hashes can answer it, because exact nearest-neighbour search in 1000 dimensions means comparing against every vector.

The bet is approximate nearest-neighbour indexing, chiefly HNSW, a multi-layer "skip-list of neighbourhoods" graph. It finds about 99% of the true nearest neighbours in milliseconds by accepting that missing about 1% is fine for similarity, which is half-trick 8. Metadata filtering rides along, so you can ask for "similar AND in stock".

Omar reaches for ⑫ for semantic and similarity retrieval. The flagship case is RAG for LLM apps, where relevant documents are fetched to stuff into a prompt, and it was 2023-25's defining workload. Recommendation retrieval, image and audio similarity, and near-duplicate detection follow. The pragmatic guidance mirrors ⑧'s below. Start with pgvector, an extension that keeps vectors next to your relational data with joins and transactions. Move to a dedicated vector database such as Pinecone or Milvus when vectors are your primary workload at hundreds-of-millions scale. Most production RAG stacks, Notion AI among them, started on pgvector.

Omar walks away from ⑫ for exact keyword and filter queries, where a hybrid of ⑦ and ⑫ usually beats either alone, fusing BM25 keyword scores with vector similarity. He walks away for tiny corpora under 100k vectors, where a brute-force scan is fast, exact and index-free. And he walks away from treating it as truth, because embeddings are derived twice over, from the documents and through a particular model version, so re-embedding on a model upgrade means a full rebuild. The delete-and-rebuild rule of ⑦ applies here doubly. Posture: derived-store semantics, where freshness equals your re-embedding pipeline's lag, and approximate by contract, making it the only category here whose reads are deliberately not exact.

Who answers "follow the connections"?

⑨ Graph databases — Neo4j, Amazon Neptune, Memgraph. This is Meera's question 4 and Priya's incident 4: the three-self-joins recommendation query about Arjun. The bet is trick 6, index-free adjacency, plus a query language shaped like the traversal itself. In Cypher, "readers who bought what Arjun bought also bought this" is written (arjun)-[:BOUGHT]->(b)<-[:BOUGHT]-(other)-[:BOUGHT]->(rec). Compare that line to the SQL it replaces. Hop cost is independent of dataset size.

Priya reaches for ⑨ when the connections are the data and the queries are multi-hop or variable-depth. Fraud rings are the killer app: accounts sharing devices sharing cards, which is how bank anti-fraud units work. Then recommendations, knowledge graphs, dependency and impact analysis, network topology, and permissions inheritance. Real users include LinkedIn's graph, which is custom-built, NASA's lessons-learned knowledge graph on Neo4j, and most large banks' fraud units.

Priya walks away from ⑨ when she has relationships but her queries are only ever one or two fixed hops, because SQL joins handle that fine. "Our data has relationships!" describes every dataset, so the honest test is narrower: do you ask variable-depth or three-plus-hop questions, often? She walks away when queries are aggregations over everyone, because graph databases are poor at OLAP. And she walks away when she needs extreme write scale, because partitioning a graph across machines is an unsolved-in-general problem, which is ④'s bet running the other way. Posture: Neo4j is single-writer ACID, so CP-ish with causal clusters, and graph stores generally choose consistency and pay for it in scale-out.

Who answers "numbers over time"?

⑧ Time-series databases — TimescaleDB, InfluxDB, Prometheus, QuestDB. Chaptr now runs 200 services, and Ben wants CPU, latency and request-rate metrics every 15 seconds, plus "p99 latency, per service, last 30 days". Metrics are a firehose with a peculiar shape. They are always appended and never updated. They are always queried by time window. Recent data is hot, and old data is only ever read as aggregates.

The bet is to exploit that shape ruthlessly. Partition by time, so dropping a whole month means deleting one partition, instantly, instead of a million-row DELETE. Compress brutally, using delta-of-delta encoding on timestamps because "every 15 s" is nearly constant, and Gorilla-style float compression, together worth 10–20×. Downsample old data automatically: keep raw for 7 days, 1-minute averages for 90, hourly forever. And make time-window aggregation the fast path. Prometheus adds the pull model, scraping your services, and is the de-facto standard for infrastructure metrics.

Ben reaches for ⑧ for metrics, IoT sensor data, financial ticks, and anything whose primary key is really (thing, timestamp) and whose queries are windows and rollups. TimescaleDB is the pragmatic pick when this is one workload among many, because it is a Postgres extension, so metrics sit next to relational data with full SQL.

Ben walks away from ⑧ when data is not time-keyed, or gets updated after the fact, or the volume is modest. A plain Postgres table with a timestamp index handles millions of rows fine; the specialized machinery pays off at billions. And he never stores business truth, such as orders, in a store configured to downsample and expire, because retention policies delete data by design. Posture: typically single-writer or per-series-partitioned, and consistency is rarely the pressure point here. Ingestion throughput and retention are.

Who answers "what happened, in order"?

⑩ Streaming logs and message queues — Kafka, Pulsar, Kinesis, and RabbitMQ, SQS. By now Chaptr's one truth, Postgres, feeds five derived stores: search ⑦, warehouse ⑥, cache ②, metrics ⑧ and recommendations ⑨. Wiring five point-to-point pipelines means ten integrations, each lossy in its own way. The fix is the one your outbox lab already taught you: publish every change once, to a place every consumer can read at its own pace.

The bet is trick 7, the append-only log, partitioned for scale, which is Kafka's design. Producers append. Each consumer keeps its own offset. History is retained for days, or forever with compaction, so a new consumer, such as next year's warehouse, replays from zero and catches up. This is a data source, not a database. It holds the flow of facts, and its query language is "read forward from an offset".

One distinction here prevents endless confusion, and it is the difference between a queue and a log. A queue, meaning RabbitMQ or SQS, hands each message to one worker and then it is gone. It is for jobs: send this email, resize this image. The message is a task to consume. A log, meaning Kafka, is durable history that many readers replay independently. It is for facts: order 123 was placed. The message is an event to remember. A job goes to a queue. A fact carrying data between systems goes to a log. Teams using Kafka as a mere job queue, and teams using RabbitMQ as an event backbone, both suffer.

Priya reaches for ⑩ when one truth must feed N systems, where CDC from the primary database into Kafka, which is your outbox lab's relay industrialized, is the standard pattern. Then event-driven microservices. Then stream processing, such as fraud scoring on the purchase stream in flight with Kafka Streams or Flink. Then buffering firehoses in front of slower stores. LinkedIn built Kafka and runs about 7 trillion messages a day; Uber and every bank's integration backbone followed.

Priya walks away from ⑩ when she needs to query. "What is the current state of order 123?" is unanswerable by a log, which can only replay; state lives in stores derived from it. She walks away for request-and-response RPC between two services, because that is an API call, and putting Kafka in the middle adds latency and an operations estate for nothing. And she walks away at tiny scale, where two services and one database want the plain outbox pattern you already know, with no Kafka cluster required. Posture: ordered within a partition, at-least-once by default, so your outbox lab's idempotent-consumer rule applies verbatim, with exactly-once available only inside Kafka's own transactional boundaries.

Who answers "and can we do this with no server at all"?

⑬ Embedded databases — SQLite, RocksDB, DuckDB. This is not a Chaptr incident but a change of setting. Chaptr ships a mobile app that needs a local offline store, and its edge service needs a key-value store inside the process, with no network hop. The bet is that the database is a library in your process rather than a server: no network, no operations, one file or directory of state. The same tricks, embedded. SQLite is a B-tree relational store, so ① in a library. RocksDB is an LSM key-value store, which is the storage engine under many big systems. DuckDB is columnar OLAP, so ⑥ in a library.

Dev reaches for ⑬ for on-device storage, where SQLite is on every phone on earth and is the most deployed database in existence. Also for local-first apps, CLI tools and test fixtures. Also for single-user analytics on files, where DuckDB querying Parquet on a laptop replaces a Spark cluster embarrassingly often. And also as the storage engine inside a system you are building yourself.

Dev walks away from ⑬ when many processes or machines must share mutable state, because embedded means single-machine. SQLite specifically serializes writers, allowing one writer at a time, which is fine for an app and fatal for a busy web backend. He walks away when he needs managed durability or replication, which is what servers buy you. One footnote cousin exists: "NewSQL at the edge", where Turso and libSQL replicate SQLite, which is niche but real. Posture: single-node ACID, and SQLite is fully ACID, trivially consistent because there is nothing distributed to disagree.

What else is on the specialist shelf?

⑭ The specialist shelf. Five more families exist that you should recognize without reaching for.

Ledger databases, meaning Amazon QLDB and immudb, are append-only tables with cryptographic hash-chaining, so history is provably untampered. They serve audit and compliance niches. QLDB itself is being retired, and that carries its own lesson: niche categories carry vendor risk, and Postgres plus append-only discipline plus hash chains often suffices.

Geospatial, meaning PostGIS, answers "points or shapes near X" using R-tree-family spatial indexes. It is overwhelmingly used as the PostGIS extension to Postgres, which is the "extension over new store" pattern you have now seen three times: for time-series ⑧, for vectors ⑫, and here for geo. Internalize that pattern. It is the single best operations-saver in this document.

Multi-model stores, meaning ArangoDB, Cosmos DB and OrientDB, speak several models in one product, mixing document, graph and key-value. They are tempting on paper, because "one store for everything!" sounds like the end of this whole problem. In practice each model is rarely best-in-class, and Cosmos DB's real pitch is Azure-managed global distribution rather than multi-modelness. Evaluate per workload as if each model were a separate product.

Hierarchical and mainframe stores, meaning IBM IMS, plus object databases, are pre-relational history. IMS still clears a shocking share of card transactions. Know they exist. The relational model largely replaced them for good reasons, chief among them ad-hoc queries.

In-memory data grids, meaning Hazelcast and Ignite, are partitioned RAM caches with compute attached, sitting in a niche between ② and ⑤.

11. How does each choice go wrong in production?

In one breath: Happy-path knowledge picks databases. Failure knowledge keeps them running. Seven recurring disasters follow, and the first three are about the gaps between stores rather than any store's own quirks.

Ben carries the pager at Chaptr. Every scenario below is a real, recurring industry incident pattern, and Ben has been woken by most of them. Read the first three most carefully. They are polyglot persistence's own failure modes, and they matter more than any single product's oddities.

F1 — The cache that became the truth. Priya's team caches data in Redis "temporarily". Months pass. Some values now exist only in Redis: shopping carts, draft posts. Then Redis restarts.

What Ben sees is that every cart in the company empties at 14:32. There is no error and no corruption. An in-memory store's contract is "may forget anything", through eviction, restart or failover, and the team silently promoted a derived store to a source of truth without giving it a truth store's durability. The response is that the data is simply gone and you apologize. Redis behaved exactly as documented; the guarantee held. What was lost was Chaptr's data, because section 5's truth-and-derived boundary was crossed without anyone noticing.

In production the rule is mechanical. For every store, ask: if this vanished right now, could we rebuild it from something else? If the answer is no, it is a truth store, and it must have a truth store's durability, or that data must move out. Redis can persist, with AOF, and Chaptr's cart belongs in Postgres or DynamoDB.

F2 — The stale derived store, and the dual-write trap. The app writes Postgres, then writes Elasticsearch. Two separate calls. One day the Elasticsearch call fails after the database has already committed, because of a deploy, a timeout, or a crash between the two. That is your outbox lab's dual-write problem, verbatim.

What Ben sees is nothing, and that is the danger. A product exists, is purchasable, and search cannot find it. Nobody notices for weeks, because nothing errors. The cause is simple and unfixable by effort: two independent writes cannot be atomic. The response has two halves. Run a periodic full reconciliation that compares truth against the index and repairs the differences. And fix the pipe, so changes are published via CDC or outbox into Kafka and then into the indexer, which section 15 draws. A search index fed by a replayable stream is not a best-effort second write. Postgres's truth survived intact here; what was lost was the completeness of the derived view, silently. In production, teams monitor lag and drift, meaning the count of mismatches, between truth and each derived store, as first-class alerts.

F3 — Read-your-own-writes, across two stores. Arjun edits his profile, which writes Postgres. The page reloads from the search-backed listing, which is Elasticsearch, 1–2 s behind. His edit appears not to have taken. So he edits again. Now Ben has either a confused customer or a duplicate record.

The cause is that every derived store is behind truth by its consistency window, which is the number your CQRS lab taught you to name. The response is to route the author's own next read to the truth store, or to apply the write optimistically in the UI, which is standard CQRS medicine. Eventual convergence survives; the system is not broken. What was lost is the illusion of one database, and that illusion is precisely what polyglot systems must engineer deliberately, per flow, in the places where it matters.

F4 — The hot partition, which Cassandra and DynamoDB people call the celebrity problem. Chaptr partitions its reading-position table by book_id. A celebrity posts about one book. That book's partition now takes 100× the traffic of any node's fair share.

What Ben sees is maddening: the cluster is 95% idle, and timeouts are spiking. One node is melting while its peers watch. The cause is that partitioning distributes keys, not load, and a skewed key defeats it. This is incident 3's hot-row problem, reborn at cluster scale. The response is to redesign the key, perhaps to book_id + hour, or to a shard suffix such as book_id#1..8 that is fanned out at read time. Note what kind of fix that is. It is a data-model change requiring a rewrite and a backfill, not a configuration toggle, and that is exactly why category ④ insisted queries be known up front. The cluster's other partitions were fine throughout; what was lost was the latency target on the hot key.

F4 has a production sibling, the unbounded partition. Use user_id as the key for an append-forever stream and one partition grows to gigabytes, at which point repairs and compactions choke. The fix is again key design, usually time-bucketing. Cassandra also punishes delete-heavy workloads, because deletes write tombstones, which are markers that must be scanned until compaction purges them. That is why queues-on-Cassandra is a notorious anti-pattern.

F5 — The unbounded document. A MongoDB user document embeds an orders: [...] array. A loyal customer reaches thousands of orders.

What Ben sees has a cruel shape: the busiest customers get the slowest pages, because every read and write now moves megabytes. At 16 MB, writes start failing outright. The cause is that embedding bets a document stays small and is read whole, and unbounded growth breaks the bet. The response is to model the relationship as separate documents, with an orders collection referencing the user, which is relational modeling inside a document store. The general law is worth memorizing. Document stores fit data that arrives and is read as a bounded unit; anything that grows without limit is a stream or a table, not a field.

F6 — The warehouse on the request path. Someone says "BigQuery is fast, let's serve the seller dashboard's live widget from it", and ships a per-page-load query.

What Ben sees is a p95 in seconds, because warehouses optimize throughput rather than latency, and some of them queue queries. He also sees a five-figure monthly bill, because scan-priced engines charge per query even when each result is one number. The cause is being on the wrong side of the OLTP and OLAP split, which makes this the mirror image of incident 1. The response is to precompute aggregates into a serving store, such as Postgres, Redis or ClickHouse, on a schedule or a stream, so the request path reads a precomputed answer. The rule: end-user requests read stores shaped for point reads, and warehouses feed those stores, never browsers.

F7 — Replica lag inside one database. This is the classic. Postgres plus read replicas; a replica falls minutes behind under load; reads routed to it serve stale data, and a customer "loses" the order they just placed.

The cause is worth stating starkly, because it surprises people. Even category ① becomes eventually consistent the moment reads leave the writer. CAP-lab thinking applies within one product. The response is to route read-after-write traffic to the primary, monitor replication lag, and treat replicas as what they are: the first derived store most teams ever run.

12. What is actually guaranteed, and what was traded away?

In one breath: Three laws survive any technology churn, and then every category has a receipt: what it bought, what it explicitly did not buy, and what it spent to get there.

Pull the whole lesson into invariants. Three system-level laws first. These outlive every product name in this document.

Law 1 — one source of truth per fact. Every fact has exactly one store whose value is definitionally correct. Every other copy is derived, may lag, and must be rebuildable from the truth alone. That is the delete-and-rebuild test you met in ⑦ and ⑫. A fact with two truth stores will eventually have two values and no arbiter, which is F1's cart and F2's drift.

Law 2 — every derived store has a consistency window, and someone must know its size. The lag from truth to a derived store is a contract term, not an implementation detail. Search may be 2 s behind. The warehouse may be 15 min behind. The machine-learning embeddings may be a day behind. Failures F2, F3 and F7 are all one sentence: nobody knew the window, or nobody monitored it.

Law 3 — cross-store operations are at-least-once, so consumers must be idempotent. The sync fabric, whether outbox, CDC or Kafka, redelivers on retry. Your outbox lab's rule now governs the whole architecture rather than one pipeline.

Solutions

Attempt each exercise first — especially rungs 3–5, where the machine itself is the answer key. Rungs 7–8 are worked design discussions, not single correct outputs; treat them as one strong answer to argue with.

Rung 1

1.1 (One good version.) Different questions want data physically arranged in different ways: finding one order wants related things stored together; adding up two years of revenue wants just the numbers stored together; search wants a word-to-items index; recommendations want connections stored as connections. One arrangement can't be all of these at once, so each heavy question-shape eventually gets its own store, all copied from one authoritative source. The skill isn't knowing fifty products — it's recognizing the shape of the question.

1.2 The source of truth is the one store whose value defines correct — if it burns down, the data is simply gone, so it gets the durability, backups, and transactions. Every other store (cache, search index, warehouse) is a copy arranged for speed: allowed to lag, expected to be deletable and rebuildable from truth. The discipline matters because two stores will disagree — the truth/derived contract is what decides who wins, and every §11 disaster (F1, F2) is that contract being crossed silently.

1.3 "Faster" at what? Meera's subject-index cards are enormously faster than her ledger at "which books mention trains" — and useless for "how much did we earn in March". Every store is faster than every other store at the question it's arranged for and slower at the rest. The true sentence: each database is faster for the access pattern it bet on. exp3 shows one engine "winning" by 566× and losing by 57.8× on the same data, depending only on the query.

Rung 2

2.1 Only the price strip's cells light up (the query names one column); the other seven strips never get touched. Pale green = cells already read — the running total of I/O spent so far.

2.2 5 places: the memtable plus (after compaction) 1 on-disk run — the number is shown as "places a read must check", and its parts are the dashed memtable box + each run box. (Mid-run, before compaction, it can be as high as 1 + 3.)

2.3 (a) append-only log (trick 7); (b) inverted index plus analyzer machinery (trick 5); (c) columnar layout + compression (trick 4); (d) hash table with atomic increment (trick 1); (e) index-free adjacency (trick 6); (f) LSM-tree + partitioning (trick 3).

Rung 3

3.1 Measured here: 354× for the lookup (anything from hundreds to thousands is a right prediction — the point is orders of magnitude, from full scan to ~3 B-tree page reads); SUM(price) was 1.25×, and wanders between 0.82× and 1.25× from run to run — i.e. unchanged, since it must visit every row regardless of any index.

3.2 Column store wins the aggregate (566× here); row store wins the single-row fetch (57.8× here). Right order of magnitude ≈ right answer.

3.3 They collapse to 8 — exactly one seek per page, because ordered keys fill a page completely before moving on. That is why auto-increment (or time-ordered UUIDv7) primary keys are standard advice: they turn every insert into the B-tree's cheap case, while random UUIDv4 keys turn every insert into a random page touch.

3.4 A time-window point-ish query at high frequency on fresh data — that's an OLTP-side read (Postgres with an index on created_at, or Redis if it must be microseconds), not the warehouse: F6 applies (latency in seconds, scan pricing, staleness of minutes-to-hours — "last 15 minutes" would be missing its most important rows).

Rung 4

4.1 The speedup shrinks — the columnar advantage is proportional to (columns in table) / (columns touched), so 4-of-8 roughly halves the gap vs 2-of-8, and SELECT * erases it entirely (plus compression benefits). Warehouse lesson: narrow queries are the whole point; SELECT * on a scan-priced engine is paying for the row-store experience at columnar prices.

4.2 Lookup: marginally better at best (it was already a B-tree walk; now the price rides along, saving the table visit). SUM(price) over all rows: in H2 it may now use the smaller index structure to scan, which helps some — but it remains a visit-everything O(n) scan, not a jump. Both observations are trick 2: B-trees change where you read, never how much a full-scan must read.

4.3 (a) More, smaller sequential blocks and far more compaction passes — total write work (write amplification) rises; (b) oscillates but averages lower per read. This is real Cassandra/RocksDB compaction tuning: the knobs trade write amplification against read amplification against space — the on-call symptom being "compaction backlog grows and p99 reads spike."

Rung 5

5.1 Core of a working answer (replacing the plain intersection):

static List<Map.Entry<Integer, Integer>> rankedQuery(
        Map<String, Set<Integer>> inverted, int k, String... terms) {
    Map<Integer, Integer> scores = new HashMap<>();
    for (String t : terms)
        for (int docId : inverted.getOrDefault(t, Set.of()))  // each matched term = 1 point
            scores.merge(docId, 1, Integer::sum);
    return scores.entrySet().stream()                         // 2-term hits outrank 1-term hits
            .sorted(Map.Entry.<Integer, Integer>comparingByValue().reversed())
            .limit(k).toList();
}

Verify: rankedQuery(inverted, 5, "blue", "locomotive").get(0) is doc 123456 with score 2, ahead of every score-1 title. (Real BM25 also weights rare terms higher and normalizes by document length — same skeleton, better arithmetic.)

5.2 Sketch: build a Map<String, Set<String>> grams mapping each 3-gram to the terms containing it (from sortedTerms); on a miss, split the query term into 3-grams, count overlaps per candidate term, take the best-overlap term(s) and re-run the normal term query. "mistery" and "mystery" share ste/ter/ery (3 of 5 grams) — comfortably the top candidate. That overlap-counting is the essence of Elasticsearch's ngram/fuzzy machinery.

Rung 6

6.1 F2 — dual-write. Retries don't help the case where the process dies between the two writes (or the second keeps failing past retry budget): the writes aren't atomic and can't be made so from the handler. Fix: write Postgres only; Elasticsearch is fed from the change stream (outbox/CDC → Kafka → indexer), plus periodic drift reconciliation.

6.2 F1 — a cache quietly promoted to truth. TTL is irrelevant; the failure is eviction under memory pressure, restart, or failover — carts vanish mid-purchase, revenue-impacting. Fix: carts are truth → durable store (Postgres/DynamoDB); Redis may cache them.

6.3 F6 — warehouse on the request path. 40/min is enough: seconds of latency per page load, and on per-scan pricing each dashboard view costs real money. Fix: scheduled/streamed aggregation into a serving table (Postgres or ClickHouse); the page reads the precomputed answer.

6.4 F5 — unbounded document. Frequent riders make the biggest, slowest documents; every append rewrites the array; the 16 MB cap eventually rejects writes for the best customers first. Fix: trips as their own collection (or a relational table) keyed by rider + time.

6.5 F4 — hot/unbounded partition. Nine partition-key values means at most nine partitions doing all the work — 2M devices funneled through 9 keys, each partition unbounded and enormous. Fix: partition by device_id (2M well-spread keys), bucketed by time if per-device history is long.

Rung 7

7.1 Working answer in three parts. Rebuildability: truth lives in Postgres; the indexer is a Kafka consumer of the CDC stream, so a full rebuild is "create new index, replay the product topic from offset 0, flip the alias" — aliases make the flip atomic, and the rebuild is a drill you've run, not a hope. During: degrade explicitly — serve category browse + Postgres full-text (or a "search is limited" banner) rather than 500s; feature-flag the search box's fancy features. Earlier detection: drift job comparing truth counts vs index counts per category; indexing-lag alert; slow rise in mapping errors. The deep answer: because the index was never truth, this is an inconvenience, not a disaster — the design decision was made months before the outage.

7.2 In the cache: stagger TTLs with jitter (no synchronized expiry); consider soft-TTL/serve-stale-while-revalidate. In the app: request coalescing / singleflight — one loader per missing key per instance, others wait; circuit-break to a degraded page rather than hammering. In the database: connection-pool caps and statement timeouts so 100% cache-miss traffic queues and sheds instead of toppling the primary; read replicas absorb what they can. Result: p95 degrades for minutes; nothing falls over. (This trio is asked, in some form, in most senior system-design interviews.)

8.2 Counter-proposal shape: the clickstream — append-only, loss-tolerant, query-known — is the only workload outgrowing Postgres, and it isn't even a database workload: it's a stream. Move it: events → Kafka (buffering, fan-out), landing in a lakehouse (cheap history) and ClickHouse (product analytics). Orders/users/payments — transactional, modest volume — stay in Postgres, which instantly loses 90%+ of its write load. "Migrate everything to Cassandra" fails the requirement audit twice: checkout loses its transactions, analytics loses ad-hoc queries — Cassandra fits neither retained workload. Sequence: (1) double-write clickstream to Kafka behind a flag (acceptable here only because click events are loss-tolerant; for money you'd outbox); (2) backfill history into the lake; (3) cut ingestion over; (4) delete the clickstream tables and watch Postgres p99 fall. Numbers that justify each step: writes/s split by table (proves what's actually hot), p99 latency and replication lag before/after, cost per month of the new pipeline vs the next Postgres instance size, consumer lag once live. Success criterion stated up front: checkout p99 back under X ms with clickstream growth decoupled from the OLTP box.
