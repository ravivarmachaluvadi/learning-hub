Lab 66 · Whole Systems

Google Spanner — the database that bought a clock

Tell a friend: Priya sells her house at the Northgate land registry and walks out with a receipt. She telephones her solicitor Dev, who is standing in the Southgate office, and he files the mortgage a moment later. The two offices stamp their documents from two wall clocks that disagree, so the register ends up claiming the mortgage came first. That is a loan against a house nobody had sold yet. Google's answer was not a more accurate clock. It was a clock that reports a range and admits how wrong it might be, plus a clerk who sits on the receipt until that range has crawled past the number she stamped. The deliberate pause is the whole invention, and its length is the price of the guarantee.

The trainer for this lab is visualization.html, also published as The Honest Clock. Nothing to install, no code to run. What the lab is made of, where its numbers come from and where it sits among your other labs are in section 17 at the end.

1. Why does a database in five countries have to buy a clock?

In one breath: You want one database spread over several countries, and you want your code to pretend it is one machine doing one thing at a time. Those two wishes fight. The referee everyone reaches for is the clock, and the clock cannot be trusted.

You want one database that runs on machines in several countries. Then a burning datacenter does not lose your data. And a user in Singapore does not sit waiting on a disk in Iowa.

You also want your application code to pretend it is talking to a single machine that does one thing at a time.

Those two wants fight. The moment the data lives in more than one place, "one thing at a time" stops being a fact about the hardware. It becomes something you have to manufacture.

And the usual way people manufacture it is very simple. Put a time on every change, then sort by time. That quietly stops working, because the machines' clocks disagree.

Here is what the disagreement costs, concretely. A payment is taken in London. The service acknowledges it. HTTP 200 comes back, a receipt is emailed, the customer's phone buzzes. A moment later a fraud check runs against a replica in Frankfurt and reads the account as it was before the payment.

Read the three moments in order. The third one is the bug.

Moment Where What happens What the record says
Time zero London the payment is taken and acknowledged the payment exists
3 ms later Frankfurt a fraud check reads the same account the payment has not happened
Afterwards everywhere nothing corrects itself both, forever

Why did Frankfurt miss it? Not because the replica was slow. It had the data. It missed the payment because the payment was labelled with a moment that the fraud check's own clock had not reached yet.

The payment is real. The acknowledgement is real. The system's own record says it has not happened.

That is the failure this lab is about. Not lost data, not late data, but data that is present, acknowledged, and invisible. It produces the worst kind of bug, the one where every log line is correct and the answer is still wrong.

To watch it happen slowly enough to fix, we move it somewhere you can see all of it at once. A land registry with two offices, Northgate and Southgate, and a clerk at each desk.

2. What goes wrong at the Northgate and Southgate registry?

In one breath: Two offices of the same land registry stamp their documents from two wall clocks that disagree. A sale filed first gets a later stamp than the mortgage that followed it. Nobody lies, nobody crashes, and the register is permanently wrong.

There is a Land Registry with two offices. One is in Northgate, one in Southgate. A registry does one job. It records who owns what, and when each change was recorded.

Every document that comes in gets a rubber stamp with the date and time. The time is taken from the clock on the office wall.

Meera is the clerk at Northgate. Arjun is the clerk at Southgate. They never speak to each other. They have no reason to.

The register is read by sorting the stamps. Asha is a searcher. When she asks "who owned 14 Mill Lane at three o'clock on Tuesday?", the registry gathers every stamped document for that address. It throws away the ones stamped after three o'clock. It reads off the survivor.

That is the whole system, and it works beautifully in one office with one clock.

Now Priya files a deed in Northgate. She is selling 14 Mill Lane to Omar. Meera stamps it, files it, and hands Priya a receipt.

The receipt is the important part. It means the registry has publicly committed.

Priya walks out and telephones her solicitor. Dev is standing in the Southgate office. She tells him: "Done. It's filed."

Only now does Dev file the follow-up document. It is a mortgage against the property, in Omar's name. Arjun stamps it and files it.

Two documents. The second one was not even started until the first was finished and announced. Any human would say the order is obvious.

But the Northgate wall clock is four minutes fast, and the Southgate wall clock is three minutes slow. So the deed is stamped 10:04 and the mortgage is stamped 9:57.

Here are the two documents side by side. The column that matters is the last one.

Document Filed by Real moment Stamp it got Why the stamp lies
The deed, selling to Omar Priya, with Meera at Northgate 10:00 10:04 the Northgate clock runs four minutes fast
The mortgage in Omar's name Dev, with Arjun at Southgate just after 10:00 9:57 the Southgate clock runs three minutes slow

Now Asha asks the perfectly ordinary question: "What did the record show at 10:00?" The registry throws away everything stamped after 10:00. That throws away the sale and keeps the mortgage. It answers: there is a mortgage in Omar's name against a property Priya still owns.

Nobody lied. Nobody crashed. No message was lost. Both clerks followed the rules exactly. The register is simply, permanently wrong, and the reason is that the two clocks were never the same clock.

Ben is the registry's duty supervisor. He is the one who gets telephoned when this happens, and he will be with us all the way to section 13.

🔮 Predict: The obvious response is "so synchronise the clocks properly." Suppose you install the best network time synchronisation money can buy and get both offices to within a few milliseconds of true time. Does the problem go away, shrink, or change shape?

Reveal

It shrinks and changes shape, which is worse than either on its own. It shrinks, because the window in which Meera and Arjun can disagree drops from minutes to milliseconds. It changes shape, because the bug now appears only when two related events happen within a few milliseconds of each other.

In a system doing ten thousand operations a second, that is constantly. In your test environment it is never. You have converted a bug that reproduces into a bug that does not.

If you guessed "it goes away", you are assuming that a small error is the same as no error. Your hybrid-logical-clocks lab already caught this exact trap from the other direction. At clock skew of 2 ms or less, the wall clock produced zero visible anomalies. At skew of 8 ms it produced 65 inversions. Same code, same rules, different weather.

3. What are the three obvious fixes?

In one breath: Hand the registry story to a competent engineer and you get one of three designs back. Each one is a real, shipped system. Two of them you have already built yourself.

Fix A: what if one clerk stamps everything?

Put a single stamping desk somewhere. Make every office, in every country, telephone that desk to get a number. Meera and Arjun stop using their wall clocks entirely. One clock, one sequence, no disagreement possible.

This is real. Engineers call the single stamping desk a timestamp oracle, and it is the design you built in the distributed-transactions lab under the name Percolator. TiDB ships it in production, with the oracle itself replicated by Raft and handing out timestamps in batches.

Fix B: what if the clocks may disagree, but the order may not?

Never trust a clock to order two events. Instead attach a counter to every document. The counter is forced upward every time a message arrives, so an effect always carries a bigger number than its cause.

Engineers call that a hybrid logical clock, and you built one. Each stamp is a pair. The first half is a real wall-clock reading. The second half is a counter of the events the wall reading cannot separate. Written down, the pair is (l, c): l is the wall reading, c is the counter.

CockroachDB, YugabyteDB and MongoDB all ship a version of it.

Fix C: what if you lock the searchers out?

Do not timestamp anything at all. Make Asha take a lock before she may read, so she simply cannot look at the register while a filing is half-done.

Engineers call this distributed strict two-phase locking, and you put numbers on it in the distributed-transactions lab too.

4. Where does each of the three fixes break?

In one breath: The locking one breaks first and hardest. The single desk breaks on the speed of light. The clever counter breaks on a telephone call, which is the one path through the world that no database can see.

Why does Fix C break first, and worst?

You already put numbers on this one. Strict two-phase locking across shards is correct. It gives serializable, correctly ordered results. It also makes every Asha queue behind every Priya, and it inherits two-phase commit's blocking problem.

Your own lab's crash storm is the argument, in one row.

Design under a crash storm Readers stranded forever What that means for Asha
Distributed strict two-phase locking 281 she waits on a lock held by a dead machine, with no timeout that is safe
Timestamp-based (the family Spanner is in) 0 she reads a past moment and never takes a lock at all

A global read of a global database, blocking on a lock held by a coordinator in another continent that has just died, is not a system you can operate. So the interesting fight is between Fix A and Fix B.

Why does Fix A break on geography, and on liveness?

The single stamping desk is in one building. Every write in Sydney now costs a round trip to that building before it can even begin.

If the desk is in Iowa, that is roughly 180 milliseconds of pure waiting, on every transaction, forever. You cannot fix it by adding machines, because the whole point is that there is exactly one of them.

It is also a single point of failure for the entire planet's write path. You can replicate it, and TiDB does, using Raft. But replication makes the desk more reliable, not closer. The oracle's problem is not reliability. It is that the speed of light is not negotiable and there is only one desk.

Why does Fix B break in a way you can already quote?

This is the important one, because Fix B looks like it wins.

A hybrid logical clock guarantees that if event X caused event Y, then X's stamp is smaller. That is genuinely enough for a huge class of systems.

Now look again at the registry story. Priya's telephone call to Dev is not a message the database can see. There is no path through the system from the Northgate filing to the Southgate filing. The information travelled through a human being and a telephone line.

Your hybrid-logical-clocks lab hit exactly this case and called it "no external consistency for strangers". External consistency is the property section 5 defines properly. For now, read it as: the order the database records is the order that really happened.

A fast-clock writer, a slow-clock reader, no causal path between them, and the result was 86 out of 86 reads stale. Not most. All of them.

🔮 Predict: Charging the writer sounds like the worst of the three. Writes are already the expensive path. Why would anyone choose it?

Reveal

Three reasons, and all three are about what the other two options do to reads.

Reads become free. If Meera has already guaranteed that her stamp is in everyone's past, then Asha needs no locks, no retries, no note carried from Priya, and no conversation with a clerk in another continent. She reads the register as of a moment, at whichever office is nearest.

The paper's own microbenchmarks put numbers on how lopsided that is.

| Operation | Latency | Throughput at 1, 3 and 5 replicas | |---|---|---| | A write | 14.4 ms | falls from 4.1 to 2.8 K ops/sec | | A snapshot read, meaning a lock-free read at an agreed past moment | 1.3 ms | rises 13.5 → 38.5 → 50.0 K ops/sec |

The cost is bounded and known, not statistical. A reader-pays design has a retry rate that depends on load and skew. A writer-pays design adds a fixed, predictable amount to every write.

You can buy the cost down with hardware. Meera's wait is proportional to how wrong her clock might be. That is a number you can shrink by spending money on clocks, which is exactly what Google did, and what nobody else could do in 2012.

If you guessed "nobody would, it is obviously worse", you were pricing the write and forgetting that most workloads are mostly reads.

5. What exactly must the registry guarantee?

In one breath: If the registry finished one document and told somebody so, and only then did anyone start a second one, the first must carry the smaller stamp. That has to hold even when the news travelled by telephone.

Strip the story back to one sentence with no jargon in it.

If the registry finished document A and told somebody so, and only afterwards did anyone start document B, then A's stamp must be smaller than B's stamp. That holds no matter which office each was filed in, and no matter how the news travelled.

Read the last clause again, because it is the whole difficulty. No matter how the news travelled.

Through the network. Through Priya's telephone call to Dev. Through a customer refreshing a page on a second device. Through a message queue. Through a human walking down a corridor.

The database cannot see any of those paths. So it cannot rely on seeing them.

The only thing common to every possible path is that time passed. Dev started after Priya finished, in real, physical, wall-clock time.

So if the guarantee is going to hold for paths the database cannot see, the stamps must be anchored to something the database also cannot see directly: actual physical time.

That is the requirement. Everything in the rest of this lab is the pursuit of it.

6. What is the key idea, in two sentences?

In one breath: Build a clock that admits how wrong it might be, then make the clerk wait out her own admission before handing over the receipt. Nobody's clock got more accurate. The trick is entirely in the honesty and in the waiting.

Two sentences. Read them slowly; the whole lab is inside them.

Sentence one: build a clock that admits it might be wrong. Instead of returning a number, it returns a pair of numbers, an earliest and a latest, and it promises that the true instant lies somewhere between them. It never returns "it is 10:04:00.000". It returns "it is somewhere between 10:03:59.996 and 10:04:00.004, and I promise it is in there."

Sentence two: stamp with the latest end, then refuse to admit the change happened until your own clock's earliest end has moved past that stamp. By the time anyone in the world hears that the change is done, its stamp is provably in the past, for every machine, everywhere.

That is Spanner. Everything else is engineering.

Notice what did not happen. Nobody's clock got more accurate. Meera's clock is exactly as good as it was; she has simply stopped pretending she knows the time to the millisecond.

And notice the consequence, because it drives every hardware decision Google made afterwards. The width of that band is, literally, the latency of every write.

Work it through. A clock that admits it might be wrong by 100 ms would make every write cost 200 ms. The stamp is taken from one edge of the band, and the wait runs all the way to the other edge.

So Google put GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every datacenter. Not to make time correct, but to make the admission of error small enough to sleep off.

Say it back. Without using the words timestamp, interval or consistency, explain to a friend why Meera holding the receipt back for a moment fixes a problem that a better wall clock does not.

A good answer sounds like "Meera writes the latest time her clock could possibly mean, which is a little bit into the future. Then she waits until even the earliest reading on her own clock has gone past it. After that she can hand the receipt over, because that moment is now behind every clock in the country, not just hers. Anyone who hears about the sale afterwards is guaranteed to write down a bigger number."

7. What are these things called, now that you have earned the words?

In one breath: Nine load-bearing terms. Every one of them names something you have already watched happen to Priya, Meera, Arjun, Dev or Asha. The words come last, on purpose.

What is external consistency?

In plain words: if the system told you an operation was done before you started another one, the system's own record must agree that yours came second.

Meera handed Priya a receipt. Dev started the mortgage after that. The register must therefore say the deed came first. When it says 10:04 and 9:57, it is telling you an order that did not happen.

Stated precisely, this is how the Spanner paper states it as an invariant. Suppose the commit of transaction T₁ happens, in absolute physical time, before the start of T₂. Then the commit timestamp of T₁ is smaller than the commit timestamp of T₂.

Why does the word external appear at all? To name the part of the guarantee that lives outside the database.

Serializability says the transactions are equivalent to some serial order. External consistency says they are equivalent to the specific serial order that a person standing outside the system with a stopwatch would have observed.

The difference only shows up when information leaves the system and comes back. A telephone call. A second device. A message queue. That is exactly why the word is external.

Hold on to this, because it is the sharpest fact in the lab. The deed at 10:04 and the mortgage at 9:57 satisfy serializability. There is a serial order, mortgage then deed, and the register is a faithful record of it. They violate external consistency, because that order is not the one that happened.

It is the same property your linearizability-vs-serializability lab called strict serializability. Spanner's papers say "external consistency". Jepsen and the academic literature say "strict serializability". Google Cloud's documentation uses both. One property, three names.

What is TrueTime, and why does it hand you two numbers?

TrueTime is Google's clock service. It is the banded wall clock from the story, built for real: it reports the time as a range instead of an instant.

It is an interface with three calls, and the second two are only conveniences over the first.

The call In words What comes back
TT.now() "what time is it, and how sure are you?" a TTinterval, written [earliest, latest], guaranteed to contain the absolute time at which you called
TT.after(t) "has moment t definitely gone past?" true or false
TT.before(t) "has moment t definitely not arrived?" true or false

Why does the term exist at all? Because every other clock API in computing hands you a single number, and by doing so hides the one fact you needed. System.currentTimeMillis() is confident and unaccountable. It has no way to tell you it might be six milliseconds out.

TrueTime's whole contribution is that it makes the uncertainty a value in your program, one you can branch on.

A worked example. At absolute time 100.000 ms, on a machine whose clock is 2 ms fast and whose error bound is 4 ms, TT.now() returns [99.998, 100.006]. The true instant, 100.000, is inside that pair. That containment is the only promise TrueTime makes. It does not claim the midpoint is right. It does not claim the machine is accurate. It claims the truth is in the box.

This is the l half of the hybrid logical clock you built in hybrid-logical-clocks, the real wall reading that is never incremented, with one addition. TrueTime also reports how much you should distrust it.

What is ε, and why is it the price list?

Start with the thing in the story. The band on Meera's wall clock has a width. Half that width is the number we care about.

Say it in words before you meet the symbol: half the width of the band is how wrong this clock might be, right now, at worst.

Engineers write that number ε, said "epsilon". So ε, half the width of the band, is ε = (latest − earliest) / 2.

One thing ε is not: a measurement of the actual error. It is a bound that the clock daemon is prepared to guarantee. It is derived conservatively from four ingredients, and the paper gives production figures for the ones that move.

Ingredient of ε In words The paper's production figure
Worst-case drift of the local oscillator how fast the crystal can wander 200 µs per second
Time since the last synchronisation how long the wander has had to accumulate a poll every 30 s
Uncertainty of the time source itself how sure the GPS or atomic master is folded into the ~1 ms below
Network delay to the time master how stale the answer was on arrival about 1 ms

Put those together and ε is a sawtooth. Right after a machine polls a time master, ε is small. It then climbs steadily until the next poll.

Do the arithmetic yourself. Thirty seconds at 200 µs per second is 30 × 200 µs = 6 ms of accumulated doubt between polls. Add roughly 1 ms for the network delay to the master, and the sawtooth runs from about 1 ms to about 7 ms, averaging about 4 ms.

Why does the term earn its own name? Because it is the price list. Almost every latency number in Spanner is written in units of ε.

You have met this quantity before under another name. It is the max-offset, or uncertainty interval, of your hybrid-logical-clocks lab. CockroachDB's default for it is 500 ms, which is the same quantity two orders of magnitude larger. That single fact is why CockroachDB cannot afford to wait it off, and bills readers instead.

What is commit wait?

In plain words: after deciding a change is committed, deliberately doing nothing for a few milliseconds before telling anybody.

In the story it is Meera putting the receipt face down on the desk and watching the clock. She has stamped the document with the late edge of her band. She waits until the early edge of her band has crawled past that stamp. Only then does she hand the receipt over.

The engineer's version, in words first: having chosen the commit timestamp, the coordinator blocks until its own clock's earliest end has passed that stamp, and only then releases locks and answers the client.

The same sentence with the symbols in it: having chosen commit timestamp s, the coordinator blocks until TT.after(s) is true, then releases locks and returns. Since s was itself chosen as some TT.now().latest, the expected wait is about 2ε, which is the full width of the band.

Why does the moment deserve a name? Because it is where the design pays for its honesty. It is the only place in the entire system where a machine is deliberately idle for the sake of correctness.

Follow one wait all the way through, with ε at 7 ms.

Step The number What it means

The early edge reaches the stamp absolute 100.014 the earliest end always trails true time by 7 ms

What is a Paxos group here, and why does it never telephone another one?

In plain words: the handful of machines, in different places, that hold copies of one slice of the data and vote on changes to it. Meera is one of them. So is Arjun.

Technically, a Paxos group is a replicated state machine over one tablet, which is one contiguous range of the key space, with one long-lived leader. Writes go through the leader. Reads may be served by any replica that is sufficiently up to date.

You already built this in paxos and raft, and this lab uses it as a black box. So why does it get a fresh entry here?

Because a Spanner database is thousands or millions of these groups, each ordering only its own writes, with no communication between groups at all unless a single transaction happens to touch two of them.

Sit with that. There is no central sequencer. The global order is not produced by any single thing. It emerges from the timestamps, and nothing in the system ever writes it down. That is the whole architectural bet.

Who is the participant leader, and who is the coordinator leader?

When one transaction touches two slices of data, each slice's leader takes part. One of them is elected to run the two-phase commit. Meera and Arjun both take part; one of them runs the show.

Precisely: for a multi-group transaction the client picks one participant group as coordinator. That group's Paxos leader becomes the coordinator leader and runs two-phase commit over the other participant leaders.

Now the sentence that is the punchline of an earlier lab. The coordinator's transaction state is written through its own Paxos group, so it is replicated.

Your two-phase-commit lab's central complaint was that a coordinator crash blocks every participant indefinitely, because the decision lived only in the coordinator's memory. Here the decision lives in a Paxos log held by a quorum.

A dead coordinator leader is replaced by another replica of the same group, which reads the decision off the log and finishes the job. The composition "2PC over Paxos" is the fix, and that one sentence is what makes it work.

What is a leader lease, and what does disjointness forbid?

In plain words: a clerk holds the desk for a fixed stretch of time, and two clerks of the same office can never hold overlapping stretches. Meera's shift ends before the next clerk's begins, with no overlap at all.

Technically, a would-be leader collects timed lease votes from a quorum. The lease defaults to 10 seconds. The invariant Spanner depends on is that within each Paxos group, each leader's lease interval is disjoint from every other leader's.

There is one more rule, and it is the story's rule about tidying up before you go home. Before abdicating, a leader must wait until TT.after(s_max). In words: until the highest stamp it ever handed out is definitely in the past. That highest stamp is what s_max means.

Why does any of this exist? So that timestamps increase across clerk changes, not merely within one clerk's shift. A leader may only assign timestamps inside its own lease interval. Disjoint leases therefore give disjoint timestamp ranges, in order, for free.

This is your split-brain lab's lesson applied to time. That lab concluded that you cannot stop a machine believing it is the leader; you can only stop its effects counting. Here the effect that must not count is stamping a document with a time that belongs to the next clerk's shift.

What is safe time, and why does it have two halves?

In plain words: the newest moment a particular office is allowed to answer questions about. "I can tell you the state as of any time up to here, and no further."

When Asha asks Arjun about a moment, Arjun must be sure that nothing filed elsewhere could still land before that moment. Safe time is his honest answer to "how far forward can I speak?"

The engineers' name for it is t_safe, and it is the smaller of two numbers.

The half In words Its value
t_safe_paxos "the newest thing this office has actually filed" the timestamp of the highest Paxos write this replica has applied
t_safe_TM "the oldest thing still half-filed, minus a tick" infinity when no transaction is prepared but undecided; otherwise min(prepare timestamps) − 1
t_safe "the furthest forward I can honestly speak" min(t_safe_paxos, t_safe_TM)

A prepare timestamp, in that middle row, is the moment a participant proposes when it promises it can commit. Section 10.4 works one through from scratch.

The rule that uses all this is one line. A replica may serve a read at timestamp t only when t ≤ t_safe.

Why must it exist? Because a read at a moment is only correct if the replica has everything up to that moment. Without the check, a lagging office would cheerfully answer a question while omitting committed filings, which is the exact bug from section 1.

Work the example, because the surprising part is the second half.

The situation The number Why
Writes applied up to 100.500 so t_safe_paxos = 100.500
One prepared transaction, prepare timestamp 100.300 so t_safe_TM = 100.299
Safe time is the smaller of the two 100.299 t_safe = min(t_safe_paxos, t_safe_TM), so t_safe = 100.299
A read at 100.400 must wait the prepared transaction might yet commit at 100.350 and land underneath it

Read the last row again. The replica "has" everything up to 100.500 and still cannot answer a question about 100.400. That is honesty about the future, not about the past.

This is the lock check in your Percolator reader from the distributed-transactions lab. Same problem, a reader meeting an undecided writer, solved on the server side by waiting instead of on the reader side by resolving.

What is the difference between the three kinds of read?

Three different things, and Spanner keeps them strictly separate. This distinction is the practical payoff of the whole design, so it is worth being exact about it.

A read-write transaction is the expensive path. It takes locks, it goes to leaders, it gets a commit timestamp, and it pays commit wait. It may abort, and the client must be prepared to retry it. Deadlocks are avoided with a rule called wound-wait, which section 11.8 tells as a story.

A read-only transaction takes no locks at all and blocks nobody. The system assigns it a timestamp, and it then executes as a set of snapshot reads at that timestamp. It is not "a transaction that happens to have only reads in it". It is a genuinely different mechanism, and it is externally consistent.

A snapshot read is a read at a timestamp or staleness bound that the client chose. It is also lock-free. The only difference from a read-only transaction is who picks the moment.

The path Locks? Who picks the moment May abort? Where it can run
Read-write transaction yes, pessimistic the coordinator, at commit yes leaders only
Read-only transaction none the system no any sufficiently current replica
Snapshot read none the client no any sufficiently current replica

Why does the distinction exist? Because the expensive machinery, meaning locks and commit wait and leaders, is needed only for writes.

Separating the read paths is what makes reads cheap, local, and endlessly scalable. It is why Spanner can serve Asha a globally consistent answer from the office in her own city.

8. Which two pictures should you carry, and where does the nice one lie?

In one breath: One picture is the registry with two new pieces of furniture. The other is four objects and three invariants. Carry both, because the nice one hides exactly four things, and all four cost money.

What changes about the furniture in the registry?

Same two offices, same rubber stamps, and two changes to what is on the wall and what the clerk does.

The first change is the clock. It now has two hands and a shaded band between them. Not one hand claiming to know the time. Two hands, and a promise from the clock's manufacturer that the real time is somewhere inside the shaded band.

When the office has just checked its clock against the observatory, the band is narrow. As the hours pass without checking, the clock widens its own band, because it knows its mechanism drifts.

The second change is a new rule for Meera. Stamp the document with the late edge of the band, which is the most pessimistic reading available. Then put the receipt face down on the desk and wait, watching the clock, until the early edge has moved past the number she stamped. Only then does the receipt leave her hand.

The wait is the invention. After it, Meera can say something remarkable: "the moment printed on this document is now in the past. And it is in the past according to every clock in the country, not just mine."

Any document that anybody starts after receiving that receipt is guaranteed to get a bigger number. Dev's mortgage included.

Which four objects does the engineer's picture have?

Four objects, and the discipline is in keeping them separate.

The data is a multi-version key-value store, written (key, timestamp) → value. Nothing is ever updated in place; a write adds a version. A read at timestamp t returns, for each key, the newest version whose timestamp is at or below t. This is ordinary MVCC, and you know it from isolation-levels.

The groups come from cutting the key space into contiguous ranges. Each range is replicated by its own Paxos group with its own leader. Groups do not talk to each other. A group's leader holds a lock table for writes, and a transaction manager for cross-group transactions.

The clock is a function from a machine to an interval containing the true instant. Its width, 2ε, is the only global parameter in the system.

The three invariants are, together, the entire correctness argument.

Invariant In words What it defends
Monotonicity within a group, stamps only ever go up, including when the clerk changes snapshot reads inside one group
Disjointness within a group, two clerks' shifts never overlap, and a clerk stamps only inside her own shift monotonicity, across leader changes
External consistency if T₁ commits before T₂ starts in absolute time, then s₁ < s₂ the order Priya, Dev and Asha actually lived through

That third one is delivered by exactly two rules, and you have now seen both. Pick the stamp as s = TT.now().latest, then commit-wait until TT.after(s).

Where does the story lie to you?

Four places. Each one is something a real system needs that a registry does not.

Lie 1: is there really one book?

In the story you imagine a single register that everything is filed into, so the order is maintained by the book itself.

In Spanner there is no book. There are millions of independent Paxos groups, each ordering only its own writes, with no channel between them.

The single global order exists only as a property of the numbers, and nothing in the system ever materialises it. Two transactions on opposite sides of the world are ordered relative to each other by arithmetic that neither one performed.

This is exactly why the timestamps have to be anchored to physical time. It is the only shared reference the groups have, precisely because they never talk.

Lie 2: is Meera's pause really free?

A clerk pausing for a moment costs nothing. Commit wait sits in the critical path of every write, and the paper puts it at about 5 ms.

Worse, it holds locks for the whole wait. So it also taxes every Asha queued behind those locks.

The analogy makes the central trade-off feel weightless. It is not weightless. It is the entire bill.

Lie 3: is the clock's honesty a fact?

The registry's clock manufacturer promises the true time is in the band. In reality that promise is conditional.

It holds only while the machine's oscillator drifts no faster than the assumed worst case of 200 µs per second. A machine whose crystal degrades past that can return an interval that does not contain the true time. Then commit wait waits for the wrong number, and external consistency breaks silently.

Spanner's answer is not "this cannot happen". It is defence in depth. Time masters cross-check each other and evict themselves on divergence. Daemons poll several masters and apply a variant of Marzullo's algorithm to reject liars. Any machine whose frequency excursion exceeds the worst-case bound removes itself from the fleet.

Your hybrid-logical-clocks lab made exactly this point and put a number on it. One node running 60 ms fast in a ±8 ms cluster infected everyone downstream, and the cluster's bound went from 16 to 67. A clock bound is a promise about hardware, and it is only as good as the hardware.

Lie 4: does anything in a registry abort?

A clerk never says "sorry, start again."

Spanner read-write transactions take locks and use wound-wait, so under contention a transaction can be aborted and must be retried by the client. Every Cloud Spanner client library ships a retry loop for this reason. Application code that does not expect an abort is broken code, and the analogy hides that completely.

10.1 What if you just stamp with the late edge of the band?

In one breath: Take the most pessimistic reading your clock offers, use it as the stamp, replicate, and tell the client. It is the careful choice, and it still produces the registry bug.

Here is the first design a good engineer writes.

When a transaction's writes are all locked and ready, ask the local clock for TT.now() and use its latest endpoint as the commit timestamp. Replicate through Paxos. Acknowledge to the client.

Why the late edge, rather than the early edge or the middle? Because the late edge is the only endpoint guaranteed to be at or after the true instant.

A stamp that is behind true time is exactly what produced the registry bug in section 2. So this design is already the careful one. It is not naive.

Now trace it. Meera has gone a long time without checking her clock against the observatory, so her band is wide: ε is 7 ms. Arjun checked recently, so his band is narrow: ε is 1 ms.

Absolute time What happens The numbers

100.000 → 102.000 Paxos replicates the commit record. applied at 102.000

From the asymmetry of the honesty.

It exists even when both clocks are exactly right.

Sit with this, because it kills the intuition that better clocks alone would fix the problem. Perfect clocks with imperfect knowledge of their own perfection still produce the bug.

If you guessed "Arjun's clock must have been slow", you have gone back to the section 2 story, where the clocks really were wrong. This is a different and nastier failure. What has to be fixed is not the clock. It is the fact that a stamp chosen from the pessimistic end has not yet arrived in everyone's past.

10.2 What does commit wait actually fix?

In one breath: If the stamp is still in the future for somebody, wait until it is in the past for everybody. The band tells you exactly when that is.

The fix follows directly from that reveal, and it is Meera's face-down receipt.

Rule (commit wait). Having chosen commit timestamp s, do not release locks and do not acknowledge to the client until TT.after(s) is true. In words: until the local band's earliest endpoint has passed s.

10.3 Now make reads cheap. How does that break?

In one breath: A read that takes no locks should be servable by the nearest office. Try it, and you rebuild the original bug on the read side.

Writes are correct now. Time for the payoff. A read-only transaction takes no locks, so it ought to be servable by any replica, including the one in Asha's own city.

Design v2. Assign the read-only transaction a timestamp s_read = TT.now().latest. Send its reads to any replica. The replica returns, for each key, the newest version at or below s_read.

Trace the break. Asha issues a read-only transaction at absolute 120.000 from Singapore. Its timestamp is assigned at a leader whose ε is 4 ms, so s_read = 124.000. It goes to replica R, a Singapore replica of G1.

R has applied Paxos writes up to timestamp 107.000. It has the deed. But Meera has since committed two more writes, at 118.000 and 121.000, and the network to Singapore is slow. R has not received them.

R does exactly what design v2 says. It returns the newest version at or below 124.000, which is the one at 107.000.

Asha asked for the state as of 124.000 and got the state as of 107.000, with no indication that anything was missing.

We have rebuilt the original bug on the read side. The write path is now honest and the read path is not.

Reveal

Not "the newest thing I have". That is the trap, and it is what R used. The number it needs is the newest point below which nothing new can still arrive.

Call it t_safe. The rule is that a replica may serve a read at t only if t ≤ t_safe. Otherwise it waits.

If you guessed "the highest write it has applied", you have picked exactly the first of the two halves, and the next section is entirely about why one half is not enough.

10.4 What is safe time, and why does it have two halves?

In one breath: One half says how far this office has actually filed. The other says how far it dare speak while a document is half-filed between two offices. Safe time is the smaller of the two.

The first half is easy.

t_safe_paxos is the timestamp of the highest Paxos write this replica has actually applied. Because timestamps within a group increase monotonically, and Paxos applies writes in order, nothing will ever arrive at or below this number again.

In the trace above, R's t_safe_paxos is 107.000 and Asha's read wants 124.000. So R waits, until the writes at 118.000 and 121.000 arrive and something above 124.000 lands. Correct, and honest.

The second half comes from a failure you already know how to construct: two-phase commit across two groups.

Transaction T₃ writes to both G1 and G2, which means both Meera and Arjun are involved. Each participant leader acquires its write locks, chooses a prepare timestamp, and logs a prepare record through its own Paxos group.

Say Meera's group prepares at 200.000 and Arjun's at 200.500. The coordinator leader will choose a commit timestamp s₃ ≥ every prepare timestamp, ≥ TT.now().latest, and greater than any timestamp it has previously assigned. Say 205.000.

It has not decided yet. The transaction is in flight, which is the story's document that is half-filed between two offices.

Now a read arrives at a G1 replica asking for timestamp 203.000.

That replica's t_safe_paxos might be 204.000, because it has applied everything G1 has committed. T₃ is prepared and undecided. If T₃ commits at 205.000 then it is above 203.000 and irrelevant, but Spanner cannot know that yet.

The commit timestamp could legally have been anything from 200.500 upward. Until the decision is made, the state of the keys T₃ touched is indeterminate for every timestamp at or above G1's prepare timestamp.

t_safe_TM is infinity when there are no prepared-but-uncommitted transactions. Otherwise it is min(prepare timestamps at this group) − 1. Here that is 199.999.

t_safe = min(t_safe_paxos, t_safe_TM), which here is 199.999.

So the read at 203.000 blocks. And here is the sting. Every read at 203.000 blocks, including reads of keys T₃ never touched, because safe time as defined so far is one number for the whole group.

The paper calls those false conflicts, and fixes them by making safe time fine-grained. The leader already keeps a lock table mapping key ranges to lock state. It stores prepared-transaction timestamps per key range in that same table, and an arriving read is checked only against the ranges it actually touches.

This looks like an optimisation and is really a survivability requirement. Without it, one slow cross-group transaction stalls every reader in its group.

10.5 What happens when the clerk's shift ends?

In one breath: Everything so far assumed one clerk per office forever. Take that away and the stamps can go backwards, because the old clerk stamped several milliseconds into the future before she died.

Leader L1 of G1 holds a lease and has handed out timestamps up to s_max = 210.000. Remember that stamps are chosen from the late edge of the band. So s_max can sit several milliseconds ahead of true time.

At absolute 205.000, L1's zone loses power. Meera's office goes dark mid-shift.

A quorum of the remaining replicas elects L2. L2's own clock says the time is about 205, so under the section 10.1 rule it would assign the next transaction a timestamp of about 205.007. That is below s_max.

Monotonicity is dead. G1 has now stamped a later write with an earlier number than an earlier write, and every snapshot read in the group is unsound.

The rules that close it. A Paxos leader holds a timed lease, 10 seconds by default, granted by a quorum of lease votes. Spanner maintains the disjointness invariant: within a group, no two leaders' lease intervals overlap. A leader may only assign timestamps inside its own lease interval, and s_max is advanced to every timestamp it assigns. Before voluntarily abdicating, a leader must wait until TT.after(s_max).

Trace the fix. L1's lease cannot have extended past its final timestamp assignment in any way that lets L2's lease start early. L2's lease can only begin after L1's has ended, and L1 could only assign timestamps within its own lease.

So every timestamp L2 assigns is inside a strictly later interval than every timestamp L1 assigned. Monotonicity across clerks falls out of disjointness for free.

Notice the shape of this argument. It is your split-brain lab's argument applied to time. That lab concluded that you cannot stop a machine believing it is the leader. You can only stop its effects counting.

Here the effect that must not count is stamping with a number from the next clerk's shift. The mechanism that stops it is an interval a machine may only act inside, granted by a quorum. That is a fencing token whose values happen to be timestamps.

And it costs, in exactly the way a ten-second shift handover costs. Because leases are 10 seconds, killing a leader zone without warning leaves each group unavailable until its lease expires.

The paper's experiment What happens to throughput How long
Kill the leader zone with no notice drops almost to zero recovers over roughly 10 seconds as leases expire
Kill it after a leadership handoff a dip of 3–4% invisible on the graph

The 10-second lease is a straight trade. Shorter leases mean faster failover and much more lease-renewal traffic.

10.6 What about the drawer nobody ever writes to?

In one breath: Safe time only moves when writes land. A group nobody writes to therefore has a safe time that never moves, and a read of it waits forever.

One more break, and it is delightfully unobvious.

Group G7 holds a reference table, the drawer of standard clause wordings that gets updated once a month. A client issues a strong read-only transaction, which picks s_read = TT.now().latest = 300.000.

G7's replicas have t_safe_paxos = 1.000, because the last write to G7 was ages ago, and t_safe_paxos is the timestamp of the highest applied write.

300.000 > 1.000, so the read blocks. It blocks forever, or until somebody happens to write to a table nobody writes to.

The rule that closes it (MinNextTS). Each leader maintains a promise MinNextTS(n): the minimum timestamp it may assign to the next Paxos write after sequence number n. A replica that has applied through n may advance t_safe_paxos to MinNextTS(n) − 1. The promise is safe across leader changes for the reason you just saw: promised timestamps lie inside the leader's lease, and leases are disjoint. A leader advances MinNextTS every 8 seconds by default, and slaves can also request an advance on demand.

The consequence is quotable, and it is the kind of thing that turns up in a support ticket. In the absence of prepared transactions, healthy replicas of an idle Paxos group can serve reads at timestamps up to 8 seconds old in the worst case.

10.7 Is there a shortcut when the read stays in one office?

In one breath: If the read touches only one group, its leader already knows a timestamp that is guaranteed correct and guaranteed not to wait. Use that instead of asking the clock.

For a read-only transaction whose scope is a single Paxos group, which is most of them, Spanner does better than TT.now().latest.

The group's leader knows LastTS(), the timestamp of the last committed write in that group. If there are no prepared transactions, assigning s_read = LastTS() is trivially externally consistent. The read sees the last write, so it is ordered after it, and there is nothing newer for it to miss.

Why is that strictly better? Because it is smaller. A smaller read timestamp is already below t_safe, so it never waits.

It is the reason a single-group strong read in Spanner costs about a millisecond rather than about 2ε.

For a multi-group scope, Spanner's shipped implementation takes the simple option: s_read = TT.now().latest, which may wait for safe time to advance. The paper describes the better option, a negotiation round with all the groups' leaders based on their LastTS() values, and says they did not implement it.

10.8 What does the whole write path look like, end to end?

In one breath: Eight steps. Six of them are the two-phase commit you already built. One is commit wait. The one that makes a coordinator crash survivable is the words "through Paxos" in steps 4 and 6.

With every refinement in place, here is the complete life of a cross-group read-write transaction. Every step is one you have now seen a reason for.

  1. The client opens the transaction and issues reads. These go to the leader of each group, which takes read locks and returns current data. Deadlocks are avoided by wound-wait: an older transaction wounds a younger one holding what it needs, and a younger one waits.
  2. Writes are buffered at the client, and nothing is sent yet. This is why reads inside a Spanner read-write transaction do not see that transaction's own writes.
  3. The client keeps the transaction alive with keepalives, then begins commit. It chooses one participant group as coordinator and sends each participant leader the buffered writes for its keys, plus the coordinator's identity. Having the client drive 2PC avoids sending the data twice across wide-area links.
  4. Each non-coordinator participant leader acquires write locks, chooses a prepare timestamp greater than any timestamp it has previously assigned, logs a prepare record through Paxos, and tells the coordinator its prepare timestamp.
  5. The coordinator leader acquires its own write locks, skips prepare, and chooses the commit timestamp s. That stamp is at least every participant's prepare timestamp, greater than TT.now().latest as of when it received the commit message, and greater than anything it has assigned before.
  6. It logs a commit record through Paxos, or an abort record if it timed out waiting.
  7. Commit wait: it blocks until TT.after(s). This typically overlaps with the Paxos round trip in step 6.
  8. It sends s to the client and to every participant leader. Each logs the outcome through its own Paxos group. All participants apply at the same timestamp and release locks.

Steps 1–2 and 4–6 are the two-phase commit you already built. Step 7 is the entire subject of this lab. And the fact that steps 4 and 6 say "through Paxos" is what makes a coordinator crash survivable rather than fatal.

10.9 What else does an honest clock make possible?

In one breath: Schema changes across the whole database, atomically, with nothing blocking. It works only because every machine agrees what a future moment means.

One feature falls out of TrueTime that is worth seeing, because it shows the idea being used for something other than commit ordering. Atomic schema changes across the whole database, without blocking.

An ordinary transaction cannot do it. The participant count would be every group in the database, potentially millions, and the table above tells you what that costs.

Instead, a schema-change transaction is assigned a timestamp in the future, registered during prepare. Reads and writes then synchronise against it. Any operation whose timestamp is before t proceeds against the old schema. Any operation whose timestamp is after t blocks until the schema change is in place.

The paper's own remark is the one to remember. Without TrueTime, defining the schema change to happen at t would be meaningless, because no set of machines would agree on when t was.

11. What are the nine ways it fails, and what survives each one?

In one breath: Nine failures, each a short story about Meera, Arjun, Asha or Ben. Each one says how to cause it, what breaks, what still holds, and what the person on call actually sees. One table at the end lines them all up.

11.1 What happens when the clock loses confidence?

Meera has not been able to check her clock against the observatory for a while, so she widens her own band. Nothing is wrong. She is simply less sure than she was.

To cause it, load simulator scenario 3, or drag the ε slider from 1 ms to 20 ms with commit wait on. In production, take two time masters in a datacenter down for maintenance. That is exactly what happened to Google on 13 April in the paper's own monitoring data, and ε rose datacenter-wide for about an hour.

Commit wait is 2ε, so every write's acknowledgement is delayed in proportion. Locks are held for the whole wait, so throughput falls for writers and for every Asha queued behind those locks. Strong read-only transactions also pick larger s_read = TT.now().latest values, so more of them wait on safe time.

The right response is nothing at all. The system is behaving correctly. It is degrading in latency in exact proportion to how much it distrusts itself.

External consistency survives, fully and without qualification. What is lost is write latency and throughput.

This is the reason a TrueTime-ε dashboard exists. When Ben sees a latency incident with no CPU anomaly, no disk anomaly and no network anomaly, but a raised ε, he is looking at a time incident. The paper names the two causes it saw: time-master unavailability, and network-link congestion inflating the round trip to the masters.

11.2 What if the clock lies?

This is the one that keeps people up at night, because it is the assumption underneath every argument in this lab.

To cause it, load simulator scenario 4, which sets one region's true offset outside its own ε and leaves commit wait on. In production it is a degrading crystal oscillator, drifting faster than the assumed 200 µs per second.

Every argument in this lab rests on the true instant being inside [earliest, latest]. If it is not, then TT.after(s) returns true while s is still in the future for other machines. Commit wait waits for the wrong number, and external consistency breaks with no error, no log line and no symptom, other than Asha occasionally seeing an impossible state.

Run scenario 4 and watch the verdict banner go red with commit wait switched on. That is the entire point of the scenario.

Spanner's response is defence in depth, and none of it is a proof. Time masters compare against each other and evict themselves on divergence. Each daemon polls several masters, some nearby, some far, some GPS and some atomic, and applies a variant of Marzullo's algorithm to discard liars. Any machine whose frequency excursion exceeds the worst case derived from component specs and operating environment is evicted from the fleet.

The paper's argument for why this is acceptable is empirical, not formal. Bad CPUs are 6 times more likely than bad clocks. So TrueTime is as trustworthy as any other component Spanner already depends on.

In principle nothing survives here. This is the assumption the whole design stands on, and what is lost is external consistency, silently.

This is why CockroachDB nodes commit suicide when they detect their offset exceeds max-offset, and why MongoDB signs clusterTime. Your hybrid-logical-clocks lab traced the infection path: one node 60 ms fast in a ±8 ms cluster dragged the whole cluster's bound from 16 to 67, and it never came back down.

11.3 What if Meera's whole office loses power?

To cause it, load simulator scenario 6. In production it is the paper's "leader-hard" experiment: kill all servers in the leader zone with no notice.

Each group's leader lease has to expire before a new leader can be elected, because disjointness forbids overlapping lease intervals. Leases are 10 seconds, and expiry times are spread across that window, so groups recover staggered over roughly 10 seconds.

How the zone is killed Completion rate Recovery
No notice, the "leader-hard" case drops almost to 0 about 10 seconds, as leases expire
After a graceful handoff, "leader-soft" a dip of 3–4% invisible on the graph

External consistency, monotonicity, durability and every committed write all survive. What is lost is availability of that group's writes, and of strong reads that need the leader for timestamp assignment, for up to a lease.

Ben's rule follows directly. Planned maintenance must drain leadership first. The difference between a 3% dip and a ten-second outage is entirely whether the handoff happened. In Cloud Spanner this is why leader region placement is a configuration decision, and why rolling operations are staged.

11.4 What if the coordinator dies mid-two-phase-commit?

To cause it, load simulator scenario 5 and kill the coordinator between prepare and commit.

In the classic protocol from your two-phase-commit lab, the participants are stuck. They have promised to be able to commit, they cannot unilaterally decide, and the only machine that knows the answer is gone.

In Spanner the coordinator's transaction state was logged through its own Paxos group, so a quorum of replicas holds it. A new coordinator leader is elected in that group, after lease expiry, and reads the decision off the log.

Atomicity, durability and external consistency all survive. What is lost is availability of the affected key ranges. Every participant holds its locks and pins t_safe_TM for as long as the decision is missing, so conflicting readers and writers wait.

For Ben, the blocking window is bounded by lease expiry rather than unbounded. That is the difference between an incident and a page at 3 a.m.

This is precisely what your distributed-transactions lab meant by "running two-phase commit over Paxos mitigates the availability problems". It is a different fix from Percolator's, which deletes the blocking by moving the commit point into the data rather than by replicating the coordinator.

11.5 Why does one half-filed document stall readers who never touch it?

To cause it, load simulator scenario 5, start a cross-group transaction, pause it after prepare, and then issue a read at "now" against an unrelated key in the same group.

As first defined, t_safe_TM = min(prepare timestamps) − 1 is one number for the entire group. So a single undecided transaction freezes the group's readable horizon. Asha's read waits even though it can never conflict with anything.

The response is the fine-grained refinement from section 10.4: prepared timestamps stored per key range in the lock table, and reads checked only against the ranges they actually touch.

Correctness of every read that does return survives. What is lost is read latency for a potentially unrelated population of readers.

This one is genuinely confusing the first time Ben sees it. Long-running or stuck cross-group transactions show up as read latency, not write latency. Cloud Spanner's lock statistics tables exist for exactly this.

11.6 What if a replica falls behind and Asha wants "now"?

To cause it, load simulator scenario 7, slow the network to one replica, then issue a strong read there.

The read timestamp s_read is close to now. The replica's t_safe_paxos is the highest write it has applied. It must wait for the gap to close.

Note the special case from section 10.6. If the group is idle, the gap closes only when MinNextTS advances, which is every 8 seconds by default.

Consistency survives. The read never returns a partial or stale answer. What is lost is latency, converted from staleness, and this is the trade made explicit.

The production response is exactly what stale reads are for. Ask for bounded staleness instead of a strong read, and Spanner picks the newest timestamp within your window that the closest replica can serve without blocking. Google's guidance is blunt about the size of the window: use at least 10 seconds of staleness, or you will not get a benefit.

For Ben, a latency spike on reads in one region with normal write latency globally is replica lag, not load.

11.7 What is a hot split, and why is it the commonest incident?

To cause it, make the first column of a table's primary key a timestamp, or an auto-incrementing integer, and write to it at volume.

Spanner assigns work in units of splits, which are contiguous key ranges, and every split has one leader. A monotonically increasing first key component means every new row lands at the end of the last split.

So every insert in the system is serialised through one Paxos group's leader, on one machine. It is as if the whole country had to queue at Meera's desk. Adding nodes does nothing at all.

This is not a Spanner quirk. It is the same range-partitioning hotspot you analysed in partitioning-and-rebalancing. Spanner is more exposed to it than a hash-partitioned store, because it deliberately keeps keys in sorted order so that range scans and interleaved tables work.

🔮 Predict: Ben's instance is at 12% CPU across every node and inserts are queueing. He doubles the node count. What happens to insert throughput?

Reveal

Nothing. It does not improve at all, and the bill doubles.

Every insert is going to the same split, and a split has exactly one leader. The other nodes have nothing to do, which is why the average CPU was 12% in the first place.

If you guessed "it roughly doubles", you were reasoning about a system where work spreads by itself. Range partitioning spreads work by key, and a monotonic key is a key that does not spread.

Spanner does do load-based splitting, which splits a range that is hot even if it is small. But it cannot split a single key, and it cannot split away the fact that the newest key is always at the end.

The documented fixes, in rough order of preference, all do the same thing: they stop consecutive writes landing next to each other.

The fix What it does When to reach for it
Put a high-cardinality column first PRIMARY KEY (UserId, EventTime) rather than (EventTime, UserId) almost always, and it costs nothing
Use a random UUIDv4 scatters rows across the whole key space when there is no natural high-cardinality column
Use a bit-reversed sequence consecutive values land in distant key ranges; Spanner ships it as a DDL object when you genuinely need a dense integer key
Prepend a computed shard ShardId = hash(key) % N, with N around your node count when you must keep an ordered key and can pay for fan-out reads

Everything correctness-related survives a hot split. What is lost is all of your throughput, and all of the money you spent on nodes.

Key Visualizer is the tool Ben opens. A bright horizontal band that marches downward over time is the picture of this bug. It is, by a wide margin, the most common real Cloud Spanner performance incident.

11.8 Why does a transaction abort, and why is the retry loop not optional?

To cause it, run two read-write transactions touching the same rows in opposite orders, at volume.

Read-write transactions are pessimistic, with wound-wait for deadlock avoidance. An older transaction wounds, meaning aborts, a younger one holding a lock it needs. A younger one simply waits for an older one.

Why wound-wait rather than building a waits-for graph and detecting cycles? Because wound-wait needs no global graph, and that matters enormously when the cycle spans continents. Your distributed-transactions lab showed why detection is hopeless there: each shard holds only half the cycle.

Serializability and freedom from deadlock survive. What is lost is the transaction, and the client's work.

The retry loop is therefore not optional. Every Cloud Spanner client library wraps read-write transactions in one, and application code must be written so that the transaction body can be re-executed from the start.

That means no side effects inside it, no caching of values read on a previous attempt, and no assumption that it runs exactly once. This is your idempotency lab's discipline, imposed on you by the database.

For Ben, a rising abort rate with flat CPU is a schema and access-pattern problem, not a capacity problem. Lock-wait statistics identify the contended key ranges.

11.9 What happens in a network partition?

To cause it, cut a region off from the quorum.

Groups whose leader is on the minority side lose their lease and stop. The majority side elects new leaders and continues. The minority side serves no writes and no strong reads.

It can still serve stale reads from its local replicas, up to the staleness it can honestly promise, which is exactly the safe-time machinery from section 10.4 doing its job.

External consistency and every committed write survive. What is lost is availability on the minority side.

This makes Spanner CP, in the terms of your cap-theorem lab. And it makes it PC/EC in the terms of your pacelc lab, because it also chooses consistency over latency when there is no partition. That second choice is precisely what commit wait is.

Eric Brewer's 2017 note on Spanner and CAP makes the practical argument. Spanner is technically CP. But because Google controls the network, and the observed availability exceeds five nines, applications may treat it as though it were CA, while remaining, formally, nothing of the sort.

12. What is guaranteed, what is not, and what did it cost?

In one breath: Six promises, six refusals, and a bill. The bill is paid in the same currency by every system in this family; they differ only in who is handed it.

Checkpoint — you are now in the engineering model. What follows is the summary an engineer should be able to give from memory when asked "so what does Spanner actually promise?"

What does Spanner actually promise?

External consistency, which is the same thing as strict serializability, for read-write transactions. It is delivered by exactly two rules, and you can now recite both: pick TT.now().latest, then commit-wait until TT.after(s). Monotonicity and disjointness make the per-group order hold across leader changes.

Consistent snapshot reads at any timestamp within the retention window, from any sufficiently up-to-date replica, with no locks and no coordination. Multi-group reads are consistent with each other, so there are no fractured reads. That is the failure your distributed-transactions lab counted at 16 in 40 runs, with a perfect two-phase commit and zero crashes.

Atomicity and durability across groups, via a two-phase commit whose every step is logged through Paxos.

Atomic, non-blocking schema change at a chosen future timestamp.

Consistent backups, and consistent MapReduce or analytics at a timestamp, for free, as a corollary of everything above.

What does it explicitly not promise?

That your transaction will not abort. Wound-wait aborts are normal, and the retry loop is part of the contract, not a defensive extra.

Latency independent of geography. A write must reach a Paxos quorum. If your quorum spans continents, your writes cost what light costs.

Correctness if the hardware violates its own drift specification. TrueTime's interval is a conditional promise. This is the one assumption in the system that no software check can fully close.

Availability during a partition, on the minority side. CP, with excellent constants.

That reads never wait. A strong read at "now" can block on safe time, whether because of replica lag, a prepared transaction, or an idle group's 8-second MinNextTS cadence.

Freedom from hotspots. Range partitioning plus a badly chosen primary key defeats the entire architecture, and Spanner will not save you from it.

13.1 How does this lab's picture become the real system?

This lab drew two Paxos groups and a clock. A real Spanner deployment stacks five layers, and each name below is the one you will meet in the paper and in Google's documentation.

Read it as the registry growing up. An office becomes a zone. Meera's filing cabinet becomes a tablet. Her cabinet drawer becomes a directory.

universe          one Spanner deployment (Google runs about three: test, dev/prod, prod-only)
  └── zone        the unit of administrative deployment and physical isolation;
                  roughly a datacenter, and the granularity at which data is replicated.
                  One zonemaster + 100–several thousand spanservers + location proxies.
      └── spanserver
                  serves 100–1000 tablets. At every tablet where it is leader it also
                  runs a lock table (two-phase locking) and a transaction manager (2PC).
          └── tablet
                  a bag of (key, timestamp) → value, stored as B-tree-like files plus a
                  write-ahead log on Colossus, the distributed filesystem.
              └── Paxos state machine
                  one per tablet, with a 10-second leader lease. The replicas of one

                  └── directory (a.k.a. bucket)
                      a set of contiguous keys sharing a prefix. THE UNIT OF DATA
                      MOVEMENT — the placement driver moves whole directories between

Two singletons sit above all of it. The universe master is essentially a status console. The placement driver looks, every few minutes, for data that should move, either to satisfy a changed replication constraint or to balance load.

13.2 What does the product look like, and which knob matters most?

The externally available product is the same system with different nouns. It is called Cloud Spanner.

Instance configurations decide where the replicas live, and this is the single most consequential choice you make.

Configuration Where the replicas are Availability SLA Survives
Regional three read-write replicas in three zones of one region; all three vote, so the quorum is local and writes are fast 99.99% a zone failure
Multi-region read-write replicas in two or more regions, plus witness replicas that vote but store no data and serve no reads, plus optional read-only replicas that hold full data, serve reads, and do not vote 99.999% a whole region
Dual-region two regions in one country, for data-residency rules 99.999% a whole region

Three consequences are worth internalising, and all three follow from things you already know.

Every write goes to the leader first, even when a closer replica exists. In a multi-region instance the leader lives in the configured leader region, so placing that region near your write traffic is a real latency lever. It is the same fact as "Priya must file at Meera's desk, wherever that desk is."

Read-only replicas do not change write cost. That is their whole reason to exist, and it follows directly from quorum size.

Witnesses make the arithmetic work without paying for the data. That is the same tiebreaker role your split-brain lab warned you to place in a third failure domain.

Capacity is bought in nodes, or in thousandths of one, called a processing unit. Data is split into splits automatically, both by size and by load, and Spanner will split a range that is merely hot.

13.3 What do you actually type?

These snippets are illustrations to read, not files to run. They show the shape of the API in the stack you work in.

Locality is expressed in DDL. Interleaving physically stores child rows inside the parent's split, which is how you keep a customer and their orders in one Paxos group. It turns what would be a cross-group two-phase commit into a single-group transaction.

CREATE TABLE Customers (
  CustomerId  STRING(36) NOT NULL,
  Name        STRING(MAX),
) PRIMARY KEY (CustomerId);

CREATE TABLE Orders (
  CustomerId  STRING(36) NOT NULL,
  OrderId     STRING(36) NOT NULL,
  Amount      NUMERIC,
  CreatedAt   TIMESTAMP OPTIONS (allow_commit_timestamp = true),
) PRIMARY KEY (CustomerId, OrderId),
  INTERLEAVE IN PARENT Customers ON DELETE CASCADE;

Read that as a distributed-systems statement rather than a schema statement. Put these rows in the same split, so that transactions over them cost 17 ms rather than 24.5 ms, and never appear in the 200-participant row of section 10.8's table.

Next, avoiding the hot split from section 11.7. When you genuinely need a dense integer key, the shipped answer is a sequence whose bits are reversed, so consecutive values land in distant key ranges.

CREATE SEQUENCE OrderSeq OPTIONS (sequence_kind = 'bit_reversed_positive');

CREATE TABLE Events (
  EventId  INT64 DEFAULT (GET_NEXT_SEQUENCE_VALUE(SEQUENCE OrderSeq)),
  Payload  JSON,
) PRIMARY KEY (EventId);

Then the retry loop, which is not optional. In Java, a read-write transaction body may be executed more than once, so it must be safe to re-run from the start.

// Illustrative. The runner may invoke run() again after an abort.
Long balance = dbClient.readWriteTransaction().run(tx -> {
    Struct row = tx.readRow("Accounts", Key.of(accountId),
                            List.of("Balance"));
    long updated = row.getLong("Balance") - amount;
    tx.buffer(Mutation.newUpdateBuilder("Accounts")
        .set("AccountId").to(accountId)
        .set("Balance").to(updated)
        .build());
    return updated;                       // no side effects in here
});

Finally, buying latency with staleness. A bounded-stale read is served by the nearest replica without waiting on safe time, and Google's own guidance is that a window under about 10 seconds is not worth asking for.

// Illustrative. Reads from the closest replica that can serve within the window.
ResultSet rs = dbClient
    .singleUse(TimestampBound.ofMaxStaleness(15, TimeUnit.SECONDS))
    .executeQuery(Statement.of("SELECT Balance FROM Accounts WHERE AccountId = @id"));

How far back may Asha look? The version-retention window bounds it: one hour by default, configurable up to one week. A read with a timestamp older than the window fails with FAILED_PRECONDITION, which is a much better outcome than silently returning garbage, and is the reason the limit exists.

Which four things get confused with this one?

External consistency versus serializability. Serializability says the result matches some serial order. External consistency says it matches the order that actually happened in real time. Every violation in this lab was serializable.

TrueTime versus NTP. NTP tries to make the clock accurate and tells you nothing about how well it succeeded. TrueTime makes the clock's own error bound a value your program can read and branch on. Spanner's daemons use NTP-like polling; TrueTime is what they publish afterwards.

Commit wait versus "waiting for replication". Commit wait is not waiting for anything to arrive anywhere. Nothing is in flight. It is a machine deliberately idling until a number it already chose is provably in the past. Meera is not waiting for the post.

Spanner versus Cloud Spanner. The same engine. The product adds instance configurations, quotas, a SQL surface and an SLA. Papers describe the former; incidents involve the latter.

Safe time versus staleness. Safe time is the server's statement of what it can answer. Staleness is the client's statement of what it will accept. The second exists so that the first never has to make you wait.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Something like this:

A database that lives on machines in several countries still has to look, to your code, like one machine doing one thing at a time. The usual way to make that illusion is to put a time on every change and sort by it. But the machines' clocks disagree by a few milliseconds, so a change that genuinely happened second can get an earlier time than one that happened first — and then a query returns a version of history that never occurred. You cannot fix this by synchronising the clocks better, because better is not exact, and the machine has no idea how far off it currently is. So instead of asking the clock for a moment, you ask it for a range it is willing to guarantee the moment lies inside — and then you know exactly how much caution to buy.

1.2 "The atomic clocks are not there to make the timestamps right — nothing can do that. They are there to make the clocks' admitted uncertainty small. Spanner's correctness comes from a rule that says: stamp with the most pessimistic reading, then wait until your own clock proves that moment has passed before telling anyone. The hardware just makes that wait about four milliseconds instead of hundreds."

1.3

Who pays Mechanism Consequence
The writer Spanner's commit wait, ≈ 2ε on every acknowledgement reads need no locks, no retries, no token, no leader round trip
The reader CockroachDB's uncertainty restarts — a read whose snapshot lands in the window retries works on commodity clocks with ε ≈ 500 ms, at the cost of an unpredictable retry rate
The client MongoDB's signed clusterTime carried between operations free for the database; only helps sessions that carry the token, and gives strangers nothing

Spanner's choice makes reads cheap because a reader arriving at any replica already knows that everything acknowledged before it started carries a smaller timestamp. It therefore needs to do nothing but pick a timestamp and read versions below it.

1.4 Nothing. That is the literal answer and it is the point. It is not waiting for a message, a disk, or a quorum — all of that has already finished. It is holding its locks and holding back the acknowledgement until its own clock's pessimistic lower edge has passed the timestamp it already chose.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 At real time 108 in scenario 2: Northgate's band is [101, 115], Southgate's is [107, 109], Singapore's is [104, 112]. All three contain 108 — that is TrueTime's entire promise, and every correctness argument in the lab rests on it. Northgate's TT.now().latest is 115. G1's s_max is 107, which is larger than any moment that has yet occurred because it was taken from the pessimistic end of a 14 ms-wide band eight milliseconds ago. A leader routinely holds an s_max in the future; that is exactly why it must wait before abdicating.

2.2 T1 arrives at 100, Paxos finishes at 102, and the acknowledgement is at 114 — so the shaded block runs 102 → 114, twelve milliseconds. It does not begin at 100 because the wait did begin at 100; for its first two milliseconds it was hidden underneath the Paxos round trip that had to happen anyway. Only the part that outlasts replication costs anything, which is why the paper's measured figure is about 5 ms rather than 2ε̄ ≈ 8 ms.

2.3 t_safe_TM is the smaller. It sits at 103.999, one tick below 104, which is the prepare timestamp Southgate's leader chose when it logged its prepare record at real time 100 — taken, like every other timestamp, from TT.now().latest. t_safe_paxos meanwhile is up around real time minus 4, far above it. The group is readable only up to 103.999 until the coordinator decides.

2.4 s_read = 115. When it arrives at 111 the Singapore replica has applied nothing at all — T1 committed at real time 108 and, with 45 ms of lag, does not reach Singapore until 153. The read answers at 164, having waited 53 ms, and it returns T1. It never returned a stale answer; it converted staleness into latency.

2.5 It joins the acknowledgement (the right-hand end of the bar, a real-time event) to the commit timestamp (the diamond, a number the system assigned). Its length is the distance between when a transaction actually finished and where the database says it sits in history. When that dotted line points left, the timestamp is in the past and everything is fine; when it points right, the stamp is still in the future for somebody, and that is precisely the window commit wait exists to close.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 With ε(Northgate) = 20: s₁ = 120, T1's acknowledgement moves to 140, of which 38 ms is commit wait beyond replication. T2 then gets s₂ = 143 and the verdict stays holding — zero violations. This is the lesson: ε does not appear anywhere in the correctness argument. It appears only in the bill. Note also that the timeline stretches to fit; a twenty-millisecond ε makes the whole story roughly four times longer.

3.2 The read reports the mortgage without the sale at exactly snapTs = 105 and 106 — the half-open interval [s₂, s₁) = [105, 107). Below 105 it sees neither document; at 107 and above it sees both, in the wrong order but without a visible tear. Two integer values out of a 45 ms window is why this bug does not reproduce in your test environment.

3.3 Raising ε to 6 while leaving the offset at +6 makes the clock honest (|6| ≤ 6), not accurate — it is still running six milliseconds fast. s₁ becomes 112, the acknowledgement moves to 112, s₂ becomes 115, and the verdict returns to holding. TrueTime never asked the clock to be right. It asked the clock to be right about how wrong it is.

3.4 Both changes give the same answer: the read answers at 119, having waited 8 ms, down from 53. That is the surprise worth sitting with. Removing the lag, or going to the leader, removes the replication component of the wait — but a strong read still asks for "now", "now" is TT.now().latest, and that is ε ahead of any write that has actually happened. The residual 8 ms is the price of the question, not of the topology. It is exactly why §10.7's LastTS() shortcut exists: for a single-group read the leader can answer with the timestamp of its last commit instead, which is already safe and never waits.

3.5 The lease expires at 151 (killed at 111, 40 ms compressed lease), so the earliest the write can be stamped is 151; it receives s = 155 and is acknowledged at 159, from a new leader in Southgate. The invariant forcing it is disjointness: no two leaders of a group may hold overlapping lease intervals, and a leader may only assign timestamps inside its own lease. Everyone can see the old leader is dead; the rule does not care, because "everyone can see" is not something a distributed system can act on.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 The violation disappears — at ε = 7/7, 3/3 and 12/12 the simulator reports zero ordering violations with commit wait off. The condition derived in 5.2 says why: inversion needs g < ε₁ − ε₂ − P, and equal ε makes the right-hand side negative.

It is not a fix, for two reasons. First, ε is not a setting. It is a report: how long since this machine last polled a time master, plus how fast its oscillator is allowed to drift, plus the network delay to the master. In production it is a sawtooth climbing from about 1 ms to about 7 ms over each 30-second poll interval, and every machine is at a different point on its own sawtooth at every instant. Second, even if you could force them equal, doing so would require synchronising the machines — which is the original problem. Safety here was restored by coincidence, and a guarantee that holds by coincidence is the most dangerous kind, because it is indistinguishable from a correct one until the weather changes. Your linearizability and hybrid-logical-clocks labs both measured that same trap.

4.2 On the fixed cadence the read in scenario 7 answers at 200 instead of 164 — a wait of 89 ms rather than 53. Nothing incorrect happens; the answer is identical. The loss is entirely to liveness. In production this is the 8-second MinNextTS cadence, and it is why a strong read against a Paxos group that nobody has written to for a while can sit there for seconds: t_safe_paxos is the timestamp of the highest applied write, and an idle group has no new writes to raise it.

4.3 Line one — s₁ < t_abs(commit₁) — is exactly the line commit wait supplies, and TT.after(TT.now().earliest) becomes true essentially immediately, so no waiting occurs and the line fails. The run it permits is scenario 1 verbatim: s₁ = 107, acknowledged at 102, s₂ = 105.

4.4 Choosing s = TT.now().earliest breaks a different line — line four, t_abs(server₂) ≤ s₂, which is what guarantees a transaction's stamp is not in its own past. Concretely, with ε = 7 at Northgate and ε = 1 at Southgate:

  • T₀ reaches Southgate's leader at 94.000. TT.now() = [93, 95], so s₀ = earliest = 93. It is acknowledged at 96.
  • T₁ reaches Northgate's leader at 100.000, well after T₀ finished. TT.now() = [93, 107], so s₁ = earliest = 93 — the same stamp as a transaction that finished four milliseconds ago, and below anything Southgate assigns next.

Commit wait cannot save this, because commit wait only proves the stamp is in the past; a stamp that is too far in the past satisfies it trivially. Both rules are needed, and they defend different lines.

5.2 Let a be T₁'s arrival at its leader, P the Paxos latency, ε₁ and ε₂ the two leaders' error bounds, and g the gap between T₁'s acknowledgement and T₂'s arrival. With commit wait off:

  s₁ = a + ε₁
  ack₁ = a + P
  T₂ arrives at a + P + g   →   s₂ = a + P + g + ε₂

  inversion  ⇔  s₂ < s₁  ⇔  P + g + ε₂ < ε₁  ⇔  g < ε₁ − ε₂ − P

Applied to scenario 1: g < 7 − 1 − 2 = 4. The actual gap is 2, so the ordering inverts — which is what the simulator shows.

Three things fall out of that one inequality, and they are the reason this bug has the reputation it has. (i) Only the difference in the two admissions matters, not their size — two machines both admitting 20 ms are safer than one admitting 7 and one admitting 1. (ii) Paxos latency subtracts from the window, so making replication faster makes this bug more likely. (iii) The window is a handful of milliseconds wide, so the transactions have to be close together in time to hit it — which happens constantly at ten thousand operations per second and never in a test.

5.3 (a) t_safe_paxos = 180 (the highest applied write). t_safe_TM = 149.999 (one tick below the single prepare timestamp). t_safe = min(...) = 149.999. (b) Reads at 130 and 149 proceed. Reads at 160 and 179 block — even though the writes at 140 and 180 are applied and sitting right there, and even if the read touches keys the prepared transaction never mentions. Until the coordinator decides, that transaction could still commit anywhere at or above 150. (c) After it commits at 190: t_safe_paxos = 190, t_safe_TM = ∞, t_safe = 190. Both blocked reads unblock immediately, and now see the transaction if their timestamp is at or above 190.

The refinement that makes this survivable in production is fine-grained safe time: prepared timestamps are kept per key range in the lock table, and a read is checked only against the ranges it actually touches.

5.4 (a) 1100 — the moment L1's lease interval ends. Not 1050, when L1 died; death is not an event the invariant is defined over. (b) 1100. Two constraints bind: a leader may only assign timestamps inside its own lease, and monotonicity requires beating s_max = 1098. The lease constraint is the tighter one, and it implies the other — which is the elegance of the design. (c) If L2 could start at 1051 and assign 1055, the group's history reads:

  write A   timestamp 1098   committed at real time ≈ 1049
  write B   timestamp 1055   committed at real time ≈ 1051   ← later, smaller stamp

A snapshot read at 1056 then returns B and not A, although A finished before B began. The invariant broken is monotonicity within a Paxos group, and with it external consistency. Note that this needs no partition and no split brain in the usual sense — it is caused purely by timestamps from two eras overlapping.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 "Skip commit wait for single-group transactions."

The claim is half right, and the half that is right is what makes it dangerous. Within one Paxos group, monotonicity does hold without commit wait: the leader forces every new timestamp above s_max, so two transactions on the same group can never invert. Test it that way and it passes.

It fails across groups, which is the case the proposal never mentions:

  • T₁ on G1 (Northgate, ε = 7) arrives at 100.000, gets s₁ = 107, and — commit wait skipped — is acknowledged at 102.
  • The client telephones. T₂ on G2 (Southgate, ε = 1) arrives at 104.000 and gets s₂ = 105.

s₂ < s₁, and a snapshot read at 106 sees the second without the first. The violated guarantee is external consistency.

The general lesson is worth keeping: monotonicity is what orders transactions inside a group; commit wait is what orders them across groups. They are not alternatives, and since groups never communicate, the second cannot be delegated to the first.

6.2 "A replica may serve a read at t once it has applied everything up to t."

  • G1 has applied everything up to 204. A transaction is prepared at prepare timestamp 200, undecided.
  • A read arrives at timestamp 203. Ignoring prepared transactions, it answers now, reporting the state without that transaction.
  • The coordinator then commits it at 202 — legal, since 202 ≥ 200.
  • A second, identical read at timestamp 203 now returns the state with it.

Two reads at the same timestamp returned different histories. The violated guarantee is the immutability of a snapshot — the property that makes consistent backups, consistent analytics and repeatable reads possible at all. And note the shape of the failure: nothing was lost, nothing was late, and neither read was individually detectable as wrong.

6.3 "A read-only transaction can use its own machine's wall clock."

Commit wait guarantees the commit timestamp is in the past of true time. It says nothing about any particular machine's reading of the time. A reader whose clock runs slow picks a read timestamp below the commit timestamp of a transaction that was acknowledged before the read even began, and misses it — which is precisely the 86-out-of-86 stale reads your hybrid-logical-clocks lab measured. Concretely: T₁ acknowledged at real 114 with s₁ = 107; a reader whose clock is 5 ms slow starts at 116 and picks 111; 111 < 107 is false, so it happens to see it — but at ε = 7 on the writer and −5 on the reader, a s₁ = 121 case misses cleanly.

The fix is not exotic: the read timestamp must come from TT.now().latest too, or — better, for a single-group read — from the group leader's LastTS(). Both are statements the system is willing to stand behind. A bare wall clock is not.

6.4 "Leases exist because of partitions; inside one datacenter a new leader can take over immediately."

No partition is required. A stop-the-world garbage collection pause, a stalled disk, or a descheduled VM is enough, and all three are ordinary events inside a single datacenter.

  • L1 holds a lease to 1100 and has assigned s_max = 1098.
  • At 1050 L1 pauses. The others see silence.
  • Under the proposal, L2 takes over at 1051 and assigns 1055 to a new write.
  • At 1054 L1 wakes, still believing — correctly, by its own lease — that it may assign timestamps up to 1100.

The overlap is the bug, and it exists whether or not L1's writes still reach a quorum, because the damage is to the timestamp ranges, not to the log. This is your split-brain lab's conclusion in its exact form: you cannot stop a machine believing it is in charge, you can only arrange that its effects do not count — and here the effect that must not count is a stamp from the next leader's era.

6.5 "Use the midpoint; it halves the wait."

It does halve the wait, and it breaks line four of the proof — t_abs(server₂) ≤ s₂. The midpoint is the clock's reading, which for a slow clock is in the past of the true instant, so a transaction can receive a stamp earlier than the moment it actually started.

  • Northgate: offset +5, ε = 7. T₁ arrives at 100 → midpoint s₁ = 105. Commit wait until earliest > 105, i.e. t + 5 − 7 > 105 → 107. Acknowledged 107.
  • Southgate: offset −6, ε = 7 (honest: |−6| ≤ 7). T₂ arrives at 109 → midpoint s₂ = 103.

s₂ = 103 < s₁ = 105, and T₂ started two milliseconds after T₁ finished. External consistency violated, with commit wait fully enabled and both clocks honest.

The moral: latest is not a conservative flourish that can be tuned. It is the second of the two rules, and it is what makes a stamp an upper bound on the moment the transaction began.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 The lost acknowledgement.

(a) Spanner guarantees the transaction either committed entirely or not at all, and that the outcome is durable and knowable — the decision was logged through Paxos, so a coordinator crash does not lose it. What Spanner does not give you is any notion of "this is the same request as the one before". The retry is a new transaction.

(b) A read-modify-write is naturally harmless: the retried transaction re-reads current state and recomputes, so applying it twice is the same as applying it once. UPDATE Accounts SET Balance = Balance − 50 is not in that class if it is issued blind; SET Balance = <value computed from a fresh read> is. The unsafe shapes are blind deltas, and inserts with a server-generated key — the retry cheerfully creates a second row.

(c) Add an idempotency key, and — this is the part people get wrong — write it inside the same transaction as the effect. A unique row keyed by the client's request identifier, inserted alongside the balance update, means the retry's insert collides and the transaction can detect that its own earlier attempt already succeeded. Put it in a separate transaction and you have reintroduced the two-writes problem the database was solving for you. This is your idempotency lab's rule, and the reason your transactional-outbox lab insists on the same single-transaction discipline.

The tell that separates them in a glance: an ε incident moves writes everywhere; a hot split moves one key range and leaves CPU pinned on a fraction of your nodes while the rest idle; a stuck prepared transaction moves reads.

The one that is not a Spanner problem is the hot split. Spanner is doing exactly what it was asked to do; the primary key is wrong. It is also, by a distance, the most common real incident — which is worth saying out loud, because it means most Spanner performance work is schema work.

7.3 Strong reads in a read-only region.

Read-only replicas hold full data and serve reads but do not vote, so they are never leaders. t_safe is computable locally — t_safe_paxos from the writes they have applied, t_safe_TM from prepare metadata carried on the log — so the serving half is fine.

The problem is the other half: choosing the timestamp. s_read = TT.now().latest can be produced locally, but then the read must block until t_safe reaches it, and t_safe in a distant read-only region trails by the wide-area replication delay. The cheaper choice, LastTS(), is only known at the leader — so a genuinely strong, non-blocking read from that region costs a round trip to the leader region and is bounded below by that latency, exactly as if the replica were not there.

What to offer the application instead: bounded-stale reads. Name a staleness window, and Spanner picks the newest timestamp inside it that the local replica can serve without blocking — no leader contact, no wait. Google's guidance is that a window under about 10 seconds does not buy you anything, because it is not comfortably larger than the replication delay you are trying to hide. So the honest offer to the product team is: "strong reads cost you a transatlantic round trip; ten-second-stale reads are local and free; which does this screen actually need?" — and for most screens, the answer is the second.

Rung 8 — Design

These are arguments, not answers. What follows is one defensible version of each.

8.1 The ledger.

Keys. Accounts keyed by (AccountId) with AccountId a UUIDv4 — never a sequence, never a timestamp. Entries (the immutable double-entry lines) keyed by (AccountId, EntryId) and interleaved in Accounts, so an account and its recent entries live in one split and one Paxos group.

Why interleaving is the load-bearing decision. The hard rule — never show a debit without its credit — is a cross-account invariant, and the two accounts in a transfer are on different splits. That is a two-participant transaction, which the paper's Table 4 prices at about 24.5 ms mean versus 17.0 ms for one participant. Acceptable. What is not acceptable is a design where a transfer touches fifty splits because balances, entries and audit rows were all keyed independently. Interleaving is how you keep the participant count at two.

Instance configuration. Multi-region, for the 99.999% SLA and regional-failure survival, with the leader region placed where the writes originate — not where the company is headquartered. Writes always go to the leader first even when a closer replica exists, so leader placement is the single biggest latency lever you have. Add read-only replicas in the other two regions: they scale reads without enlarging the write quorum.

Read paths. Strong read-only transactions for anything in the money path — a balance check before authorising, a post-transfer confirmation screen. Bounded-stale reads (15–30 s) for statements, dashboards, exports and analytics; these are the bulk of traffic by volume and none of it by risk, and moving them off the leader is what keeps the leader region under its CPU target.

Monitor. (1) CPU against the 45% multi-region target, because that headroom is the failover plan. (2) Transaction abort rate — rising aborts with flat CPU means contention, which means the schema, which means a hot account. (3) Key Visualizer, for the hot split you will eventually introduce.

What to give up, deliberately. Cross-account uniqueness constraints enforced in the database for anything other than the account key itself, and any "global counter" of any kind. Both are single-key hotspots wearing a business-logic costume.

What survives from this lab, and what does not. All of the mechanism survives — you get external consistency without doing anything. What does not survive is the simplification that transactions are cheap: at 20,000 writes/second across an 80 ms triangle, the Paxos quorum latency dominates ε entirely, and commit wait's ~5 ms is noise. The lab's central cost is not your central cost, and knowing which one dominates your workload is the actual skill.

8.2 No atomic clocks, ε ≈ 250 ms.

Writer pays (Spanner's choice). Every write acknowledgement gains roughly 500 ms. For a 95%-read workload that is 5% of requests becoming unusably slow, and locks are held throughout, so the remaining 95% queue behind them. Rejected — not because it is incorrect, but because the constant is 60× wrong.

Reader pays (CockroachDB's choice). Reads whose snapshot lands inside the 250 ms uncertainty window restart. The rate depends on how often a read lands within 250 ms of a write to the same keys — low for cold data, brutal for a hot row. Cost is statistical rather than fixed, which is harder to reason about but far cheaper on average for a read-heavy workload. Recommended, with the caveat that you must monitor the restart rate as a first-class metric and treat a rising one as a schema problem.

Client pays (MongoDB's choice). Cheapest of all and the weakest: it only orders operations for clients that carry the token. Your 95% reads include analytics jobs, other services and support tools that have no session. Rejected as the primary mechanism; worth adding on top, since it is nearly free.

The thing to say out loud: all three pay the same ε. The engineering question is never "how do I avoid the cost", it is "which of my three populations can absorb it", and for a read-heavy workload the answer is the writers — except that Spanner's design is the one that doesn't charge readers, so the recommendation inverts precisely because ε is large.

8.3 The counter-proposal.

Concede the strong point first, because it is strong: Spanner is expensive, and most data does not need external consistency. A product catalogue, a session store, a notification feed, an audit log — none of these are worth a globally distributed transactional database, and putting them there is a real and common waste.

The question that decides it: "Which invariants must never be seen broken, by whom, and on how many devices?"

Not "must never be broken" — a saga gets you that eventually, with compensations. The word doing the work is seen. If the answer is "a customer must never see a debit without its credit, on any device, at any instant", then you need atomic visibility across shards, which your distributed-transactions lab measured a well-built 2PC-plus-dual-write failing at 16 fractured audits in 40 runs with zero crashes. A saga does not fix that; a saga chooses it and adds compensations. If the answer is "eventually consistent is fine, we reconcile overnight", then they are right and you should agree quickly.

The honest middle: split the estate. Ledger and balances on Spanner; everything else on cheaper storage. The decision ladder from your distributed-transactions lab still holds — one database > co-locate the keys on one shard > outbox > saga > distributed transaction — and Spanner is the last rung, reserved for invariants that must never be seen broken.

8.4 The uncomfortable paragraph.

Spanner's external-consistency guarantee is conditional. It holds as long as every machine's clock stays inside the error bound that machine publishes — in practice, as long as local oscillators drift no faster than 200 µs/s. This is not a theoretical caveat that software can close: if a clock's interval stops containing the true instant, commit wait waits for the wrong number and the ordering guarantee fails silently, with no error, no exception and no log line. Google's defences are real and layered — time masters cross-check and evict themselves, daemons poll several independent sources and discard outliers, and machines whose measured frequency excursion exceeds spec remove themselves from the fleet — and the published justification is empirical rather than formal: bad CPUs are roughly six times more likely than bad clocks, so TrueTime is about as trustworthy as anything else we already depend on. That is a reasonable basis for a decision. It is not a proof, and we should not write it down as one.

The operational control to insist on: an alert on TrueTime ε itself, treated as a correctness signal and not a performance one. Every other layer of the defence lives inside Google's infrastructure and is invisible to you; ε is the one number the platform exposes that moves when the assumption is under strain. A latency incident with no CPU, no disk and no network anomaly, but a raised ε, is a time incident — and knowing that in advance is the difference between a twenty-minute diagnosis and a four-hour one.
