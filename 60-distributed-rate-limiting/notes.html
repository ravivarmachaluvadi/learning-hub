Lab 60 · Running It In Production

Distributed Rate Limiting — one promise, twenty people enforcing it

Tell a friend: An office gives every employee one free breakfast plate an hour, and Arjun behind the counter enforces it perfectly with a notebook. Then the office opens twenty counters, and Meera walks down the line taking a plate from every one of them. Nobody broke a rule. The rule simply stopped being enforceable the moment there was more than one clerk. Your rate limiter is Arjun: exactly right on one machine, and quietly letting through twenty times the limit on twenty. The fix is not a cleverer counting algorithm. It is deciding how out of date each clerk's view of the shared register book is allowed to be, and this lab prices every point on that dial.

Run the twenty counters yourself in visualization.html: pick a coordination design, kill the store, watch the scoreboard. What this lab is, what it is not, and where every number came from is stage 19.

BEGINNER MODEL — stages 1 to 9

1. What goes wrong if nobody builds this?

In one breath: Sending Asha a request costs a stranger nothing. Serving it costs Asha real money. So one stranger with a broken loop can spend everything she has, and the customers who did nothing wrong get nothing back.

Asha runs an online payments service. Anyone on the internet can send it requests. Sending one costs the sender almost nothing. Serving one costs Asha a database query, a call out to the card network, and a slice of a CPU.

That asymmetry is the whole problem.

One caller with a bad while loop. One customer's misconfigured retry. One person trying to guess passwords. Any of the three, and Asha's service spends all of its capacity on them. Everyone else gets slow responses or errors, and everyone else is the customers who were behaving perfectly.

Asha did not get attacked. She got crowded out.

A rate limiter is the thing that says "you, specifically, have had enough for now" so that everyone else keeps working. Without one, four things happen, and none of them look like a security incident on the dashboard.

What Asha loses What it looks like on an ordinary Tuesday
The service itself One bad client, and every customer sees timeouts
The budget The cloud bill tracks abuse instead of business
Fairness A paying customer's traffic is starved by a free one
A product She cannot sell "10,000 calls a month" because she cannot enforce it

That last row is the one people miss. A limit you cannot enforce is not a weaker promise. It is not a promise at all, so it cannot be sold, written into a contract, or defended when a customer complains.

2. Why does one plate an hour become twenty?

In one breath: A rule that one person can enforce with one notebook stops being a rule the moment two people are enforcing it from two notebooks.

A large office gives every employee one free breakfast plate per hour.

At the start there is one counter. Arjun stands behind it with a notebook. Someone walks up, Arjun looks up their name, sees whether they already took a plate this hour, and either hands one over and writes it down, or says "come back later".

This works perfectly. Arjun's notebook is the truth. Arjun is the only person who can change it. Nobody gets two plates.

Then the office grows to 5,000 people. The queue at Arjun's counter is now forty minutes long. So the office does the obvious thing. It opens twenty counters, with twenty clerks, and puts Ben on the door to send each arriving person to whichever counter is free.

Meera works out what has happened before anyone else does. She takes a plate from Arjun. Then she walks to Bela's counter and takes another. Then a third from the counter next to Bela's. Bela has no idea. Bela's notebook has never heard of Meera.

🔮 Predict: Twenty counters, twenty clerks, every clerk following the one-plate rule exactly. How many plates can Meera walk out with in one hour?

Reveal

Twenty. One per counter, because each notebook is a separate memory of who has eaten.

If you said one, you assumed the rule lives in the office. It does not. It lives in Arjun's notebook, and there are now twenty notebooks that have never spoken to each other.

If you said "somewhere between one and twenty, depending on luck", you are closer. You have also found the thing that makes this hard. The number depends on how Ben spreads people across counters, and stage 4 turns that into the second, nastier failure.

Twenty counters means twenty plates per hour per person, not one.

Nobody wrote a bug. Every clerk followed the rule exactly. The rule itself stopped being enforceable the moment there was more than one clerk. Hold that sentence; it is the thesis of the whole lab.

3. What does every engineer build first?

In one breath: You keep the count in a variable inside your program. It is fast, it is correct, and it is correct for exactly as long as there is one program.

The naive solution keeps the counter in the application, as a hash map from customer to a count, in memory, in the service process. It deserves that name kindly, because it is the right first move:

if (counts.get(customerId) >= 100) return TOO_MANY_REQUESTS;
counts.increment(customerId);

This is Arjun with his notebook, written in Java. No network call. Nanoseconds. No extra infrastructure to run, pay for, or page anybody about.

It is genuinely correct and genuinely fast. It is the right answer for exactly as long as there is one process.

4. What breaks the moment there are twenty copies?

In one breath: You configured one hundred requests a minute. Your customers get two thousand, and the number changes every time the cluster grows.

Asha is running for real, so she runs 20 instances behind Ben the load balancer. The limit she configured is 100 requests per minute. The limit her customers actually experience is 2,000 per minute, because each instance enforces 100 independently and Ben spreads each customer's traffic across all of them.

That is the visible failure. Here is the deeper one:

The limit is a property of the whole system, but the state that enforces it lives inside one process.

And it gets worse in ways that are not just "off by 20×". Read the middle column as the thing Asha would actually see happen.

What happens What Asha sees Why it is worse than the 20×
Autoscaling takes 20 instances to 60 at peak The effective limit silently triples It triples at exactly the moment she most needed it to hold
A rolling deploy restarts every instance In-memory counters gone, everyone gets a fresh 100 An abuser only has to wait for her next release
Sales asks for "100 requests/minute" in a contract She cannot write it down honestly The real number depends on deployment topology

🔮 Predict: The obvious fix is to give each of the 20 instances a limit of 5, so 20 × 5 = 100. Does that work?

Reveal

The ceiling becomes correct, and that is real progress — you will never exceed 100 again. But you have created the opposite error.

Ben does not send each customer's traffic evenly to all 20 instances. Priya sends 60 requests a minute, and 40 of them happen to land on instance 7. Instance 7 allows 5 and rejects 35, while the other 19 instances sit there holding 95 unused allowance with Priya's name on it.

So Priya is told "limit exceeded" at 5% of the limit she was sold. You have traded over-admission (letting too many through) for under-admission (rejecting traffic you promised to serve).

Under-admission is often the worse bug. Over-admission shows up as a capacity problem you can see on a graph. Under-admission shows up as a support ticket from a customer who is being lied to.

If you said "yes, that works", you were reasoning about the ceiling only. The ceiling is half the promise. The other half is that Priya gets what she paid for.

Hold onto this pair. Meera getting twenty plates and Priya being turned away with plates going spare are the two ends of the axis the entire design lives on.

5. What are we actually asking for, in plain words?

In one breath: Many machines must keep one promise, on every request, fast enough that nobody notices, without becoming the thing that breaks.

Say it in plain words, because everything after this is the pursuit of it:

Many independent machines must enforce one shared promise, on every single request, fast enough that nobody notices, and the enforcement must not itself become the thing that breaks.

Four clauses, and five sources of difficulty, because the last clause carries two. Read the right-hand column as "why you cannot just do the obvious thing".

Clause Why it is hard
many independent machines The state is shared, so somebody has to coordinate
one shared promise Coordination has to be correct, not approximately correct
on every single request Whatever you build runs 100% of the time, at full traffic
fast enough It sits in front of the user; every millisecond is added to every call
must not become the thing that breaks It is now a dependency of everything. If it dies, does your API die with it?

Notice the tension. Clause 2 pulls one way and clauses 3, 4 and 5 pull the other. Perfect coordination means Arjun walks to the shared book on every single plate. Walking on every plate is slow, expensive, and a new way for the whole counter to stop.

6. What is the one idea the whole design turns on?

In one breath: The place that decides and the place that knows cannot be the same place. So every decision is made from a slightly old copy of the truth, and how old is a number you choose.

Separate the place where the decision is made from the place where the truth lives — and then choose, deliberately, how out-of-date each decision point is allowed to be.

That second half is the part people miss. There are many places where a decision happens: Arjun's counter, Bela's counter, eighteen more. There is one logical truth. They cannot be in the same place, so every clerk is working from a slightly old copy.

How old you let that copy get is a dial, not a bug. Turn it to zero and you get perfect enforcement at maximum cost and maximum fragility. Turn it up and you get cheap, fast, resilient enforcement that is slightly wrong.

Distributed rate limiting at scale is choosing where on that dial to sit — per limit, not per system. Asha's billing limit and her abuse limit will sit at different points on the same dial, in the same cluster, on the same afternoon.

7. Which words are being used as if they were one?

In one breath: Half a dozen different mechanisms all end with somebody being told no. They are not interchangeable, and knowing which is which is most of the design.

This is the terminology stage, and it is deliberately not a dictionary. Each word below is a thing that has already happened to Arjun, Meera, Priya or Ben. The precise definitions are collected again in the glossary at stage 18.

Which four words all end with somebody being told no?

A rate limit is a cap on how often someone may do something. Precisely: a maximum number of accepted operations per identity per unit of time. Asha's promise is that merchant_42 may call POST /payments 100 times per minute, every minute, forever.

The word exists to separate a cap on rate from a cap on total. A cap on the total is a quota: 100 ever, or 100 GB this month, and when it is gone it is gone. A rate refills. That difference decides whether an exhausted customer waits sixty seconds or opens a support ticket.

Throttling means slowing someone down rather than refusing them. Precisely: delaying or queueing the excess instead of rejecting it. The word exists because "rate limit" is casually used for both behaviours, and they fail in opposite ways. Rejecting is instant and pushes the problem to the client. Delaying keeps the request and spends your memory and the client's patience instead. Reject now, or accept and delay: that comes back as a real architectural choice in 10.6.

Load shedding is dropping requests because you are struggling, whoever sent them. Precisely: rejecting traffic based on server health rather than on caller identity. Your load-shedding lab is Ben looking over his shoulder at the waiting room; a rate limiter is Ben checking a list. A rate limiter protects fairness between callers and is configured in advance. Load shedding protects survival and reacts to this second. You need both. A rate limiter alone will happily let 10,000 well-behaved customers, each inside their limit, collectively melt Asha's database.

Word The question it asks Where you have met it
Rate limit are you over your allowance for this minute? stage 2, Arjun's notebook
Quota have you used up your total for the period? this section, and stage 18
Throttling how do I slow you down instead of refusing you? 10.6, policer versus shaper
Load shedding can I finish what I already have? 12, the thing this design cannot do

Who decides, and where does the truth live?

An enforcement point is the machine that actually says yes or no. Precisely: the component in the request path that evaluates the limit and can reject. Arjun is one. Bela is another. Each of Asha's 20 API gateway pods is one. The word exists because in a distributed limiter there are many of these and only one source of truth, and confusing the two is the central error of the whole subject.

The counter store, also called the decision store, is the shared place that remembers how much everyone has used. Precisely: the datastore holding per-key limiter state, readable and writable by every enforcement point. In the office it is the register book. In Asha's system it is a Redis cluster holding merchant_42:payments → {tokens: 37.2, lastRefill: …}.

A key, also called a limit dimension, is who the limit applies to. Precisely: the string identifying one independent bucket of allowance. In the office it is a person's name. The counting itself is trivial; almost all the design judgment is in choosing this string. merchant_42 is one key, merchant_42:POST /payments is a different and narrower key, and both can exist at once with different limits. This gets its own stage: 10.5.

What are the two opposite ways to be wrong?

Over-admission is letting more through than you promised. Precisely: the true number accepted in some window exceeds the configured limit. It is Meera with twenty plates. Limit 100/min, 20 uncoordinated nodes, actual 2,000/min, so 20× over-admission.

Under-admission is rejecting traffic you promised to serve. Precisely: rejecting a request while the global limit had unused allowance. It is Priya turned away at instance 7 while nineteen other instances hold her unused 95.

Both words exist because they are different failures with different victims. Over-admission hurts Asha, in capacity and cost. Under-admission hurts Asha's customer, by breaking a promise. Every coordination design in stage 10 trades one against the other, and you cannot have neither.

Which two dials does the rest of the lab turn?

Fail-open or fail-closed is what an enforcement point does when the counter store is unreachable: let everyone in, or nobody. Kiran has gone home sick and the clerks must still serve breakfast. The term exists because there is no safe default. Fail-open on a limit that guards Asha's database turns a Redis blip into a stampede. Fail-closed on a limit that guards a nice-to-have turns the same blip into a total API outage.

A hot key is one entry that everyone hammers. Precisely: a single key whose request rate exceeds what one store partition can serve, and a store partition is one CPU core. Dev is Asha's biggest merchant and does 8,000 rps by himself, and every one of those requests touches the one entry merchant_42. Sharding a datastore spreads keys across machines, so it does nothing at all for a single key. Stage 11's F3 is this failure with numbers on it.

The staleness budget is how out-of-date a node's view is allowed to be. Precisely: the maximum age of the shared state a decision may be based on. This is the dial from stage 6, and naming it as an explicit number is the single most useful thing you can do in a design review for this system.

Term The one-line version Where the thing happened
Enforcement point the machine that says yes or no stage 2, Arjun and Bela
Counter store the shared book everyone reads stage 8, Kiran in the middle of the room
Key which bucket of allowance you spend from 10.5
Over-admission more got through than you promised stage 2, Meera's twenty plates
Under-admission you refused someone who had allowance left stage 4's reveal, Priya at instance 7
Staleness budget how old a decision's information may be stage 6, and 10.7 makes it a number

8. Which two pictures should you carry?

In one breath: One picture is twenty clerks and one register book in the middle of the room. The other is the same thing with three kinds of wire drawn separately, because those wires have completely different jobs.

Carry both mental models. The first one you can hold in your head during an argument. The second one tells you which wire to change.

What does the simple picture look like?

The office breakfast counter, grown up.

Twenty clerks, each at their own counter, each able to hand out a plate. In the middle of the room Kiran sits with the one register book that records what everyone has taken. Every clerk can walk to Kiran. Walking takes time, and while Arjun is walking, the other nineteen are still serving people.

Every design in this document is an answer to one question: how often does a clerk walk to Kiran, and what do they do while they are not there?

Three planes live in that picture, and they are worth naming separately because they have completely different requirements.

The data plane is client to gateway to backend. It carries every request, microseconds matter, and it must never be down.

The coordination plane is gateway to counter store: the dotted lines to Kiran. This is the entire subject of this lesson. How much of it exists at all is your design.

The control plane is rules to gateways: which customer has which limit. It changes rarely, is read constantly, must be cached locally, and must survive its own outage. Omar owns it, and 10.10 is his stage.

Where does the story lie to you?

Four places. Each lie is a real design consideration that the office hides.

Walking is slow but reliable; networks fail silently. Arjun always comes back from Kiran's desk. A store call can hang for 30 seconds and then time out, which is far worse than a fast failure, because the request thread was held the whole time. In the office, coordination has a cost. In reality it has a cost and a failure mode, and the failure mode is what wakes Asha at 3am. Stage 11, F4 — and F2 is the one that actually takes her down.

Kiran is one person; Redis is one process per shard. That is nearly the same thing, for a different reason. The office makes the bottleneck look like a queue at the book. The real bottleneck is that a single key lives on a single core, so adding machines to a Redis cluster does nothing at all for Dev's flash sale. Stage 11, F3.

Everyone in the office shares one clock on the wall. Servers do not. Two nodes can disagree about what time it is by tens of milliseconds, or, when NTP misbehaves, by minutes. Every window-based limit depends on nodes agreeing where the window boundary is. Stage 11, F5.

People arriving at counters are independent. Requests are not. When Asha's backend slows down, clients time out and retry, so rejected traffic comes straight back. The office has no equivalent of a rejected person instantly rejoining the queue and bringing a friend. This is why the limiter's response format, Retry-After included, is a load-bearing part of the design rather than a nicety. Stage 11, F7.

INTERMEDIATE MODEL — stage 10

10. How would you actually design this?

In one breath: Pin what it must do, work out how big it really is, and decide where it physically sits. Then spend almost all your time on the one hard part, which is how often a clerk walks to Kiran.

We will build it the way Asha would in a design review. Requirements first, then the size of the thing, then its physical position, and then coordination, which is where the lesson actually lives.

10.1 What must it do, and how well?

A worked example makes every later number concrete. Ours is Asha's payments API.

Five things it must do:

  1. Reject requests that exceed a configured limit, with 429 Too Many Requests.
  2. Let Omar configure limits per identity, per endpoint group, and per plan tier (free / standard / enterprise), taking effect within about a minute of being changed.
  3. Apply several limits to one request at once (per-merchant AND per-endpoint AND per-IP) and reject if any of them is exceeded.
  4. Tell a rejected client when to come back (Retry-After), and let a succeeding client see how much it has left (X-RateLimit-Remaining).
  5. Charge expensive endpoints more. A bulk report is not one unit of work, so limits are spent in weighted cost, not raw request count.

Now the numbers Asha has to commit to, which is where the design is actually decided. The third row is the one people forget to ask about.

Requirement Target Why this number
Added latency p99 < 5 ms The API's own p99 budget is 150 ms; the limiter runs on every call and must be invisible
Availability higher than the service it protects It is in the path of 100% of traffic. If it is less available than the API, it is the API's availability
Accuracy exact for billing limits, ±5% acceptable for abuse limits Different limits genuinely deserve different answers — see 10.7
Throughput 40,000 rps peak Derived below
Scale-out Adding gateway nodes must not change the effective limit The stage-4 failure, stated as a requirement

That accuracy row unlocks the whole design. "How accurate does this limit need to be?" is a product question, not an engineering one. Asha will get a different answer for Priya's billing limit than for Dev's abuse limit, in the same system, on the same day.

10.2 How big is this thing, really?

Rough numbers, done out loud, because they decide the architecture.

Start with traffic.

1 billion API calls/day
  ÷ 86,400 s              = ~11,600 rps average
  × 3.5 peak-to-average   = ~40,000 rps peak

Each request is checked against 3 limit dimensions (merchant, endpoint, IP), so the coordination plane sees three times the request rate:

40,000 rps × 3 dimensions = ~120,000 counter checks/sec at peak

Now state size, which is the surprise.

2,000,000 merchants total
  ~150,000 active in any peak minute
  × 3 dimensions          = ~450,000 live counters
  × ~120 bytes each       (token bucket = 2 numbers, plus key + store overhead)
                          = ~54 MB

Even if Asha keeps every merchant resident, 2M × 3 × 120 B is about 720 MB. That fits in one commodity machine's RAM with room to spare.

🔮 Predict: Which one forces Asha into a distributed store first: memory, or throughput?

Reveal

Throughput. Memory is never the constraint here, and this inverts how people instinctively design it.

If you said memory, you were reasoning by analogy with every other data store you have built, where "two million customers" means shard it. Here the entire working set is 54 MB, and the entire keyspace is 720 MB. One machine holds it.

The single most useful thing to notice in this whole document: memory is never the constraint. Throughput and latency are.

Asha is not building a big distributed database. She is building a very small, very hot one. That is why the answer is a Redis-shaped thing and not a Cassandra-shaped thing, and why every hard problem below is about access, not storage.

That conclusion decides the store. A single Redis shard is single-threaded and realistically serves ~50,000 scripted operations/sec, because an atomic limiter check is a small Lua script rather than a bare GET. So 120,000 checks/sec needs at least 3 shards for raw throughput, and Asha would run ~6 for headroom and failover.

Then the latency budget, which is the other half of the same story. Read the right-hand column against the 5 ms target from 10.1.

Hop Typical p99 Fits in a 5 ms budget?
Same-host (sidecar / in-process) ~0.05 ms Trivially
Same availability zone ~1–2 ms Yes, with room for one call
Cross-AZ ~5–10 ms No — already blows the budget
Cross-region ~70–150 ms Absolutely not

Two conclusions fall straight out of that table, and they are structural rather than a matter of taste.

One store call per request is affordable, but only one, and only in-AZ. Any design needing two round trips is dead on arrival, which quietly kills the most obvious design in 10.7.

Cross-region coordination on the hot path is impossible. Not slow. Impossible. This is why every global limit is really a set of regional limits. See 10.9.

10.3 Where does the limiter physically live?

Before any algorithm, decide the physical placement. It determines how many enforcement points exist, and therefore how bad the coordination problem is.

The second column is the one that matters: it is the number of Arjuns.

Placement Enforcement points Protects Fatal weakness
① Client SDK Every client Nothing you control The client is the attacker. Useful only as politeness — a well-behaved SDK backing off — never as enforcement
② Edge / CDN 100s of PoPs worldwide Everything, including your bandwidth bill Coordination across PoPs is cross-region → impossible on the hot path. Must be approximate
③ API gateway 10s of nodes, one region All services behind it Cannot see limits that depend on service-internal facts (e.g. "this merchant's balance is low")
④ Sidecar One per service instance Per-service limits, including service-to-service Most enforcement points of any option → the hardest coordination
⑤ Service library One per instance Just that service Duplicated in every service, in every language, redeployed to change

Asha's choice: ③ the API gateway, with ② at the edge for crude volumetric protection. Three reasons.

The gateway is already in the path, already terminates TLS, already authenticates. So it already knows who the caller is, which is exactly what a key needs. Doing the check anywhere earlier means doing it before you know the identity, which limits you to IP-based limits.

There are tens of gateway nodes, not hundreds. Twenty Arjuns is a hard coordination problem. Six hundred is an intractable one.

The edge tier is not trying to be accurate. Its job is to absorb the 10-Gbps garbage flood so it never reaches the gateway. Crude and approximate is the correct answer there.

That gives a general rule worth stating out loud: layer your limiters, and let each layer be as accurate as its position allows. Edge is approximate and huge. Gateway is accurate and per-customer. Service is precise and narrow.

        G-->>C: 201 + X-RateLimit-Remaining: 37
    else rejected
        G-->>C: 429 + Retry-After: 12
    end

Two details there are easy to skim past and expensive to get wrong.

The rules lookup is local. It is served from an in-memory cache on the gateway, and it must never be a network call. Omar's control plane, and why that cache must outlive its own source, is 10.10.

All three limits are checked in one call, not three. Three round trips would triple both the latency and the load on Kiran. Worse, it would leave a partial-consumption bug: if limits 1 and 2 pass and limit 3 fails, you have already spent quota from 1 and 2 on a request you then rejected. Check them together, consume only if all pass.

10.5 What is the key? (the most underrated decision)

In one breath: The counting is a hundred lines of code. The string you count against is where real systems go wrong, and almost nobody discusses it.

A key is the string identifying one independent allowance. In the office it is the name Arjun looks up. Here are the candidates, with the way each one fails.

Key Good for Fails at
IP address Anonymous endpoints, crude abuse control 50,000 users behind one corporate NAT share a limit; an attacker with a botnet has 50,000 keys. Both errors at once
API key / merchant ID Everything after authentication Requires the caller to be authenticated first — useless for login and signup endpoints
User ID Per-human fairness A single merchant's server-side integration is one API key and many users
API key + endpoint group Stopping one expensive endpoint from eating the whole allowance More keys, more counters, more configuration to explain
Global (one key for an endpoint) Protecting a specific fragile downstream It is a hot key by construction — every request touches it. See F3

Three rules follow, and each of them came from a real incident.

Limit on the most specific identity you can authenticate, and fall back outward. Authenticated merchant, then API key, then IP, in that order. Anything keyed on IP is both too coarse and too easy to evade, so use it only where you genuinely have nothing better. That means the unauthenticated endpoints, and nothing else.

Login endpoints need two keys at once. Key on IP, which stops one host trying many accounts, and on the target username, which stops a botnet trying one account. Either alone is trivially bypassed. This is the single most common rate-limiting design bug in production systems.

Charge by cost, not by count. "1,000 requests/minute" means something wildly different for GET /ping and POST /bulk-settlement. Give each endpoint a weight and let one request consume 20 units. Otherwise the limit does not correlate with the thing you were actually protecting.

🔮 Predict: Asha's limit is keyed on the API key. Dev's integration is deployed across 12 of his own servers, all sharing one API key. He complains that his traffic is being rejected even though "each server is well under the limit". Whose bug is this?

Reveal

Nobody's — it is a key granularity mismatch, and it is a product conversation, not an engineering one.

If you said "Dev's, he should shard his own traffic", you are technically right and commercially wrong. If you said "Asha's, the limiter is broken", look again: it is enforcing exactly what was sold.

The limit is per API key, which is exactly what Asha sold him. Dev's mental model is per server. The system is behaving as specified; the specification does not match how the customer deployed.

The available fixes are all product decisions, which is why this belongs in a design doc:

  • Let Dev mint sub-keys, one per server, each with its own allowance. Clean, and it makes key granularity match deployment granularity.
  • Raise the limit on that key and let him self-distribute. Simple, but Asha has now lost the ability to protect against his runaway server.
  • Charge by cost, so his cheap health-check traffic stops consuming the same allowance as his expensive calls.

The engineering lesson: the key is a contract with the customer about how they may deploy. Choose it knowing that, and document it. A limiter whose key granularity surprises the customer generates support load forever, no matter how correct the counting is.

10.6 Which counting algorithm?

In one breath: Six ways to count the same thing. They differ in how much they remember per person and in what they do at the edge of a window, and only one of those differences changes your architecture.

At this altitude these are six short stories rather than a lecture. Imagine each one as a different rule written on the front of Arjun's notebook. The runnable side-by-side is Appendix A if you want it.

Fixed window. Arjun counts plates per clock hour and resets on the hour. Meera learns the reset time, takes ten plates at 10:59 and ten more at 11:01, and walks away with 2× the limit across the boundary. Two numbers of state, and an exploitable, predictable flaw.

Sliding log. Arjun writes down the exact time of every plate and counts backwards one hour on each request. Perfectly exact, and the notebook now grows with traffic rather than with people. Measured at our scale in E2, that is about 74 GB.

Sliding counter. Arjun keeps this hour's count, last hour's count, and how far into this hour we are, then blends them as if last hour's plates were spread evenly. Three numbers, and about 0.003% of decisions come out wrong. That is Cloudflare's published figure, and it is a good general default.

Token bucket. Arjun keeps a bowl of tokens that refills at a steady drip. Each plate spends one token; an empty bowl means come back later. Two numbers, and burst is an explicit dial you can set separately from the rate.

Leaky bucket, as a queue. Instead of refusing Meera, Arjun puts her in a line that empties at a fixed pace. The output is perfectly smooth and nobody is rejected, but a queue is unbounded latency wearing a disguise.

GCRA. The same decisions as the token bucket, stored as a single number: the earliest time the next plate is allowed. One number of state, and much harder to explain in a code review. Redis's CL.THROTTLE uses it.

Now the one comparison table. The last column is the sentence you would actually say in a design review.

Algorithm State per key Notable flaw Use when
Fixed window 2 numbers Allows 2× the limit across a boundary; the bug is exploitable and predictable Never for real limits; fine for rough metrics
Sliding log 1 timestamp per request Exact, but memory grows with traffic — about 74 GB at our scale Small key counts, audit-grade accuracy
Sliding counter 3 numbers Approximates the previous window as evenly spread; ~0.003% of decisions wrong A good general default
Token bucket 2 numbers Explicit, controllable burst — which must be tuned separately from rate The production default
Leaky bucket (queue) queue Zero burst and a perfectly smooth output; it is a shaper, so it adds unbounded latency instead of rejecting Outbound traffic to a partner who will ban you for bursting
GCRA 1 number Same decisions as token bucket, harder to explain in a code review Extreme key counts; Redis CL.THROTTLE uses it

Asha picks the token bucket for the gateway, for three architectural reasons rather than mathematical ones.

Its state is 2 numbers with no per-request growth, so a trip to Kiran carries a fixed tiny payload no matter how much traffic a key has seen.

Its refill is computed lazily from a timestamp. There is no background timer sweeping millions of keys, which means a key that goes quiet costs nothing and can simply be evicted by TTL.

Burst is a separate, explicit dial from rate. That matters for real customers. "100/minute, but you may spend 20 of them at once" is a sentence Asha can put in documentation. That sentence is the difference between a limiter that works for bursty integrations and one that generates tickets.

One distinction from those six stories changes your architecture, so carry it forward.

A policer answers yes or no immediately and rejects the excess. It protects your backend and pushes the problem to the client, who must retry.

A shaper accepts the request and releases it later at a steady pace. It protects the downstream and pushes the problem into latency and memory.

Police your public edge, because you can tell strangers to retry. Shape your outbound calls to partners, because you cannot ask a card network to retry, and it will suspend you for bursting.

10.7 Coordination — how often does a clerk walk to Kiran?

In one breath: Twenty clerks, one book. Every design below is a different answer to how often a clerk walks over, and what they do while they are away.

Everything so far was setup. This is the design.

We have 20 gateway nodes, twenty Arjuns, and one logical truth. We will build it the way you would actually arrive at it: start with what a smart engineer tries first, and let each version break.

Throughout, the scoreboard is four numbers: accuracy · store load · added latency · behaviour when Kiran goes home.

v1 — what if every clerk enforces the whole limit?

This is not a design. It is what you get by accident, and it is stage 4.

Twenty nodes each enforce 100/min, so the customer gets 2,000/min, and the number changes the moment autoscaling changes the node count.

It is unusable for any limit that is a promise. It is genuinely fine for exactly one thing: a crude per-node safety valve, such as "no node will ever process more than 5,000 rps regardless of who is asking". There, the per-node number is the thing you meant.

v2 — what if each clerk gets one twentieth?

Give each node limit ÷ N. The ceiling is now correct. Asha will never exceed 100 again.

And as stage 4 already showed, the floor collapses. Ben does not spread traffic evenly, so Priya hits the wall at a fraction of her real limit while unused allowance sits idle on nodes she never reached.

It also breaks on autoscaling. N changes, so every node's share must change, which means every node needs to know the current cluster size. You have accidentally created a coordination problem inside the design whose entire selling point was having no coordination.

This works only when you can guarantee even distribution and a stable node count. That is rarer than it sounds.

v3 — what if the clerks share Kiran's book: read, decide, write?

Now the obvious real fix. Put the counter in Redis. Every node reads it, decides locally, and writes it back.

Watch what two clerks do when there is one token left.

sequenceDiagram
    participant N1 as Node 1 (Arjun)
    participant N2 as Node 2 (Bela)
    participant S as Redis (Kiran)
    Note over S: tokens = 1  (one left!)
    N1->>S: GET merchant_42
    S-->>N1: tokens = 1
    N2->>S: GET merchant_42
    S-->>N2: tokens = 1
    Note over N1,N2: both nodes now believe they hold the last token

    N2->>S: SET tokens = 0

🔮 Predict: At 40,000 rps with a 1 ms one-way hop to the store, roughly how often does that overlap happen?

Reveal

Constantly. It is the normal case, not an edge case.

If you guessed "rarely, only under contention", you are picturing the office, where two clerks reaching the book at the same instant is a coincidence. At these speeds it is not a coincidence; it is the steady state.

The window of vulnerability runs from the moment the read lands to the moment the write lands: the read's return trip plus the write's outbound trip, so about 2 ms here. At 40,000 rps that means roughly 80 requests are inside the window at any moment, every one of them reading the same counter value and spending the same tokens. For a hot key, essentially every decision is made from a stale read.

This is why "we'll just put the counter in Redis" is not a solution by itself. The store being shared does not make the operation atomic. The read and the write are two separate trips, and the gap between them is where the limit leaks.

Worse, it is a bug that scales with success. It is invisible in staging at 10 rps and severe in production at 40,000.

So v3 is broken. It is also twice as slow as it needs to be, at two round trips per request, which 10.2 already ruled out.

v4 — what if Kiran makes the decision himself?

The fix for the gap is to remove the gap. Send the decision to Kiran instead of fetching his data to the counter. In Redis that is a Lua script, or CL.THROTTLE from the redis-cell module. The script reads, refills, decides and writes, and no other client ever observes an intermediate state.

One round trip. No race. Exact enforcement across all 20 nodes.

Scoreboard number v4's answer
Accuracy Exact
Store load 120,000 ops/s — the full traffic rate
Added latency +1 round trip (~2 ms p99 in-AZ)
Store dies Every request must decide alone — see F1

This is the correct baseline, and for a great many systems it is also the final answer. Do not skip past it looking for something cleverer. If 120k ops/s fits your store and 2 ms fits your budget, you are done, and everything below buys efficiency at the cost of accuracy.

Two problems stop it being the answer for Asha.

v5 — does sharding fix Dev's flash sale?

The first problem is raw volume. 120,000 ops/s exceeds one Redis shard's ~50k, so we shard: keys are hashed across 6 shards, and each shard owns a slice of the keyspace.

That fixes aggregate throughput. It does nothing whatsoever for the limit endpoint:POST /payments, Asha's global 30,000 rps cap. That is one key, one key hashes to one shard, and one shard is one CPU core. Every request in the system touches it. Dev's flash sale is the same shape: one merchant, one key, all the traffic.

Sharding spreads keys across machines. A hot key is one key. Adding machines cannot help it. This is the single most important thing to understand about scaling a counter store, and it catches everyone who assumes "we're on a cluster" means "we scale".

The fix is to split the key itself. Replace endpoint:POST /payments with 16 sub-keys …#0 through …#15, each carrying 1/16th of the limit, and have each node pick one at random per request.

Notice what that is. It is v2's static division, applied to the store instead of the nodes, with exactly v2's weakness back in miniature. A sub-bucket can be exhausted by chance while its siblings have room, so you under-admit slightly. The error shrinks as traffic grows, because the random spread evens out, which is convenient: hot keys are by definition the ones with lots of traffic.

Sub-key splitting is necessary for any global or per-endpoint limit. Use the smallest number of sub-keys that fits under one core's capacity, because every extra sub-key adds under-admission.

v6 — what if a clerk takes twenty tickets at once?

The second problem is cost. Even sharded, Asha is doing 120,000 store operations per second and adding 2 ms to every request. Both are pure overhead.

So change what Arjun asks for. He does not ask Kiran for one token. He atomically takes a lease, which is a block of say 20 tokens. Then he serves the next 20 requests at his own counter with no walk at all.

Store load drops by roughly the batch size. Latency drops to near zero for 19 requests out of every 20.

🔮 Predict: What does a node do with leased tokens it has not spent when the traffic moves elsewhere?

Reveal

It strands them, and this is the design's real cost.

Arjun takes 20 tokens, serves 3 requests, and then Ben starts sending that customer to other counters. Those 17 tokens are already deducted from Kiran's book. The store believes they are spent. Bela is told there is no allowance left while 17 units of it sit unused on a counter nobody is walking to.

If you said "they just go back", that is what you must build: a TTL on the lease, so unspent tokens expire and return to the pool after, say, 1 second. That bounds the damage but does not remove it. During that second the quota really is unavailable.

Net effect: v6 under-admits. Worst-case stranded quota is roughly N × batch, so 20 nodes × 20 tokens = 400 units in flight. If the limit is 100/min, that is catastrophic. If the limit is 30,000/s, it is noise.

The rule this gives you: batch size must be small relative to the limit. Leases work beautifully for high limits and are unusable for low ones.

There is a second, sneakier cost. On a key that is already saturated, a node asks for a batch, is granted zero, and has burned a round trip to be told no. Every rejected request costs a full store call, exactly when the store is busiest. Production systems fix this with a short negative cache: "this key was exhausted 50 ms ago, don't ask again yet".

v7 — what if nobody walks to Kiran at all?

The last step. Nodes stop calling the store per request or per batch. Each node counts locally and, every T milliseconds, publishes what it spent and learns the global total. The clerks shout their tallies across the room instead of walking.

Hot path: zero network calls, zero added latency. Store load no longer depends on traffic at all.

The error is whatever the nodes collectively spend while out of touch:

worst-case over-admission  ≈  N × (per-node rate for that key) × T

Put Asha's numbers in it. The right-hand column says what each number is.

Quantity Value In words
N 20 gateway nodes, all counting locally
per-node rate 300 rps a key doing 6,000 rps, spread over 20 nodes
T 500 ms how long a node goes without telling anyone
overshoot 3,000 requests 20 × 300 × 0.5, admitted above the promise
as a share of a 30,000/s limit 10% fine for an abuse limit, not for a billing one

Turn the dial the other way. T = 100 ms gives 600 requests of overshoot, which is 2%, at five times the store load. That is the staleness budget from stage 6, made numeric.

🔮 Predict: v7's store load is "N nodes × their active keys ÷ T". Asha has 450,000 live counters and 20 nodes. Sync every 1 second. What is the store load — and is v7 actually cheaper than v4's 120,000 ops/s?

Reveal

20 × 450,000 ÷ 1 s = 9,000,000 ops/s. Seventy-five times worse than the per-request design it was supposed to improve on.

If you said "much cheaper, it takes the store off the hot path", you had the right mechanism and the wrong denominator. Taking the store off the hot path means the cost no longer scales with requests. It now scales with keys, and Asha has 450,000 of those.

This is the trap in v7, and it is the insight that produces the real architecture. Look at where each design's cost actually comes from.

v4, per-request, costs one trip per request. Priya's key, seen twice a minute, costs two trips a minute. Cheap, and exact.

v6 and v7, local plus sync, cost a trip per key per node per interval, whether that key saw a million requests or one. Cheap only if the key is busy.

They are cheap in opposite regimes. So do not choose between them. Apply each to the keys it suits.

Key class Share of keys Share of traffic Design Why
Cold (< 1 rps) ~99.9% ~40% v4 central atomic Few requests each, so per-request cost is tiny — and you get exactness free
Hot (> 100 rps) ~0.1% (a few hundred keys) ~60% v7 local + periodic sync Huge traffic, so per-request cost is unaffordable; few keys, so sync cost is trivial

Run the numbers with Dev and the other 199 busiest keys treated as hot:

hot:  20 nodes × 200 keys ÷ 0.5 s   =   8,000 ops/s
cold: 120,000 × 40%                 =  48,000 ops/s
                                       ------------
total                                  56,000 ops/s   (was 120,000)

Store load halved. Hot-key contention gone. Added latency removed from the majority of traffic. And the long tail of customers still gets exact enforcement: Priya and two million others, the ones with small limits where accuracy matters most.

That two-tier split is the production architecture. It is also why "which algorithm should I use?" is the wrong question. The interesting decision is which keys get which coordination design.

What if you never need to coordinate — can you route the key to one owner?

Worth naming explicitly, because it is the cheapest correct answer whenever it is available.

Suppose Ben stops sending people to whichever counter is free, and instead sends them by name: everyone whose name starts with M always goes to Bela. In engineering terms, the load balancer routes by hash of the rate-limit key, so every request for merchant_42 always reaches gateway node 7.

Node 7 then has complete, exact, local state for that merchant. No store. No coordination. No added latency. The distributed problem simply does not exist.

flowchart TD
    C[Clients] --> LB["Router<br/>hash(merchant_id) → node"]
    LB -->|merchant_42| N7["Node 7<br/><b>owns</b> merchant_42<br/>exact local state"]
    LB -->|merchant_99| N3["Node 3<br/><b>owns</b> merchant_99"]
    N7 --> B[Backend]
    N3 --> B

The costs are real, and they are structural rather than incidental.

You must control the routing layer, and you can only hash on one dimension. Asha needs three simultaneous limits: merchant, endpoint and IP. You cannot route by all three at once, so at best this covers one of them.

The owning node is a hot spot. Dev's entire flash sale now lands on one node.

Scaling or losing a node reshuffles ownership. During the reshuffle two nodes briefly both think they own a key. That is a short over-admission window on a scale-up under load, which is exactly the event when you least want one.

Node 7 dying loses that merchant's counter state entirely.

Owner routing is excellent for a single dominant dimension in a system where you own the router. This is essentially how partitioned stream processors handle per-key state. It is not sufficient alone for a multi-dimensional public API.

10.8 Which store can actually do this?

In one breath: The store must answer in under a millisecond, decide without help, forget things by itself — and it does not need to survive a restart.

The requirements are unusual, and they rule out most databases.

It needs sub-millisecond p99, so it is in-memory. It needs atomic read-decide-write, so it needs server-side scripting or a purpose-built command. It needs automatic expiry, because 450k live counters out of 6M possible means dead keys must evict themselves, and TTL is not optional.

🔮 Predict: Does the counter store need to be durable — that is, must it survive its own restart with the counters intact?

Reveal

No, and this is the counter-intuitive one.

If you said yes, you were treating the counters as data. They are not; they are a fact about the last sixty seconds, and in sixty seconds they are worthless.

Losing the counters means everyone briefly gets a fresh allowance. That is a minor, self-healing accuracy incident. Paying for durability on the hot path of 100% of your traffic, to protect data that expires in a minute, is a bad trade.

The consequence is concrete: run Redis without persistence and treat it as expendable. No AOF fsync on the hot path. Replicate for availability, not for durability. If Asha loses a shard, she loses those counters, and the correct response is a metric, not a page.

With those four requirements in hand, the candidates sort themselves out.

Candidate Verdict
Redis / Valkey The default. In-memory, Lua for atomicity, native TTL, CL.THROTTLE if you want GCRA built in
Memcached incr is atomic but there is no scripting, so a token bucket cannot be done in one operation
Relational DB Atomicity yes, sub-millisecond at 120k writes/s no. Also: this is the fastest way to turn a rate limiter into an outage

10.9 Can a limit be global across regions?

In one breath: No, not in real time. Physics already decided this in 10.2's latency table, so pick which kind of approximate you want.

Cross-region coordination on the hot path is impossible, so a "global" limit cannot be globally enforced as it happens. Asha chooses one of three, and she chooses per limit.

Approach How Accuracy Use for
Independent regional limits Each region enforces the full limit locally Total = limit × regions Abuse protection, where the point is per-region capacity anyway
Split the limit Each region gets a fixed share (US 60%, EU 40%) Correct ceiling, under-admits when traffic shifts Predictable geographic traffic
Async global reconciliation Regions enforce locally, stream counts to a global aggregator, which adjusts each region's share every few seconds Converges; wrong during the lag window Billing-adjacent limits that must be roughly globally correct

The honest position, and the one to say out loud in a design review: a global limit enforced across regions is always approximate.

If a limit genuinely must be exact and global, it cannot be enforced at the edge. It must move to whatever single-region component already serialises the operation, which is usually the database write that actually performs the action. Rate limiting is not the right tool for a hard global invariant. A transaction is.

10.10 Who decides who gets what limit?

In one breath: Omar's rules are a separate system with a separate failure mode, and the gateway must keep working when that system is down.

The limits themselves, which merchant gets what, do not live with the counters. They live in Omar's control plane.

flowchart TD
    A["Admin / API<br/>Omar sets limits"] --> DB[(Rules DB)]
    DB --> PUB["Publisher<br/>versioned snapshot"]
    PUB -.->|"push / poll every ~30s"| G1["Gateway<br/>in-memory rules cache"]
    PUB -.-> G2["Gateway<br/>in-memory rules cache"]
    G1 --> D{"decision"}

Three rules, each learned the hard way.

The rules cache is in memory on every gateway, and it never expires on failure. If Omar's service is down, gateways keep using the last snapshot they have, forever. A config system outage must not become a traffic outage. Stale limits are almost always better than no limits.

Snapshots are versioned and applied atomically. Half-applied rule sets produce inconsistent enforcement across nodes, which is impossible to debug because the answer depends on which node you hit.

Ship a hardcoded default. A brand-new gateway that cannot reach the rules service on startup must still enforce something sane, rather than starting up fully open.

10.11 What does the client see when you say no?

In one breath: The rejection is part of the design, because it decides whether rejected traffic goes away or comes straight back with friends.

HTTP/1.1 429 Too Many Requests
Retry-After: 12
RateLimit-Limit: 100
RateLimit-Remaining: 0
RateLimit-Reset: 12

Four things in that little block are load-bearing.

Send 429, not 503. 503 means you are broken, and it tells well-behaved clients and load balancers to retry aggressively and possibly mark you unhealthy. 429 means they are over their allowance.

Retry-After must be honest. If Asha says 12 seconds and is still rejecting at 12 seconds, every rejected client retries in lockstep. She has then built a synchronised retry storm, with her own header as the synchroniser. Compute it from the actual limiter state — when will there genuinely be a token? — and expect clients to add jitter.

Send the headers on success too. Priya, who can see she has 4 of 100 left, can slow herself down. A client that only learns at rejection cannot.

Return which limit was hit when several apply. "Rate limited" with no indication of which limit is unactionable, and it generates support tickets that Asha will answer by reading logs.

ENGINEERING MODEL — stages 11 to 12

11. What are the eight ways this breaks?

In one breath: The happy path was the easy half. Here are the eight things that actually happen to Asha's limiter, and the one that takes her down is not the one anybody expects.

Each failure below is a short story: how it starts, what Asha sees, and what she ships to survive it. The whole catalogue is compared in one table at the end of the stage.

F1 — what happens when the counter store goes down?

Kiran goes home sick. In engineering terms: the Redis primary fails, a failover takes 8 seconds, or a network rule change makes the store unreachable from one availability zone.

Watch it: in the visualization, choose v4 central atomic, then press Kill the store and try each fallback.

Every enforcement point loses the truth at the same moment. Arjun and Bela and eighteen others are all suddenly holding notebooks with no register book behind them. There is no correct answer here, only three choices with different victims.

Fallback Behaviour Consequence
Fail open Allow everything Your API stays up and your backend takes 100% unfiltered traffic — including whatever abuse the limiter was suppressing. A Redis blip becomes a database outage
Fail closed Reject everything Total API outage caused by a component that is not on the critical path of doing any actual work
Degrade to local Each node falls back to limit ÷ N locally Neither correct nor catastrophic — you keep serving, roughly within the intended envelope

Degrade-to-local keeps the ceiling roughly holding: Meera will not get twenty plates. It loses exactness, and it loses fairness across nodes, because v2's skew problem comes back for the duration and Priya starts seeing rejections she has not earned.

Degrade to local is the standard answer, and Asha chooses the fallback per limit, not per system. A limit protecting a fragile downstream should fail closed for that endpoint only. A limit that exists for billing fairness should fail open, because she can reconcile money later, but she cannot un-reject a customer's payment. Write the fallback into Omar's rule definition alongside the limit, so it is a product decision that somebody reviewed, and not a global default nobody remembers choosing.

F2 — why is a slow store worse than a dead one?

Kiran does not go home. Kiran gets slow. Somebody runs a KEYS command from a debugging session, or a big key expires, or memory pressure triggers eviction. The store does not fail. It answers in 400 ms instead of 1 ms.

🔮 Predict: Which is worse for Asha's API: the store being down for two minutes, or the store answering in 400 ms for two minutes?

Reveal

Slow is far worse, and it is the single most common way a rate limiter becomes the outage it was built to prevent.

If you said "down is worse, at least slow is still working", follow the threads. Every gateway request now waits 400 ms on the limiter. Request threads pile up. The gateway's connection pool exhausts. Requests that would have been allowed start failing, not because of the limit, but because the thing checking the limit is holding them. Clients time out and retry, doubling load. Asha's service is now down, and the component that took it down is the one whose entire job was to protect it.

Slow is more dangerous than down. Down is detected in milliseconds and triggers your fallback. Slow keeps the failure invisible while it consumes every resource you have.

The bitter part: limit accuracy is perfect throughout. The limiter is enforcing beautifully. What it lost was availability, which was the entire point of having it.

Asha ships all four of these, not one of them.

An aggressive timeout on the limiter call of 5–10 ms, not the library default. A timeout is treated exactly like F1's outage, and takes the fallback path.

A circuit breaker. After N consecutive timeouts, stop calling the store entirely for a few seconds and run degraded. This also stops her adding load to a store that is already struggling.

A bulkhead: a bounded, separate connection pool for limiter calls, so exhausting it cannot starve everything else.

An alert on limiter latency and fallback rate, not just on store uptime. The dashboard question is "what fraction of decisions were made without the store?" That number going from 0% to 3% is the earliest signal she gets.

F3 — what happens when one key gets all the traffic?

Dev runs a flash sale and does 15% of Asha's traffic by himself. Or the global limit endpoint:POST /payments is touched by every request in the system, which it is, by construction.

Watch it: in the visualization, set Traffic shape → one dominant key and compare store load per shard.

One key lives on one shard, which lives on one core. Adding shards does nothing. That core saturates, its latency climbs, and F2 follows it down.

The limit itself stays correct the whole time. What Asha loses is the ability to scale by adding machines: for that key she has hit a hard ceiling, and no purchase order fixes it.

The fix is either v5's sub-key split into k buckets of limit ÷ k, which reintroduces a little under-admission, or promotion to the local-plus-sync tier. Promotion stops the key touching the store per request at all. Detect hot keys continuously, as a top-N by request rate sampled at the gateway, and promote them automatically. A design that requires a human to notice a hot key will not survive its first flash sale.

F4 — what happens when the cluster resizes?

Autoscaling adds 20 gateway nodes during a traffic spike. Or a deploy rolls, and for ninety seconds Asha is briefly running 40.

Anything with a per-node share is now instantly wrong. v2's limit ÷ N is dividing by a stale N, so it is either over-admitting, when nodes think N is smaller than it is, or under-admitting. v7's error bound N × r × T grows with N precisely when traffic is highest.

Central atomic, v4 and v5, keeps everything through this, and that is its quiet superiority. Kiran does not care how many clerks are in the room. Any node-share design instead loses the ceiling itself, at exactly the worst moment.

So prefer designs that are indifferent to N. Where you cannot, derive N from the service registry rather than from configuration, and accept that during a scale event the limit is approximate. This is a strong argument for keeping the long tail on v4: your accuracy should not depend on your deployment topology, which was the original complaint back in stage 4.

F5 — what happens when the clocks disagree?

NTP drifts. A VM pauses for a live migration. Two nodes handle a leap second differently. Arjun's watch and Bela's watch now disagree by 50 ms, or, when something is genuinely broken, by minutes.

What that costs depends entirely on where time is read, which is why this failure sorts the designs so cleanly.

Where time is read What clock skew does Which designs
Inside the store Nothing. All nodes implicitly agree, because only one clock is consulted v4, v5
On each node A node running 5 minutes fast believes far more time has elapsed, refills buckets that should be empty, and hands out free allowance v1, v2, v6, v7
At a window boundary Nodes disagree about where the boundary is, so the doubling bug fires at several different moments and smears over-admission across a wider interval any fixed-window algorithm

That top row is an underrated advantage of central atomic, and it is rarely mentioned in design reviews.

Each node still orders its own events correctly. What Asha loses is the global limit, silently. There is no error, no log line, just more traffic admitted than she configured.

Read time in one place wherever possible, and let that place be the store. Monitor clock offset as a first-class metric on every gateway and alert on it. Prefer monotonic clocks for measuring elapsed time locally, because a wall clock can jump backwards, and a negative elapsed time in a refill calculation produces spectacular results.

F6 — what happens to quota a dead node was holding?

Arjun takes 20 tickets from Kiran and is then hit by a bus. In v6 terms: a node has leased 20 tokens and is killed.

Kiran's book already decremented those 20. Nobody will ever spend them. Every other node sees less allowance than actually exists. Repeat that across a rolling deploy, and a meaningful slice of every customer's limit simply evaporates.

The ceiling survives — this failure never over-admits. The floor does not. Priya is rejected below her limit, and the cause is invisible both to her and to Asha.

Three remedies. Keep lease TTLs short, about 1 second, sized so stranded quota stays small relative to the limit. Return the unused lease on graceful shutdown. And never use leases for small limits, where N × batch is a large fraction of the limit in the first place.

F7 — how does an honest-looking Retry-After build a retry storm?

Asha rejects Priya and tells her when to come back. The retry time is computed loosely: rounded to the next window, or a fixed constant, or off by a hair.

It goes wrong in two different ways.

Too early, even slightly. Priya obeys the header exactly, retries at that microsecond, and is rejected again, because the token had not quite arrived. She retries again. Asha has created a client that is polling her as fast as her own header permits. Her logs will show Priya as an abusive client, when Priya is in fact the only one doing exactly what she was asked.

This is not hypothetical. The algorithm appendix's own test retry_after_is_honest_for_every_limiter failed until floating-point slack was added to the comparison, because elapsed × rate does not land exactly on integer token counts in binary floating point. A limiter can be arithmetically wrong by 4 × 10⁻¹⁰ tokens and produce a production retry storm.

Identical for everyone. Every rejected client is told "retry in 12 seconds", so all of them retry in the same millisecond. Asha's own header is now a synchroniser, converting scattered load into a coordinated spike, once every 12 seconds, forever.

The limit is still enforced perfectly through all of this. What is lost is the purpose of the limit, because total load goes up rather than down: rejected traffic returns immediately and in lockstep.

So compute Retry-After from real limiter state — when will there actually be a token? — round up and never down, add server-side jitter so clients are spread rather than synchronised, and document exponential backoff for SDK authors. Rejecting a request is cheap. Rejecting the same request forty times a second is not.

F8 — what does one bad rule do?

Omar sets a limit to 0. Or he ships a rule set with the wrong plan-tier mapping. Or the publisher pushes a half-written snapshot.

The result is instant, total, correctly-implemented rejection of legitimate traffic. Every guarantee in stage 12 holds. Nothing is broken. The business is down.

That is what makes this failure so effective: there is no technical guarantee to lose, and nothing on the failure dashboards to find.

Asha's defences are all in Omar's plane. Validate rules on write, so a limit of 0 requires an explicit override flag. Version snapshots and make rollback a single fast operation. Roll new rule sets out gradually and watch the rejection rate. And run a shadow mode: evaluate a new rule, log what it would have rejected, and enforce nothing.

Shadow mode is the highest-value feature in a rate-limiting control plane, and it is almost always built after the first incident rather than before it.

12. What can you promise, and what can you not?

In one breath: With a sharded atomic store you can promise the ceiling holds no matter how many machines you run. You cannot promise fairness, global exactness, ordering, or protection from everyone behaving well at once.

What is guaranteed?

With sharded central atomic, v4 and v5, Asha can put four things in writing.

The ceiling holds. In any window, admitted ≤ limit + burst, regardless of how many gateway nodes exist or how Ben distributes traffic across them.

Atomicity. No two requests can both consume the same last unit of allowance. The decision and the state update are one indivisible step, which is exactly what v3 did not have.

Independence. Dev's traffic cannot consume Priya's allowance. One key's counter is one key's counter.

Node-count independence. The enforced limit does not change when Asha scales, deploys, or loses a node.

What is explicitly not guaranteed?

These are the sentences to say out loud in a design review, before somebody assumes the opposite.

Fairness within a key. If Dev's allowance is 100/min, the limiter says nothing about which 100 requests get through. A tight retry loop will systematically beat a polite client to the tokens.

Global exactness across regions. Physically impossible on the hot path. See 10.9.

Exactness during any degradation. A store outage, a store timeout, a cluster resize or clock skew each move you onto a fallback path that is approximate by design.

Ordering. A limiter is not a queue. Requests are not served in arrival order, and nothing is preserved for later.

Protection from aggregate load. Ten thousand customers, each perfectly within their individual limits, can still overwhelm Asha. Per-key limits do not sum to a system limit. That needs load shedding as a separate mechanism, and it is the most common gap in otherwise well-designed systems.

What did you trade away, and for what?

Every row here is a deliberate purchase, made in the stage named in the last column.

You gave up To get Where
Zero added latency Exactness across nodes v4: +1 round trip on every request
Exactness Zero latency and low store load v7: bounded over-admission on hot keys
Some of the customer's allowance Fewer store calls v6: stranded lease quota
Perfect enforcement during failures Staying available at all F1's degrade-to-local
A globally exact limit Being able to run in more than one region 10.9

The failure mode of an inexperienced design is not choosing wrong. It is not noticing that a choice was made.

Where is the safety-versus-liveness line here?

Safety means nothing bad happens: the limit is never exceeded. Central atomic gives Asha this unconditionally.

Liveness means something good eventually happens: allowed requests actually get served. Fail-closed preserves safety and destroys liveness. Fail-open does the reverse.

F1's fallback decision is precisely a choice of which one to sacrifice during a partition. That is the same shape as the trade you saw in the cap-theorem lab, applied to your own infrastructure rather than to a database. This time you are the database, and Kiran is the replica you cannot reach.

A good answer sounds like

"Failing closed means that when we cannot reach the counter book, we refuse everybody. The limit is then never exceeded, which is true and useless: we have turned a broken register book into a total outage, and the register book was never doing the actual work. What we want instead is for each clerk to fall back to a small local share — not exactly right, but still serving. And which way we fall is a decision per limit: refuse-everybody is right for the limit guarding a fragile downstream, and let-everybody-through is right for the one that only decides who gets billed."

What did this document deliberately leave out?

Seven pieces of real work that a production system needs and this lesson skipped. Each one names what handles it instead.

Distributed quota accounting for billing. Rate limits are approximate; invoices are not. Real systems keep a separate, durable, exact usage ledger fed asynchronously, never the limiter's counters. See transactional-outbox and event-sourcing.

Authentication and identity resolution. We assumed the gateway already knows the caller. Resolving an API key to a merchant and a plan is itself a cached lookup with its own failure modes.

Warm-up and cold start. A fresh gateway node with empty local state behaves differently for its first few seconds, which is significant during a scale-up.

Adaptive and concurrency-based limits. A fixed rate limit is a guess about capacity. Systems like Netflix's concurrency-limits derive the limit from observed latency, which is a TCP-congestion-control-shaped idea, instead of from a config file.

Priority and graceful degradation. When over the limit, reject the health checks and analytics calls before the payments.

Security of the limiter itself. If a limit is keyed on a header the client controls, the limit is optional. Key only on values you derived server-side.

Cost accounting for the store. 120k ops/s of managed Redis is a real line item, so the design choice in 10.7 is partly a budget decision.

Solutions

Rungs 1–4 have right answers. Rungs 5–8 are design work — what follows is a worked position you should be able to argue with, not a marking scheme.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Because the limiter's memory lives inside one process. Each server counts only the requests it personally handled, and none of them can see the others, so twenty servers enforcing "100 per minute" each let 100 through and the customer gets 2,000. Nobody wrote a bug — the rule simply stopped being enforceable the moment there was more than one enforcer.

1.2 Over-admission — more gets through than you promised. Victim: you (capacity, cost, the thing the limit was protecting). Shows up as a graph. Under-admission — you reject requests while allowance was actually available. Victim: your customer. Shows up as a support ticket, because from their side you are simply lying about the limit you sold them, and every dashboard you own says the limiter is working perfectly.

1.3 "…how out-of-date each enforcement point's view of the shared truth is allowed to be." (Anything equivalent to the staleness dial is right.)

1.4 Sharing the data is not the same as sharing the decision. If each node reads the counter, decides locally, and writes it back, then every node that reads during the gap between another node's read and write sees the same stale value and spends the same tokens. Correctness requires the decision to happen in one indivisible step at the store — not merely for the number to be stored there.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 Data plane = the solid grey lines (clients → router → gateways → backend). Coordination plane = the dashed violet lines from each gateway down to the counter store. The control plane (rules: who gets what limit) is not drawn, because the simulator holds the limit fixed at 60/s — there is nothing to distribute. In production it is the third set of arrows and it fails independently of the other two (README F8).

2.2 v1: each gateway stores a complete token bucket for the full limit — its bar is its own private allowance, and there are N of them. v7: each gateway stores what it believes the whole world has spent this second — the count it last learned, plus its own spending since. It resets every second because v7 is counting inside a fixed one-second window; at the window boundary everyone's count goes back to zero.

2.3 One bar is one sub-key: an independent bucket carrying a fraction of the promise. With 4 sub-keys and a 60/s promise, each carries 15/s. A request picks one at random, so a request can be rejected by an exhausted sub-key while another sub-key still has room.

2.4 v6 shows roughly a third of v4's store operations (measured: ~62/s against ~180/s at 180 rps). The requests producing no store operation are being decided entirely locally, out of the block of tokens the gateway already leased. A gateway only talks to the store when its lease runs dry — plus a short "this key was empty a moment ago" pause so a saturated key does not cost a round trip per rejection.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 Admitted goes to roughly 8 × 60 = 480/s, capped by whatever traffic you are actually offering (at 180 rps you will simply see all 180 admitted — the limit has effectively stopped existing). Store operations stay at zero, which is the point: this design is free precisely because it is wrong.

3.2 From 60/s to about 15/s. Sticky sends every request for this key to one gateway, and that gateway is only permitted limit ÷ 4. The other three hold 45/s of allowance that no request will ever reach. Note the asymmetry with 3.1: the same routing change that fixes v1 completely destroys v2.

3.3 (a) Admitted drops sharply toward the promise — from around 175/s at 1000 ms to roughly 70/s at 100 ms. (b) Store ops rise by the same factor, roughly 4/s to 40/s. Ten times the coordination buys you about ten times less error. That is the entire trade, and it is linear.

3.4 Admitted climbs steeply. The mechanism: the write lands 2 × hop milliseconds after the read, and every request arriving inside that window reads the same stale counter. The number of requests in the window is rps × 2 × hop, so at 180 rps and a 1 ms hop about 0.4 requests overlap (almost no leak), while at 12 ms about 4.3 overlap and each of them spends tokens the others already spent. The bug's severity is the product of your latency and your traffic — which is exactly why it is invisible in staging.

3.5 Degrade: about 60/s under round robin, because limit ÷ N happens to add up when traffic is even — switch the router to skewed and it collapses. Fail open: all 180/s; the limiter has stopped existing. Fail closed: 0/s; a perfectly-enforced total outage.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 v1 (no coordination) + sticky routing. Every request for the key reaches the same gateway, so that gateway's local state is complete and exact. This is key-owner routing. Its three costs: (i) you must own the router and can only hash on one dimension, so it cannot serve three simultaneous limits; (ii) the owning gateway becomes a hot spot for your biggest customer; (iii) scaling or losing a node reshuffles ownership, and during the reshuffle two nodes briefly both believe they own the key — an over-admission window on exactly the event (a scale-up under load) when you least want one. Node death also loses that key's counter outright.

4.2 You will find something in the 150–300 ms region holds within ~15%. Then doubling the traffic pushes it back out of tolerance, because the error is N × per-node rate × T — it is proportional to traffic, and your load test fixed the traffic. The lesson: a sync interval tuned at one traffic level is not a constant, it is a point measurement. Size it from your peak rate for that key, or make it adaptive.

4.3 You trace a curve of diminishing returns. Store ops fall roughly as 1/batch — big gains from 2 to 10, small ones from 20 to 30 — while under-admission grows roughly linearly with N × batch as stranded allowance. It stops helping once the batch is large enough that a gateway routinely fails to spend it before the lease expires; past that you are paying accuracy for savings you no longer receive. Rule of thumb from the README: batch must stay small relative to the limit, which is why leases are for large limits only.

4.4 Because sub-key splitting costs accuracy through variance, and variance only shows when the sub-keys are near their capacity rather than far past it. At 80 rps against a 60/s promise split 8 ways, each sub-key gets about 10 requests/s against a 7.5/s allowance — close enough that random arrival lumpiness leaves one sub-key rejecting while another has unspent tokens, and its buffer (burst ÷ 8) is too small to smooth it out. At very high traffic every sub-key is permanently saturated, so each simply admits its full 7.5/s and the total is exact.

The implication is uncomfortable and worth stating in a design review: splitting a key degrades accuracy precisely in the regime where the customer is near their limit — which is the only regime they notice. It is safe when the key is genuinely, continuously saturated, and that is exactly what "hot key" means. Split hot keys; do not split ordinary ones.

Rung 5 — Specify

5.1 The atomic store operation

CHECK_AND_CONSUME(keys[], costs[], now, limits[], bursts[]) -> Decision

reads   : for each key, {tokens, lastRefillAt}
writes  : for each key, {tokens, lastRefillAt}   — only if ALL keys pass
returns : allowed        : bool
          blockedBy      : key name or null
          retryAfterMicros : int
          remaining[]    : per key

GUARANTEE: the whole read → refill → decide → write sequence is indivisible.
No other caller may observe or modify any of these keys in between.

Three points that carry the design:

  • All keys in one call, consume only if all pass. Checking the three dimensions in three calls triples latency and store load, and leaves you consuming quota from limits 1 and 2 for a request that limit 3 rejected.
  • now is supplied by the store, not the caller. That is what makes the design immune to clock skew (F5) — one clock is consulted, so all gateways implicitly agree.
  • retryAfterMicros and remaining are the two outputs that exist purely for the HTTP response, and the gateway cannot compute them itself: both depend on the post-decision state of a counter that the gateway does not hold and that other gateways are concurrently changing. Computing them gateway-side is how you get a dishonest Retry-After and the retry storm in F7.

5.2 Rule schema

rule:
  id: rl.merchant.payments.standard
  version: 47                      # snapshots are versioned and applied atomically
  match:
    plan: standard
    endpointGroup: payments-write
  key: [merchant_id, endpoint_group]   # the identity this allowance belongs to
  limit: 100
  window: 1m
  burst: 20                        # tuned separately from rate, and documented to customers
  costs:                           # a request is not one unit of work
    default: 1
    POST /payments/bulk: 20
    GET  /payments/{id}: 1
  onStoreUnavailable: degrade_local   # degrade_local | fail_open | fail_closed
  mode: enforce                    # enforce | shadow
  response:
    exposeLimitName: true          # false for security limits (see 7.2)

onStoreUnavailable sits inside the rule on purpose. F1 has no globally correct answer: a limit guarding a fragile downstream should fail closed, one that exists for billing fairness should fail open. Making it a per-rule field turns it into a reviewed product decision instead of a global default nobody remembers choosing.

5.3 Hot/cold promotion policy

  • Signal: requests per second for a given key, measured per gateway and summed at the store during the periodic sync (you already have that channel).
  • Grain: the full rate-limit key, not the merchant — merchant_42 may be cold while endpoint:POST /payments is scorching.
  • Window: a 10-second sliding rate, so a single burst does not promote.
  • Thresholds: promote above 100 rps; demote below 25 rps. The gap is deliberate.
  • At the moment of promotion: the key's authoritative counter must be drained into the new scheme, not duplicated. Concretely: the store marks the key hot with an effective-from timestamp; gateways that see the flag stop per-request calls and begin syncing; the store's counter continues to be the aggregation point, so nothing is double-counted. The dangerous window is gateways learning the flag at different times — during it, some are per-request and some are syncing, which is safe (both are conservative) but briefly under-admits.
  • How a naive version oscillates: with a single threshold, promoting a key removes per-request store traffic, which is the very signal you were measuring — so the key looks cold again, gets demoted, becomes hot, and flaps. The gap between promote and demote thresholds (hysteresis) plus measuring rate at the gateway rather than at the store are what prevent it.

Rung 6 — Debug

6.1 — "exact in staging, 3× in production." There are exactly two candidates, and they are distinguishable.

  1. Node count — staging runs one instance, production runs several, and the design has no coordination (README v1). The over-admission factor would equal the instance count and would be flat with traffic.
  2. The read-modify-write race (v3). Over-admission is rps × 2 × store-hop shaped, so it would grow with traffic and vanish overnight.

Tell them apart in one query: plot the over-admission factor against request rate. Flat → it is your topology; rising → it is the race. Also check store operations per request: two ops per request means a read and a separate write, which is the race by construction. The fix for (1) is to coordinate at all; for (2) it is to move the decision into the store as one atomic script — not a bigger Redis.

6.2 — Tuesday 14:00, latency 90 ms → 2.5 s, no Redis errors. This is F2: the store is not down, it is slow, and slow is more dangerous than down. A weekly scheduled job (an analytics scan, a KEYS sweep, a background save, bulk key expiry) is blocking Redis's single thread. Every gateway request now blocks on the limiter, request threads pile up, and requests that would have been allowed start failing.

The fix is four things, not one: an aggressive timeout on the limiter call (5–10 ms, treated exactly like an outage); a circuit breaker so you stop adding load to a struggling store; a bulkhead so the limiter's connection pool cannot starve everything else; and alerting on limiter latency and fallback rate rather than on store uptime — uptime was green the whole time.

6.3 — 400,000 password attempts, never rejected. The key is wrong, not the counting. Limiting login by IP alone means an attacker with a botnet has as many allowances as they have addresses; 400,000 attempts spread over 100,000 hosts is 4 each, comfortably under 5/minute. This is README 10.5, rule 2: login needs two keys at once — per source IP and per target username. The IP key stops one host trying many accounts; the username key stops many hosts trying one account. Either alone is trivially bypassed, and this is the single most common rate-limiting design bug in production.

6.4 — total volume went UP 40% after shipping the limiter. F7. Two mechanisms, probably both: a fixed 30-second Retry-After given to every rejected client turns scattered load into a synchronised spike every 30 seconds (your own header is the synchroniser); and if the retry time is even slightly early, obedient clients retry, get rejected again, and poll you as fast as you permit. Check also that you are returning 429 and not 503 — 503 tells well-behaved clients and load balancers that you are broken, which triggers aggressive retries and health-check failures. Fix: compute the retry time from real limiter state, round up, add server-side jitter, and document exponential backoff.

6.5 — half the nodes on the new rule for ninety minutes. F8, control plane. The rule set is being distributed without versioning or atomic application, so nodes converge at their own pace and there is no way to see which node is on which version. The customer's experience depends on which gateway they land on — which is why it looked random. Fix: versioned snapshots applied atomically, a per-node "current rule version" metric so divergence is visible, gradual rollout watched against rejection rate, single-operation rollback, and shadow mode so a new rule's effect is measured before it is enforced.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 — a hot-tier gateway dies holding 40 unpublished spends. The other gateways believe the global count for that key is 40 lower than it really is, so they will admit roughly 40 extra requests before the window rolls. The error is over-admission, and it is silent.

Two mechanisms that bound it:

  • Shorten the sync interval. Damage is linear in T, so halving the interval halves the worst case. Cost: proportionally more store load, paid continuously to insure against a rare event.
  • Publish before spending — reserve the allowance at the store, then spend it locally. A death now strands reserved-but-unspent quota instead of hiding spent-but-unpublished quota, converting over-admission into under-admission. Cost: this is v6, so you inherit v6's latency on the reserve path and its stranded-quota problem, and you have chosen which error you prefer rather than eliminating error.

A third, cheaper partial measure: cap what any one gateway may spend between syncs at limit ÷ N, which bounds the worst case regardless of T. It reintroduces v2's under-admission whenever traffic is uneven.

7.2 — telling the customer which limit they hit. The store operation must return blockedBy (which key failed) plus that key's own reset time, since only the store knows both. The gateway then applies a disclosure policy, which comes from the rule's exposeLimitName field:

  • Limits the customer owns (their merchant limit, their plan's endpoint limit): name it and return its Retry-After. This is actionable and reduces support load.
  • Security limits (per-IP, per-username login limits, global endpoint caps): return a generic 429 with no limit name, no remaining count, and a jittered retry hint. Naming them lets an attacker calibrate — they learn the exact threshold, the exact window, and whether they have found the right dimension to spread across.
  • Never return RateLimit-Remaining for a limit you would not name; the header leaks the same information more precisely.

7.3 — unauthenticated endpoints. You have no API key, so:

  • Key on the source IP — stops one host enumerating many accounts. Useless against a botnet, and it punishes large NATs, where thousands of legitimate users share one address.
  • Key on the target identifier (the username or email being logged into / reset) — stops many hosts attacking one account. Useless against spraying, where the attacker tries one common password against a million different accounts, touching each username once.
  • Therefore also key on the endpoint globally — a cap on total failed logins per minute across the whole system. This is the only key that catches spraying, and it is a hot key by construction, so it needs sub-key splitting.

An attacker with 50,000 IP addresses defeats the first key completely and is unaffected by the second unless they concentrate on one account. What actually stops them is the third key plus escalation that is not rate limiting at all: proof-of-work or CAPTCHA once the global failure rate is anomalous, account lockout with out-of-band recovery, and credential-stuffing detection on password reuse. State the limit of the tool honestly: rate limiting raises the cost of abuse, it does not prevent a determined distributed attacker, and a design review that claims otherwise is wrong.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 Three-region payments limiter

Shape of the answer: three limit classes, three different coordination designs. Anyone who proposes one mechanism for all three has not read the requirements.

Capacity. 80,000 rps global peak, split roughly US 45% / EU 35% / IN 20% → 36k / 28k / 16k rps. Three dimensions per request → ~108k / 84k / 48k counter checks/s regionally. Three million merchants × 3 dimensions × ~120 B ≈ 1.1 GB if fully resident — again, memory is not the constraint; the 108k checks/s against a ~50k-per-shard ceiling is, so US needs ≥3 shards for throughput and would run ~6.

Class 1 — free tier, 100/min, 3M merchants. Cold keys: a handful of requests each. Per-request atomic at the regional store (v4/v5). It is exact, and exactness is nearly free here because the traffic per key is tiny. For the global-versus-regional problem, use home-region pinning: a merchant is assigned a home region and their traffic is routed there, so their counter is single-region and genuinely exact. Merchants who genuinely send from everywhere are the exception and can be reconciled as Class 2. Fallback: fail_open. Accuracy here matters far less than availability, and these are the customers you least want to break.

Class 2 — enterprise, contractual, globally accurate to 1%. Do not attempt to count globally in real time; cross-region round trips are 70–150 ms and the latency budget is 5 ms. Instead split the quota into regional shares and reallocate the shares asynchronously: a global controller collects each region's consumption every 1–2 s and re-divides the limit in proportion to observed demand, holding ~10% back as an unallocated reserve for sudden shifts.

The key property: shares always sum to the limit, so the ceiling can never be exceeded — the error is one-sided (under-admission) and appears only when demand shifts between regions faster than the controller reallocates. Within a region, each share is enforced by atomic per-request checks, so it is exact. Fallback: degrade_local to the last-known share, which stays safe because shares sum correctly even when the controller is unreachable.

Class 3 — the 25,000 rps global cap on POST /payments. This is an external contract with a penalty, so treat the ceiling as inviolable and buy safety with under-admission. Fixed regional shares (say 11,250 / 8,750 / 5,000) reallocated by the same controller as Class 2, but with a safety margin — allocate 23,000 of the 25,000 and keep 2,000 permanently unissued. Inside each region this is one hot key by construction, so split it into sub-keys per stage 10.7 / F3.

One addition the other classes do not need: shape rather than police the outbound leg. You cannot ask a card network to retry, and it will suspend you for bursting, so the calls leaving your system toward it go through a leaky-bucket queue that emits at a steady rate. Police the front door, shape the back door.

What you cannot fully meet, and what to negotiate. The enterprise 1% global accuracy commitment is not achievable as an instantaneous guarantee: a customer that abruptly moves all traffic from EU to US will be under-admitted for the duration of one reallocation cycle, and a controller partition freezes the shares entirely. Negotiate the contract into a form you can actually honour: define the limit as measured over a rolling 60-second window rather than instantaneously, state the regional-share model in the contract, and commit to never exceeding the limit (which you genuinely can) rather than always reaching it.

On-call dashboard, in the order you would read it during an incident: rejection rate split by limit class; limiter decision latency p99/p999; fallback rate (fraction of decisions made without the store — should be zero); and share-reallocation lag together with top-N keys by rate.

8.2 Moving to 50 cells

Survives unchanged: the algorithm choice (token bucket), key design and the cost-weighting scheme, the response contract, the control plane and its shadow mode, and the per-rule fallback policy. Inside a cell you still run several gateway nodes against one store, so the whole v4/v5 discussion applies at cell scale — with a much smaller N, which makes degrade-to-local far more accurate.

Becomes unnecessary: cross-region reconciliation for merchant-scoped limits. If a merchant maps to exactly one cell, their counter is exact by construction — this is key-owner routing promoted to an architectural principle, and it is the single biggest benefit of the move.

The new problem the cell router creates: it is now a stateful, critical component in front of everything, and merchant→cell assignment is authoritative state that must be consistent across every router instance. Two consequences. First, rebalancing a merchant between cells (for capacity, or when a cell is drained) creates a window in which two cells hold counter state for the same merchant and both enforce the full limit — a 2× over-admission window on exactly the operation you perform under load. You need an explicit handover: freeze in the old cell, drain, then activate in the new one, accepting brief under-admission instead of over-admission. Second, endpoint-global limits get worse, not better: the 25,000 rps cap must now be divided 50 ways instead of 3, so each cell holds 500 rps and the under-admission from static division becomes severe. Those limits need their own aggregation path that does not follow the cell boundary.

8.3 The argument for not building any of this

Scale. We serve 400 rps at peak across three application instances. Our limits exist to stop a single misbehaving integration from saturating our database — they are safety valves, not products. Nobody has bought a contractual rate limit, and no number in our configuration was derived from anything more rigorous than "this feels like more than any sane client would send".

The error is smaller than the uncertainty. With three instances, an uncoordinated limiter is wrong by 3×. But the limit itself is a guess with at least that much slack in it, so a 3× error on a number we invented is not a correctness problem — it is a rounding difference. Setting each instance's limit to a third of the intended figure and accepting the routing skew costs us nothing we can measure, and our load balancer's distribution is even enough that the skew stays small at this instance count.

What we would be buying. A shared counter store is a new hot dependency in the path of 100% of our traffic, with sub-millisecond latency requirements, its own failure modes, its own on-call burden, and a well-documented tendency to take down the service it was installed to protect when it gets slow rather than down. We would be adding a component whose worst day is worse than the problem it solves. Use the gateway's built-in per-node limiting, write the assumption down in an ADR, and revisit it the day we sell a customer a number — because that is the day the limit stops being a guess and starts being a promise.
