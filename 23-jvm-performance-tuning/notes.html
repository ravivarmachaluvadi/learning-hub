Lab 23 · The JVM Underneath: One Machine, Many Threads

JVM Performance Tuning — how to find the one thing that is actually slow

Tell a friend: Omar waits eleven minutes for a sandwich, the owner hires two more staff, and the queue does not move at all. Nobody ever stood at the door with a stopwatch and split those eleven minutes into where they went. Split them, and six of the seven pieces are tiny while one is huge: the wait for the shop's single toaster. A request into your Java service splits the same way, into seven pieces, and exactly one of them is holding you back today. Performance tuning is finding that piece, and pasting in JVM flags is what people do instead — a flag can only change how the JVM spends time it is already spending.

The trainer for this lab is visualization.html, also published as The Bottleneck Machine. What the lab is made of, where its numbers come from, and where it sits among your other labs is in section 17 at the end.

BEGINNER MODEL — stages 1 to 9

1. Why does this problem exist at all?

In one breath: Your service is usually fast and occasionally awful. That is the exact shape that destroys trust. And the fix people reach for first is a handful of JVM flags, which cannot touch most of the problem.

Meera runs a service. It answers most requests quickly. But once in every hundred, somebody waits two seconds.

One in a hundred sounds like a rounding error. It is not. A page that makes twenty calls to Meera's service will contain one of those slow calls most of the time. So her customers experience something "usually fine and randomly awful". That is the profile that destroys trust in software.

Meera is asked to fix it by Friday. She searches, finds a page titled "Top 10 JVM flags for performance", and pastes six of them into the startup command.

Three things can happen next. Sometimes nothing changes. Sometimes it gets worse. Occasionally it gets better and she never finds out why — and that is the worst of the three, because now she believes something false.

The sentence people get wrong. "We have a latency problem, so let's tune the JVM." Almost every time somebody says that, they mean let's paste in some flags. But a flag can only change how the JVM spends time it is already spending. Suppose 60% of Meera's request is spent asleep, waiting for a database to answer. There is no flag that touches that 60%. Not one, in any JDK ever shipped. The first job is not to tune. The first job is to find out which 60% she has.

Not doing that job costs three things, and all three are concrete.

  1. Money. If you do not know which resource is the constraint, you buy more of all of them. Fleets routinely run at three times the size they need, because the real limit was one lock nobody ever timed.
  2. Latency you cannot explain to anyone. "It's the GC" is what people say when they do not know. It usually is not the GC.
  3. Time. A week spent optimising a method that is 2% of the request is a week gone. The graph does not move. Now nobody believes performance work is worth funding.

2. What happens when you follow one customer with a stopwatch?

In one breath: Four people work in a sandwich shop, the queue is out of the door, and the owner hires two more. Nothing improves, because nobody checked where the eleven minutes were actually going.

Forget computers for a moment. There is a sandwich shop with four people in it.

  • Asha takes the order at the counter.
  • Bharat toasts the bread. There is one toaster.
  • Chen puts the fillings on.
  • Dee wraps it and takes the money. There is one till, and only Dee has the key to it.

At 12:30 the queue is out of the door. Omar joins the back of it and waits eleven minutes for one sandwich. The owner wants this fixed by Friday.

The owner does the obvious thing and hires two more people. Now six people work in a kitchen built for four. The queue is exactly as long as it was. Everyone bumps into each other. And Dee, who still has the only till key, has a line of five colleagues standing behind her holding finished sandwiches, waiting their turn to ring up.

Here is what the owner never did. Stand at the door with a stopwatch and follow Omar all the way through. Do that, and this is what you write down.

🔮 Predict: The owner can do exactly one thing on Friday. Hire a third extra person, make Chen twice as fast at fillings, or buy a second toaster for sixty pounds. Which one moves Omar's eleven minutes, and roughly by how much?

Reveal

The second toaster, and it is not close. Omar spends 3 minutes 5 seconds waiting for the toaster to be free. That queue exists because there is one toaster, so a second toaster deletes most of it. It also shortens the 6 minutes 10 seconds spent outside the door, because customers clear faster.

If you said make Chen faster, look at the fillings row: 30 seconds. Chen could work at infinite speed and Omar saves 30 seconds out of 660. That is 4.5%, and the queue is still out of the door.

If you said hire another person, the shop already tried that. Six people in a four-person kitchen did not move the queue at all, because the toaster does not toast faster when more people watch it.

Notice what the stopwatch did. It did not tell the owner how bad things were — everyone already knew it was eleven minutes. It told them where the eleven minutes went, and that is a completely different kind of number. The second one is a diagnosis. The first one is a complaint.

3. Why not just make everything bigger?

In one breath: The instinct in a kitchen and in a JVM is the same: buy more of whatever you can buy. Every one of those levers is real. Every one of them is a lottery ticket until you know what is slow.

Arjun looks at Meera's service and proposes the obvious things. In Java they look like this.

  • More heap. Push -Xmx from 512 MB to 4 GB. Memory is cheap.
  • More threads. Take the pool from 16 to 200. There is clearly work waiting.
  • More instances. Scale the deployment from 4 pods to 12.
  • A different collector, because somebody on the internet said theirs is faster.
  • A pile of flags copied from a blog post about a different JDK, a different workload, and a machine with a different number of cores.

Every one of these is a real lever. Every one is sometimes the right answer. And every one here is being applied without knowing what is actually slow. That is what makes the whole exercise a lottery.

🔮 Predict: A service answers 100 requests a second on a 16-core machine. Its p99 is bad. The CPUs are only about 11% busy — 100 requests a second needing 18 ms of CPU each is 1.8 cores of demand on 16, so there is loads of hardware sitting idle. Somebody quadruples the heap from 512 MB to 4 GB. What happens to p99?

Reveal

Nothing. p99 was 1,327 ms before and 1,327 ms after — the same to the millisecond. [MODEL, scenario 1 on the visualization. The three tags used in this lab, [MODEL], [HAND] and [JVM], are explained in section 17.]

Nothing happened because nothing was ever wrong with memory. Over the whole run the collector ran twice, and the average request lost 0.0 ms to it. What the average request actually lost was 87.3 ms standing in line for a lock that only one thread at a time may hold. That is Dee's till key.

Change the right thing instead. Shrink that one-at-a-time section from 45% of the work to 6%, and p99 falls from 1,327 ms to 211 ms — on the same heap, on the same hardware, with no flags at all.

If you guessed that a bigger heap would at least help a bit, you were reasoning that memory pressure is always in the mix somewhere. Here it was not in the mix at all. Zero is a real number.

4. How does "make it bigger" break, and what does that reveal?

In one breath: The heap experiment did nothing, which is merely disappointing. The thread-pool one does something worse: it goes backwards.

The heap change was harmless. The other three on Arjun's list are not, and the thread-pool one is worth stopping on.

Take a handler that is pure computation. It needs 20 ms of CPU and makes no database call. It runs on 8 cores, and more requests arrive than the machine can handle. The queue is full, so there is definitely work sitting there for extra threads to pick up.

🔮 Predict: You raise the pool from 8 threads to 128. Same machine, same cores, same code, same arriving traffic. What happens to the number of requests answered per second?

Reveal

It falls by 29.6%: 300.0/s at 8 threads, and 211.3/s at 128. The p99 goes from 1,623 ms to 8,798 ms [MODEL].

The cores were already busy every single millisecond at 8 threads. There was no spare capacity for the extra 120 threads to use. What they added instead was cost: cache traffic between cores, and an operating system shuffling 128 runnable things onto 8. Each thread's work inflated by 1.75×.

If you guessed "flat, because the cores were already full", you had the right mechanism and stopped one step early. Extra threads do not merely fail to help. They charge rent. No kitchen analogy predicts this, and stage 10 gives you the formula that does.

So the naive approach fails. But how it fails is the interesting part, and it points at something deeper than "measure first".

Look again at Omar's eleven minutes. The owner's problem was not that they failed to count. They had a number: eleven minutes. It was real, correct and honestly collected. It was also completely useless.

A single total tells you how bad, and never why. The thing you naturally reach for is how long did it take, and that cannot in principle find the bottleneck. A total is one number. A diagnosis is that total split by where the time went. Everything else in this lab is that one sentence, applied harder.

5. What exactly must the method do?

In one breath: Four jobs, in order. Split the time, name the biggest piece, work out the ceiling before you start, and prove afterwards that the piece you attacked actually shrank.

We need a method that does four things, and the order matters.

  1. Split a request's elapsed time into parts. Each part must be blamed on one identifiable cause, and the parts must add up to the whole. No time may be unaccounted for. Unaccounted time is exactly where the problem hides.
  2. Name the largest part — separately for the typical request and for the slow ones. You are about to see how often those two are slow for completely different reasons.
  3. Predict the ceiling before you spend the week. If the part you plan to attack is 20% of the request, deleting it entirely still leaves you 80%. You want that number before you start, not after.
  4. Prove the change. Split the time again afterwards and show that the part you attacked really shrank. Not that some number somewhere moved.

Anything that does not do all four is guessing with extra steps.

6. What is the one idea underneath all of it?

In one breath: Two sentences. Every millisecond of a request is being spent on something identifiable. And at any moment, exactly one of those things is your limit.

First: a request's elapsed time is a budget, and every millisecond is being spent somewhere. Not "roughly accounted for". Exactly accounted for. If a request took 250 ms, then there are 250 milliseconds, each of which was spent doing one nameable thing. Label them all and you have a diagnosis instead of a complaint.

Second: at any given moment the system has one bottleneck, and improving anything else changes nothing. This is not a slogan. It is arithmetic. The toaster wait is 3 minutes of Omar's 11. Make Chen's fillings station infinitely fast and you save 30 seconds, which is 4.5%. The queue is still out of the door.

Everything else in this lab is those two sentences pushed harder. The flags come at the very end, and there are far fewer of them than you expect.

7. What do engineers call all of this?

In one breath: You have already felt every problem these words name. Here is the terminology, grouped by the job each word does, with every one pointing back at the moment you met it.

This is the whole vocabulary of the subject, and it is smaller than it looks. Nothing below is a new idea. Each word is a name for something Omar, Dee or Meera has already run into.

Which three things can you want, and why can't you have all three?

Omar cares how long his sandwich takes. The owner cares how many sandwiches leave the shop per hour. The landlord cares how much space the shop occupies. Those are three different questions and they pull against each other.

Throughput is how much work finishes per unit of time: requests per second, messages per second, rows per second. It is the only performance number a business cares about directly, because it maps to capacity and therefore to money. You met it as the "requests answered" column in stage 4, and it is that column that exposed 128 threads as a disaster.

Latency is how long one unit of work takes, from the point of view of whoever is waiting for it. Omar's eleven minutes is a latency. It needs its own word because it and throughput move in opposite directions constantly. Batching raises throughput and raises latency. A bigger queue raises throughput slightly and raises latency enormously.

Footprint is how much memory the whole process occupies as the operating system sees it, not as the JVM sees it. It matters because it is what your container limit is compared against. Exceeding it does not make you slow. It makes you dead, and stage 11's F3 is that death.

jvm-garbage-collectors called these three the corners of a triangle, and you must trade inside it. A concurrent collector buys low latency by spending throughput and footprint. A bigger heap buys throughput by spending footprint. Batching buys throughput by spending latency. There is no setting that improves all three at once. If you think you have found one, you have found a bug you were previously suffering from — which is a different and much happier thing.

How do you talk about latency without lying?

Line every request up in order of how long it took. The percentile is the value below which that share of them fall. The p99 is the number that only one request in a hundred beats.

Why not just take the average? Because the mean is a genuinely bad summary of latency, and it is worth knowing exactly why. Latency distributions have a long right tail: a few requests are enormously slower than the rest. The mean gets dragged part-way towards them and ends up describing nobody at all.

Tail latency is the name for that slow end — p95, p99, p99.9, and the worst case. It gets its own word because the tail has its own separate causes. In this lab's baseline run, the average request spent its time waiting on the database, while the slowest 5% spent theirs on CPU. Same run, same code, two completely different diagnoses. Fix the average and the tail stays. The visualization has a toggle for exactly this, and it is the single most useful control on the page.

Utilisation is the fraction of the time a resource is busy. Engineers write it as the Greek letter rho, ρ. You already own the dangerous part from queueing-theory: waiting is not proportional to load. Headroom, or spare capacity, is capacity minus offered load — the work the resource could still absorb. That lab proved it is headroom, not utilisation, that sets your latency. Which is why "we have 20% headroom" is not, on its own, a sentence about safety. Keep the word; stage 13 uses it.

What are the words for where the time actually goes?

The bottleneck is the one resource whose exhaustion currently limits the system. The word exists to make it sayable that there is only one of them at a time. Bharat's toaster is the shop's. And when you fix it, the bottleneck moves to whatever is now the limit — fix the lock in scenario 1 and the bottleneck becomes CPU immediately. That is why performance work is a loop and not a task.

Now the most important distinction in the whole lab. At every instant, a thread is doing one of exactly two things. It is executing instructions on a core, which is on-CPU. Or it is not, which is off-CPU: asleep waiting for a network reply, blocked on a lock, parked, or runnable with no free core to run on. Dee ringing up a sandwich is on-CPU. Dee standing in line for the till key is off-CPU, and she looks exactly as busy from across the room.

Why does that distinction matter more than any other? Because your CPU profiler can only see the first kind. Take a handler that does 6 ms of work and makes one 180 ms database call. It spends 4.5% of its elapsed time on-CPU [MODEL]. A CPU profiler pointed at that service is describing 4.5% of the problem and is completely silent about the other 95.5%. It does not warn you. It has no way to.

The time budget, also called the decomposition, is the split of one request's elapsed time into named parts that sum to the whole. It is Omar's stopwatch table. This lab uses seven parts and stage 8 names them all. It is the central artefact of the subject; everything else is an instrument for producing it.

Which four instruments answer which question?

These four get confused constantly, and confusing them wastes weeks. Each one answers a different question, and none of them answers another one's.

The instrument The question it answers What it is like Where it shows up
Monitoring is it bad right now, and getting worse? continuous, cheap, aggregated, always on dashboards and alerts, stage 13
Profiling inside one process, where is the time going? sampled, detailed, usually on demand this is what produces the decomposition
Tracing for one request, which services and spans, and how long each? follows a request across processes step 1 of the method, stage 10
Benchmarking is version B faster than A, under conditions I control? deliberately artificial F9, and why it is dangerous

A sampling profiler does not record every method call. It wakes up many times a second, looks at what each thread is doing right now, and writes it down. Why work that way? Because an instrumenting profiler that records every call changes the program's timing so much that its answers stop describing the real program. The arithmetic is simple: at 100 samples per second over 10 seconds you have 1,000 observations, so a method appearing in 600 of them was on-CPU roughly 60% of the time.

A flame graph is the standard picture of a profile. Each box is a method. The box's width is the share of samples containing it. Boxes stack upward to show who called whom. Here is the whole skill in five words: look for wide boxes, not tall ones. Tall means deep call stacks, which is aesthetics. Wide means time, which is the answer.

A safepoint is a point in compiled code where a thread is willing to stop, and where the JVM knows the exact layout of that thread's stack. You met it in jvm-garbage-collectors: a pause cannot start until every thread has reached one. This lab adds two new consequences. Some profilers can only take a sample at a safepoint, which gives them a systematic blind spot (F8). And the time the slowest thread takes to arrive at one is called time-to-safepoint — it is added to every pause, and it is invisible in the garbage-collection log (F6).

How does the same code get faster while you watch?

When a method first runs, the JVM executes its bytecode one instruction at a time. That is the interpreter, and it is like a new hire reading the recipe card aloud, line by line, for every sandwich. Correct, and slow.

While the program runs, the JVM counts how often each method is called and how each branch behaves. It then compiles the hot ones into native machine code using what it has observed. That is JIT, or just-in-time, compilation. It can beat a compiler that runs before the program does, because it knows things a static compiler cannot. One example: which class actually turns up at a particular call site 99.9% of the time.

The JVM's default arrangement is tiered compilation: interpreted first, then a fast compiler that produces mediocre code quickly (C1), then a slow compiler that produces excellent code (C2). Why two compilers? Because C2 is far too slow to run on everything, and C1 gets you 80% of the benefit almost immediately.

Warmup is the period after startup during which your code is still slower than it will eventually be. Deoptimisation is what happens when the JIT's guess turns out to be wrong. It compiled on the assumption that "this call site is always ArrayList". When something else turns up, the compiled code is thrown away and the method drops back to being interpreted while it re-profiles. Why you care: a rare code path taken for the first time under load can make a hot method transiently much slower, for no reason visible in your metrics.

Escape analysis is the compiler proving that an object never leaves the method it was created in, so it need not be allocated on the heap at all. You met it in the GC lab. Here it matters for one specific reason: it is the single largest cause of a benchmark reporting that your code takes zero time.

What sets the ceiling you can never beat?

Only Dee has the till key. Every sandwich, however many staff you hire, passes through that one till one at a time. The share of the work that must happen one thread at a time is called the serial fraction, and it is written as the letter s. Locks, a single writer, one connection, one shared counter — all of them are s.

Amdahl's law turns that into a ceiling you can compute. With serial fraction s running on N threads, the best possible speed-up is 1 / (s + (1−s)/N). As N grows, that approaches 1/s and never gets past it. The point of the law is that the ceiling is a number you can work out before you buy anything.

Amdahl's curve flattens. Real systems come back down, and that needs a second cost. Every thread must keep a consistent view of shared data, and the number of pairs of threads that have to agree grows with the square of the thread count — N². Add that term and you get the Universal Scalability Law: speed-up = N / (1 + s(N−1) + κN(N−1)). The Greek letter kappa, κ, is the price of that agreement. Only the USL predicts a curve that turns downward.

Which two words exist because measurement fools you?

Coordinated omission is timing from the moment you got served rather than from the moment you started waiting, so the delays are systematically left out of the data. You met it in queueing-theory as a load-generator problem. F7 shows you its far more common form, which lives in your own application code.

Microbenchmark is a benchmark of a very small piece of code. It needs its own word and its own warning because at that size the JIT compiler's ability to prove your result is unused, and delete it, exceeds your ability to notice (F9).

8. Which two pictures should you carry in your head?

In one breath: Carry the sandwich shop, because it is right about most things and you can reason with it in a meeting. Then carry the seven buckets, because they are what you actually write down. Then learn the four places the shop lies.

What does the shop get right?

The shop gets you further than it has any right to. Four things from it are true of every service you will ever tune.

  • The queue outside the door is real, and it is usually the biggest number.
  • There is exactly one toaster, and it is the whole problem.
  • Hiring more staff into a small kitchen makes things worse.
  • The only way to find the toaster is to follow one customer with a stopwatch and write down every interval.

Here is the precise version, and it has two halves that you look at together.

Where does the shop lie to you?

Four places, and every one of them matters.

Lie 1: in a shop the bottleneck stays put. In a JVM it moves the moment you touch it — buy Bharat a second toaster and Dee's till becomes the constraint. The shop does that much too. But a JVM's bottleneck also moves without you. The same service is compiler-bound in its first ten seconds, CPU-bound at midday, and collector-bound at month-end when the cache is full. A performance diagnosis has a shelf life.

Lie 2: in a shop the stations are independent. Bharat toasting does not slow Chen down. But in a JVM the garbage collector's threads and your handler threads run on the same CPUs. So choosing a collector that works concurrently does not make its work free. It moves the work out of a pause and into a tax on every one of your requests. At a 200 MB/s allocation rate, ZGC in this model spends 40% of the machine on collecting while your code runs [MODEL]. Its longest freeze is 6.4 ms against Serial's 519 ms, an 81× improvement, and yet its p99 is only 2.6× better. The tax ate most of the win.

Lie 3: the staff do not rewrite themselves while working. Your code does. For the first few hundred calls a method is interpreted, then compiled roughly, then compiled well. Any number you collect during that window describes a program that no longer exists.

Lie 4: in a shop, one more worker is never worse than none. In a JVM it routinely is. Six people in a four-person kitchen is a mild inconvenience. But 128 threads on 8 cores did 29.6% less work than 8 threads [MODEL]. No kitchen analogy predicts a curve that turns downward, and stage 10's second formula is the one that does.

Method v3 — why does asking for shorter pauses make p99 worse?

Watch it fail. [JVM] MaxGCPauseMillis is a goal, not a promise. The main lever G1 has for meeting it is to shrink the young generation, so each collection has less to do and each pause is shorter. Frequently you get a worse p99 than you started with. The number she asked for went down. The thing she wanted got worse.

The deeper mistake is that she tuned the symptom. A collection pause has exactly two dimensions, and they have completely different causes.

  • How often a collection happens = how much garbage you make ÷ how much room there is to make it in.

Method v5 — why does buying more threads eventually subtract?

Everything is now honest, the bottleneck is CPU, and CPU is a thing you can buy. So Meera doubles the threads, and then doubles them again.

It breaks the way stage 4's table already showed, and here is the formula behind it. Two costs grow as you add threads.

The first is the serial fraction, Dee's till key, written s. Some part of the work happens one thread at a time. Amdahl's law says speedup = 1 / (s + (1−s)/N). As N grows this approaches 1/s and stops. At s = 5% the ceiling is 20×. You get 9.1× at 16 threads, 15.4× at 64, and 18.6× at 256 [MODEL].

The second is coherence, written κ. Every thread must keep a consistent view of shared data, and the number of pairs that have to agree grows with N². The Universal Scalability Law adds that term.

speedup(N) = N / ( 1 + s(N−1) + κN(N−1) )

🔮 Predict: A program has a 5% serial fraction and a small coherence cost, κ = 0.1%. Amdahl says the ceiling is 20× and that 256 threads should get you 18.6× of it. What does the USL say 256 threads actually gets you?

Reveal

3.2× — which is less than the 8.0× you get from 16 threads [MODEL]. The best this program can ever do is 9.0×, at 31 threads. Every thread after that subtracts.

Amdahl's error here is not that it is too optimistic about the ceiling. It is that it has no term at all for the cost of threads coordinating with each other, so it can only ever flatten. Real systems come back down.

If you guessed something near 18×, you were trusting Amdahl, which is the normal mistake and the reason the USL exists. The peak is computable in advance: N* = sqrt((1−s)/κ).

Amdahl flattens. The USL turns downward, and its peak is computable before you buy anything, at N* = sqrt((1−s)/κ). At s = 5%, κ = 0.1% [MODEL]:

Threads 1 16 31 64 128 256
Amdahl says 1.0× 9.1× — 15.4× — 18.6×

256 threads does less work than 16. That is not a modelling artefact. It is why "just add more" eventually stops working everywhere, in every system. And it is why the honest answer to "how many threads?" is a number you find by sweeping, not a number you increase.

The fifth repair. Work out the ceiling before adding capacity. If your serial fraction is 10%, no amount of hardware gets you past 10×. Shrink s instead of shopping.

F3 — why did the container die without saying anything?

Priya is paged. The container exited with code 137. There is no OutOfMemoryError, no stack trace, and nothing at all in the application log. Somebody had set -Xmx to the container's memory limit.

Why: the Java heap is not the process. On top of the heap sit thread stacks, metaspace, the code cache, the collector's own data structures, direct byte buffers, and whatever native libraries allocate. When the process exceeds the container's memory limit, the kernel's OOM killer terminates it. The JVM never gets a chance to say anything, because it was not the one that noticed.

Nothing survives this. The process is gone, and everything in flight with it.

In production: [JVM] use -XX:MaxRAMPercentage, which sizes the heap as a share of the limit, rather than a hard -Xmx derived from it. That leaves room for the JVM's own non-heap needs. Turn on Native Memory Tracking (-XX:NativeMemoryTracking=summary) when the resident size and the heap size disagree. And always set -XX:+HeapDumpOnOutOfMemoryError, so that when it is a real heap exhaustion you have something to look at. jvm-garbage-collectors flagged this one too. It is repeated here because exit 137 with no stack trace is a signature worth recognising instantly.

F4 — is it a heap problem, or is it a garbage problem?

The numbers [MODEL]: at 64 KB per request the collector ran twice in the run. At 512 KB it ran 20 times. Eight times the garbage, eight times the collections, exactly as eden ÷ allocation rate predicts. Quadrupling the heap took it back down to 2.

Why: collection frequency is your allocation rate divided by your available room. Both of those terms are yours to change, and only one of them is free. The collector keeps up until it cannot, at which point pauses lengthen and eventually you get an allocation stall.

Correctness is untouched, and no reachable object is ever lost. What you lose is throughput, because collector CPU is CPU your requests do not get. You also lose tail latency.

In production, allocation profiling points straight at the line. Use JFR's ObjectAllocationSample, or async-profiler in allocation mode. The usual culprits are string concatenation in a loop, boxing in a hot path, building intermediate collections that are immediately discarded, and logging that formats a message before checking whether the level is enabled. Reach for allocation before heap. It fixes frequency and pause length, where heap fixes only frequency.

F5 — why is the machine 11% busy and the service still slow?

Correctness stays intact — that is what the lock is for. What you lose is any hope of scaling, because this is Amdahl's s and therefore a hard ceiling.

In production you find it in thread dumps showing many threads BLOCKED on the same monitor, or in JFR's JavaMonitorEnter events. The fixes, in order of preference:

  1. do less inside the section;
  2. never hold it across I/O;
  3. shard it, so different keys take different locks;
  4. replace it with a compare-and-set loop;
  5. make the data immutable, so no lock is needed at all.

It moved 92% of the samples from the method that is actually hot to a method that barely computes at all.

Nothing here is broken. Each tool reports exactly what it measures. What is lost is the correspondence between the profile and reality, silently, with no warning in the output.

In production, prefer a profiler that does not need safepoints. async-profiler uses AsyncGetCallTrace and perf events, and JFR's method sampling is also not safepoint-limited. And note the third row: when the CPU profile and the wall clock disagree, believe the wall clock about latency and the CPU profile about CPU. They are answering different questions.

F9 — why does the benchmark say the method is free?

Ben writes a loop that calls his method a million times and times it. It reports 0.3 nanoseconds per call, which would be about one clock cycle. The team concludes the method is free.

Why: the result was never used, so the JIT proved the whole computation had no effect and deleted it. That is dead-code elimination. Four related traps sit next to it. The input was a constant, so it was folded at compile time. The first iterations ran interpreted and dominated the average. Escape analysis removed the allocation Ben was trying to measure. And the loop was compiled with knowledge of one call site that will not exist in production.

Nothing was measured, so nothing is at risk yet. What is lost is the entire result — and, much worse, the decision that gets based on it.

In production, use JMH. It exists specifically to defeat these: warmup iterations, forked JVMs, Blackhole consumption of results, and @State objects the compiler cannot fold. And treat any microbenchmark result as evidence about a microbenchmark, until it turns up in a whole-system number.

F10 — why did the low-pause collector make p99 worse?

Arjun switches to ZGC to fix the tail. The freezes get dramatically shorter and the p99 gets worse. Here is the comparison [MODEL, 1.2 GB retained, 1 MB of garbage per request].

Collector median p99 Longest freeze CPU tax
Serial 43 ms 573 ms 519 ms 0%
Parallel 41 ms 207 ms 153 ms 0%
G1 41 ms 200 ms 67 ms 20%
ZGC 44 ms 220 ms 6.4 ms 40%

ZGC cut the longest freeze by 81× and its p99 came out worse than G1's.

Why: a concurrent collector does not delete the work. It moves the work off the pause and onto the CPU bill, and that bill scales with how much garbage you make. At this allocation rate it is eating 40% of the machine, and that CPU comes out of your requests. Drop the allocation rate and ZGC becomes cheap.

The pause guarantee is intact — ZGC really does keep its freezes tiny. What is lost is throughput, and with it the very p99 Arjun was trying to buy.

In production: [JVM] G1 has been the default since JDK 9, and it is the right answer until numbers say otherwise. ZGC is generational by default since JDK 23, and its non-generational mode was removed in JDK 24. It is for large heaps with strict tail requirements, and on real workloads it typically costs 5–15% throughput plus extra footprint. A collector change is never a free upgrade. It is a trade, and you need the before-and-after decomposition to know whether you won.

F11 — why does adding threads take work away?

Meera sweeps the pool size under a fixed load. At 8 threads the service answers 300.0/s. At 128 threads it answers 211.3/s — 29.6% less work on identical hardware [MODEL]. The p99 goes from 1,623 ms to 8,798 ms, and each thread's work inflates by 1.75×.

Why: past the point where the cores are saturated, extra threads add no capacity at all, but they do add coherence traffic and scheduling churn. This is the USL's κN(N−1) term, and it is why the curve turns down.

Correctness is fine. What is lost is throughput, latency, and the intuition that more is more.

In production, find the peak empirically. Sweep the pool size under a fixed load and keep the size at the top of the curve, not past it. And hold the opposite lesson at the same time, because queueing-theory cuts the other way: too few threads makes you a smaller service, and that lab pinned a 10-server pool's throughput at 50/s. Both cliffs are real. The target is the flat bit between them, and it is usually wider than people fear.

F12 — why did a week of optimisation move nothing?

Somebody makes a method twice as fast. It was 2% of the request. They saved 1%. Nobody can see it on a graph. This is the very first thing anyone does.

Why: they never computed the ceiling. Amdahl applies to optimisation as well as to parallelism. Suppose the part you are attacking is fraction p of the whole. Making it k times faster gives you 1 / ((1−p) + p/k) overall. As k → ∞ you asymptote at 1/(1−p). At p = 2% that ceiling is 1.02×.

In production, compute that number before the work starts. It costs a minute, and it has cancelled more badly-aimed weeks than any other single habit in this document.

F13 — why is throughput perfect while everybody times out?

Meera's queue has no limit, and the arrival rate goes past capacity. The dashboard looks completely fine.

The numbers [MODEL]: 400/s offered against a capacity near 300/s. After 40 seconds the queue held 487 requests, 1,688 requests had been refused, throughput sat at exactly 300/s, and p99 was 1,675 ms.

Why: an unbounded queue converts an overload into unbounded latency instead of into refusals. Every request that eventually times out consumed a handler thread for nothing.

Throughput stays intact, which is precisely why the dashboard looks fine. What is lost is goodput — the answers delivered within the deadline, as opposed to merely delivered. queueing-theory pinned this down exactly. Across the whole range of queue sizes, throughput never moved: 7,711 either way. Goodput ran from 64 requests answered in time with an unbounded queue, to 7,033 with a cap of 5.

In production, bound every queue, and size the bound with that lab's rule maxQueue ≈ deadline/S − c. [JVM] The defaults are against you here. A ThreadPoolExecutor with a bare LinkedBlockingQueue is unbounded, and Spring Boot's spring.task.execution.pool.queue-capacity defaults to Integer.MAX_VALUE.

12. What can tuning promise, and what can it never promise?

In one breath: No flag changes what your program computes. Almost everything else people assume is guaranteed is not. And every win in this lab was paid for with something.

What is guaranteed, no matter what you set?

Correctness is not a tuning parameter. No flag in this document changes what your program computes. The Java Memory Model's guarantees, from java-memory-model, hold identically under every collector, every compiler tier, and every thread count. A race that exists at -Xint exists at C2. The compiler makes it more likely to show; it does not create it.

No reachable object is ever collected. That is jvm-garbage-collectors. Dee never throws away a sandwich somebody is still waiting for. Every collector here is safe. They differ only in when, and in what size pieces, you pay.

The JIT will eventually compile hot code, and its optimisations preserve semantics. Deoptimisation is the mechanism that keeps that true when a speculation turns out to be wrong.

Little's Law holds exactly. L = λW is an identity, not a model. It assumes nothing about arrival pattern, service distribution or ordering, and queueing-theory checked it to 0.01 jobs. Your pool must be at least λ × W threads. That is a floor, and it is exact.

What is not guaranteed, though people assume it is?

  • That a flag does the same thing in the next JDK. Flags get removed: -XX:+AggressiveOpts went in 12, CMS in 14, non-generational ZGC in 24. Defaults change, and ergonomics rewrite your settings based on the machine. A tuning inherited from a JDK 8 service is a liability, not a head start.
  • That a p99 improvement is stable. A percentile is a property of a distribution, and the distribution moves with your data, your traffic mix and your cache state. A 20% p99 win seen over five minutes may be noise. The honest test is whether the bucket you attacked shrank.
  • That your benchmark predicts production. Different data shape, different cache hit rate, different neighbours on the machine, different serial fraction.
  • That adding resources helps at all. F11 shows it can subtract.
  • That the bottleneck stays where you found it. It moves the moment you fix it. That is what fixing it means.

What did you trade to get the win?

Every improvement in this lab was paid for. Being able to name the currency is most of what separates tuning from cargo-culting.

The win Paid for with
Shorter pauses (a concurrent collector) Throughput and footprint — up to 40% of the machine at high allocation rates [MODEL]
Fewer collections (a bigger heap) Footprint, and a longer worst-case pause when a full one does happen
Both, by allocating less Engineering time, and sometimes less readable code
Higher throughput (batching, bigger queues) Latency, always
Lower latency under load (shedding, small queues) Throughput and some refused requests — traded deliberately, for goodput
More parallelism Coherence cost, which grows as N² and eventually reverses the gain
Faster startup (AOT caches, CDS) Build complexity, and an artefact that must be kept in step with the code

What is the one sentence to keep?

jvm-garbage-collectors ended on this: the work is conserved; you are choosing when and in what size pieces to pay it. That generalises to this whole subject, with one important exception.

Tuning moves work around. Buying Bharat a second toaster does not make Omar's sandwich need less toasting; it stops him queuing for it. There are only three ways to genuinely remove work.

  1. Do less. A better algorithm, fewer allocations, fewer round trips, one query instead of N+1.
  2. Do it at a better time. Cache it (caching), batch it, precompute it, or do it asynchronously off the request path.
  3. Do not do it at all. Admission control, load shedding, returning a cached-but-stale answer, saying no early instead of slowly.

Every one of those is a code or design change, and none of them is a flag. That is the honest summary of the subject: flags redistribute; only design removes.

13. What changes when you leave this lab's model?

In one breath: The model has one queue. A real service has six, and you are almost certainly configured about the wrong one. Then: nine flags that earn their place, a longer list that does not, and four things for the dashboard.

Everything in this section is [JVM] — documented behaviour of real virtual machines, not something this lab ran. Check anything decision-critical against your exact JDK's release notes. Flags move between versions more often than people expect.

How many queues are you actually standing in?

This lab's model is one process, one pool, one lock, one downstream call, one heap. It is Omar's single queue outside a single door.

A real service has five or six queues in series, and it is configured about exactly one of them. That is queueing-theory's finding, and it is the single most useful correction to the mental model here. The queues are: the socket accept backlog, the web server's worker pool, your executor's queue, the connection pool to the database, the database's own queue, and the thread pool of whatever it calls in turn.

Little's Law applies to each one separately. The one you tuned is usually not the one that is full.

Which flags earn their place?

Nine of them. Set these and stop.

Flag Why it earns its place
-XX:MaxRAMPercentage=<n> Size the heap as a share of the container limit, never as a hard -Xmx copied from it — F3. Leave room for stacks, metaspace, code cache and direct buffers.
-Xlog:gc*:file=...:time,uptime,level,tags Turn it on before you need it. The cost is negligible and the alternative is asking for a repeat of the incident.
-Xlog:safepoint* (when investigating) The other log. It separates "reaching safepoint" from "at safepoint" — the only way to see F6.
-XX:+HeapDumpOnOutOfMemoryError -XX:HeapDumpPath=... The difference between a diagnosis and a shrug.
-XX:StartFlightRecording=... or JFR on demand One recording covering collections, allocation, locks, exceptions, thread states and compilation, at very low overhead, already in the JDK.
-XX:+ExitOnOutOfMemoryError (usually) A JVM that has exhausted its heap rarely recovers usefully. Failing fast and letting the orchestrator replace it beats limping.
-XX:ActiveProcessorCount=<n> (only when needed) For when the JVM's view of the machine is wrong and you cannot fix the cgroup — F2. Prefer fixing the container.
-XX:+AlwaysPreTouch (latency-critical only) Touch every heap page at startup so the first requests do not pay for page faults. Costs startup time; buys a smoother first minute.
A collector choice, made once, with evidence G1 by default. Parallel for batch jobs where throughput is everything and pauses do not matter. Serial for genuinely small containers. ZGC for large heaps with strict tail requirements, accepting the CPU tax — F10.

Which flags do not earn their place?

  • -XX:MaxGCPauseMillis set low. A goal, not a guarantee. G1 meets it by shrinking the young generation, which means more collections, more promotion, and often a worse p99. That is method v3.
  • -Xmn, NewRatio or SurvivorRatio on G1. G1 sizes the young generation adaptively to meet its pause goal. Pinning it fights the collector and disables the adaptation you are relying on.
  • -XX:+UseStringDeduplication without having first shown that duplicate strings are a meaningful share of your live set.
  • Anything copied from a blog post about a different JDK. -XX:+AggressiveOpts was removed in JDK 12, CMS in JDK 14, non-generational ZGC in JDK 24. A JVM will happily ignore an unrecognised flag or, worse, refuse to start.
  • -Xint or -Xcomp. Debugging tools. -Xcomp compiles everything with no profile data, which is usually slower than letting tiering work.
  • A bigger thread pool, as a reflex. That is F11.

What belongs on the dashboard?

Four things, and the last two are the ones usually missing.

  1. Latency percentiles taken at the edge — p50, p95, p99, p99.9. Never the mean as your primary latency signal, and never taken from inside the handler (F7).
  2. Throughput and goodput, separately. Goodput is answers delivered within the deadline. queueing-theory watched throughput stay constant across a range where goodput fell 100-fold.
  3. time_in_queue. If you add one metric this year, add this one. It is the boundary between "the service is too small" and "the service is too slow", and those have completely different fixes.
  4. Spare capacity, and the rate of change of queue depth, rather than utilisation. queueing-theory: latency is set by spare capacity, not by capacity, and utilisation is a lagging, non-linear proxy for it.

Plus, always on: garbage-collection pause count and duration, allocation rate, container CPU-throttling counters, and resident set size against the container limit.

What do virtual threads change, and what do they leave alone?

java-virtual-threads made blocking cheap. Here is precisely what that does to this lab.

  • It removes the "waiting for a thread" bucket as a capacity problem. You no longer need a pool sized by Little's Law, because you can have a million threads. But the queue does not disappear. It moves from a visible ThreadPoolExecutor queue to an invisible pile of parked continuations. So you must add backpressure deliberately, with a Semaphore or a concurrency limiter, where the pool used to provide it for free.
  • It changes nothing about CPU. A CPU-bound service gets no benefit whatever. The cores are the cores.
  • It changes nothing about locks. Dee's till key is unchanged. F5 is identical on virtual threads: same memory model, same monitors.
  • It adds to the collector's job. A virtual thread's stack lives on the heap, so a million of them is a million live objects the collector must keep and scan. jvm-garbage-collectors flagged this, and it is real.
  • Pinning brings the old ceiling back without warning. It is caused by a native frame, or by synchronized before JDK 24 and JEP 491.

What does this lab's model deliberately leave out?

Each of these has caused real incidents, and none of them is in the simulator.

  • Class loading and metaspace. A large application spends real time loading classes at startup, and metaspace has its own limit and its own OOM.
  • On-stack replacement (OSR). A long-running loop can be compiled while it is executing. The model compiles at method granularity only.
  • Deoptimisation storms. A rare path taken under load can knock hot code back to interpreted, repeatedly.
  • NUMA. On a multi-socket machine, which socket's memory an object lives in matters.
  • Page faults, swap and transparent huge pages. A heap the kernel has swapped out produces pauses no JVM tool can explain.
  • The CPU cache hierarchy and false sharing. Real cross-talk has structure that the model's single κ coefficient does not.
  • Connection pools, downstream queues and cascading failure. The "database" here is a fixed delay that never queues, never times out and never sheds.
  • Network variability, retries and retry storms. queueing-theory watched a metastable failure grow to 37,115 queued jobs from a single burst. Nothing here can do that.
  • Security, TLS handshakes, serialization costs, logging I/O — all of which routinely turn out to be the answer.

The model is a teaching instrument for the shape of the problem, not a capacity planner. Its ratios are the lesson. Its milliseconds are not.

  • queueing-theory gave you the two formulas that generate this whole subject. Little's Law, L = λW, is how you size a thread pool. Kingman's formula is why a service at 80% utilisation and a service at 95% utilisation are different animals. You already know that latency is set by spare capacity, not capacity, that 1/(1−ρ) is the multiplier, and that coordinated omission is a way of measuring that hides exactly the stalls you care about. All of that is load-bearing here.
  • java-multithreading gave you locks, contention, the convoy, the thread-pool sizing arithmetic D × ceil(N/W), and the rule never hold a lock across I/O. In this lab that same lock becomes a number: the share of your work that has to happen one thread at a time. That number turns out to put a hard ceiling on how fast your service can ever be.
  • java-virtual-threads taught that blocking became cheap. It did not become free, and it did not become invisible. Stage 13 comes back to what virtual threads do and do not change about everything in this document.
  • io-multiplexing established that a server's real job is waiting. Its headline finding was that 2,000 idle connections cost 0 ms of CPU with the right mechanism. That is the reason the biggest block in this lab's default breakdown is usually waiting on something else. It is why Omar's shop spends most of its day waiting for a bread delivery rather than making sandwiches.
  • work-pressure-and-deadlines and caching show up in stage 12: the fastest work is work you did not do, and the cheapest millisecond is the one you never spent.

JVM Performance Tuning — Solutions

Numbers marked [MODEL] come from the simulator in visualization.html, run headlessly. [HAND] means worked from stated rules — check the arithmetic yourself. [JVM] is documented real-JVM behaviour, not measured here.

Your numbers on the page will match these exactly: the engine is deterministic, so the same settings always produce the same run.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Performance tuning is finding out where a request's time actually goes and then changing the one thing that dominates it. "Add some JVM flags" is usually wrong because a flag can only change how the JVM spends time it is already spending — and in most services the largest block is something no flag touches (waiting on a downstream call, waiting for a free thread, waiting for one lock). You have to know which block is biggest before any flag can be the right answer, and once you know, the answer is often not a flag at all.

1.2 Suppose 97 customers are served in 40 ms and 3 wait 2 seconds. The average is about 99 ms, which describes nobody: not the 97, and certainly not the 3. Worse, the average is stable — you can double the number of 2-second waits and the average barely twitches, so an alert on it fires long after the service has become unusable. Alert on a percentile instead: p99 says "one customer in a hundred waits at least this long", which is a sentence about a real person. Keep the mean only for capacity arithmetic, where it is exactly the right number.

1.3 At any moment there is exactly one bottleneck, and every change to anything else buys nothing. It implies that "add more memory and see if it helps" is not a plan but a lottery ticket — and one you can check for free before buying, by looking at how much of the request is currently spent on memory-related work. On the lab's scenario 1 that share was 0.0 ms out of 117.6 ms, so quadrupling the heap moved p99 from 1,327 ms to 1,327 ms [MODEL].

1.4 A CPU profiler samples threads while they are executing on a core. A thread asleep in a socket read is not on a core and produces no samples at all. So for a handler doing 6 ms of work around a 180 ms database call — 4.5% of its elapsed time on-CPU [MODEL] — the profiler's output is a perfectly accurate description of 4.5% of the problem, and nothing in the output says so.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 [MODEL] On the defaults the widest bucket is waiting on the database, 25.0 ms, with running your code at 19.9 ms just behind it. Median 41 ms, p95 42 ms, p99 248 ms.

2.2 [MODEL] On Slowest 5% it flips: running your code is 93.7 ms and the database is still 25.0 ms. The sentence: the typical request is slow because it waits; the slow requests are slow because they compute — a small share of requests need several times the usual CPU, and that population, not the queue and not the collector, is what makes this p99. Two different diagnoses from one run, which is why the toggle exists.

2.3 The Handler threads row: busy / total on the right, the bar showing what share are busy. Read it together with the row beneath it, Waiting for a thread, which is the queue depth. The pool is too small when all threads are busy and the queue is non-zero and growing — either number alone proves nothing. All threads busy with an empty queue is a pool being used efficiently.

2.4 Whatever it says — the point is that the sentence names a phase and gives you the per-bucket split for that one request. Off-CPU is everything except running your code and code not yet compiled: the database wait, the lock wait, the queue wait, the frozen time, and ready-to-run-with-no-core.

2.5 (a) The Allocation → collections row, and the line under the resource sheet: "A collection every N s". (b) "Longest freeze so far" on that same line. (c) "Total time frozen: X% of the clock", also there. All three are on the resource sheet because all three are properties of a resource, not of a request.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 [MODEL] p99 goes from 1,327 ms to 1,266 ms — a 4.6% change, which is nothing. Doubling again to 128 threads gives the same 1,266 ms. The lock bucket actually goes up slightly (87.3 → 92.0 ms), which is the tell: more threads means a longer line for the same single-file door. Threads are not what is scarce here; permission to enter the one-at-a-time section is.

3.2 [MODEL] With no database call and 40 ms of CPU work, the bottleneck becomes running your code (44.3 ms of a 51 ms average). Median falls to 33 ms — the database wait is gone — but p99 rises to 536 ms, because the heavy requests now cost 40 ms × their multiplier with nothing to dilute it, and CPU utilisation has climbed to 38%. Note what happened: the median improved and the tail got worse. If you had only been watching the median you would have called this a win.

3.3 [HAND] Little's Law: L = λW = 200 × 0.043 s = 8.6 threads busy on average. That is a floor, not a setting. [MODEL] measured:

24 192.7 /s 41 ms 173 ms

Eight threads — just below the floor — does not make the service slower, it makes it a smaller service: throughput is pinned at 174.7/s and the queue does the rest. Nine barely works but with a p99 of 226 ms, because the average being 8.6 means the instantaneous demand is often above 9. From twelve onwards nothing changes at all: 24 does not beat 16. The rule from queueing-theory: size to λS ÷ ρ_target, so 8.6 ÷ 0.7 ≈ 13 threads.

3.4 [MODEL] This one is designed to catch you.

defaults (128 KB/req) 248 ms 2 young 3.7 ms every 10.24 s
8× the garbage (1 MB/req) 253 ms 22 young + 2 old 17.0 ms every 1.28 s
…and 8× the heap 249 ms 2 young 4.1 ms every 10.24 s

Eight times the garbage produced eleven times the collections and moved p99 by five milliseconds. If you predicted a disaster, you predicted the mechanism correctly and the consequence wrongly — and the reason is the whole lesson: garbage collection was never this service's bottleneck. It was 0.0 ms of the average request. Eight times almost nothing is still almost nothing. The heap change then undid the frequency exactly, which confirms interval = eden ÷ allocation rate [HAND: 512 × 0.30 = 153.6 MB of allocation area; at 128 KB × 120/s = 15.0 MB/s that is 10.24 s].

3.5 [HAND] s = 10%, so the ceiling is 1/0.10 = 10× — for any number of cores, forever. At 16 cores Amdahl gives 6.40×; at 128 cores, 9.34×. Eight times the hardware buys 1.46×. The implication: the proposal is worth at most 46% and costs 8× the machine, so the money belongs in reducing the 10%, not in buying cores. And this is the optimistic number — it ignores coherence entirely.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 [MODEL] You need three things at once: the code must not be compiled, there must be no database wait to dilute it, and the service must not be saturated (or the queue swamps everything). For example interpreter only, database wait 0 ms, 60 requests/second, 32 threads → code not yet compiled 80.2 ms against running your code 22.3 ms. Ticking Restart cold, 40 req/s, no database call, 16 cores gives 65.5 ms vs 17.0 ms in the first four seconds.

It does not happen on the defaults for two reasons. First, the defaults start warm — fresh is off, so the handler is already at C2 and the multiplier is 1.0×. Second, even cold, the 25 ms database wait is unaffected by any compiler, so it dilutes the penalty. The more of your request is waiting, the less warmup matters.

4.2 [MODEL] The Share of the work under one lock slider, at about 30%: lock becomes the widest bucket at 25.2 ms and p99 goes to 705 ms.

The interesting part is what happens if you keep going. At 45% the widest bucket is no longer lock but queue (p99 1,621 ms), and at 60% more so. That is the answer to "which resource is a lock": a lock is a single-server queue, so it obeys queueing-theory directly — and once its utilisation passes 1, it stops being a delay inside the service and becomes the capacity limit of the service. The waiting then relocates to in front of the thread pool, where it is much harder to attribute to the lock that caused it.

4.3 [MODEL] Attempts, from cores 16 / quota 2 / pool 32 / 200 req-s, p99 5,214 ms:

Change p99 Throughput
nothing 5,214 ms 74.0 /s
remove the database wait 6,031 ms (worse) 75.0 /s
pool 32 → 8 2,661 ms 104.0 /s
offered load 200 → 60 /s 325 ms 63.3 /s
work per request 18 ms → 4 ms 75 ms 196.0 /s
work 4 ms and load 120 /s 80 ms 109.3 /s

Only two things worked, and they are the same thing: reduce the CPU demanded per second — either by serving fewer requests or by making each one cheaper. Shrinking the pool helped a little (less oversubscription) but could not fix capacity that is not there. Removing the database wait made it worse, because now every thread wants a CPU all the time.

What this tells you: CPU starvation is a capacity problem, and tuning cannot create capacity. The only levers are do-less-work, serve-fewer-requests, or get-more-CPU. Which is exactly why the real fix is to correct the container limit, and why F2 is worth being able to recognise in seconds rather than days.

4.4 [MODEL] With cross-talk at 0.00%, the same sweep:

Pool Throughput p99 Work inflation
8 300.0 /s 1,434 ms 1.00×
16 300.0 /s 1,445 ms 1.04×
32 300.0 /s 2,350 ms 1.11×
64 290.0 /s 4,028 ms 1.26×

Most of it survives, and that is the finding. At 128 threads the throughput loss against the 8-thread baseline was 88.7 requests/second with cross-talk and is still 77.7 without it — so cross-talk accounted for only about an eighth of the damage (211.3 → 222.3 /s). The rest comes from the model's oversubscription penalty — the cost of an operating system moving 128 runnable threads across 8 cores. So there are two independent mechanisms and both are real: coherence traffic between running threads, and scheduling churn from runnable ones.

It is liveness that is at risk, not safety. Nothing computes a wrong answer; the system simply does less of it, and eventually so little that requests time out. That is the same distinction java-multithreading drew, one level up.

4.5 [HAND, tab 2] With s = 0 and κ = 0 the curve is the straight line speedup = N — perfect scaling, no ceiling, forever. Raising only cross-talk gives a curve that rises, peaks at N* = sqrt((1−s)/κ) and then falls; raising only the serial share gives a curve that rises and flattens at 1/s, never turning down. So: the serial term sets how high you can ever get; the coherence term sets where you should stop. They are different questions, and a system can be limited by either.

5.2 [HAND] Allocation rate = 256 KB × 300/s = 76,800 KB/s = 75.0 MB/s. Allocation area = 1,024 MB × 0.30 = 307.2 MB.

  • Young collection every 307.2 ÷ 75.0 = **4.096 s**
  • Old-generation collection every 8 × 4.096 = **32.77 s**

Halve the allocation per request → 37.5 MB/s → young every 8.192 s, old every 65.54 s. All three numbers scale exactly linearly, because interval = area ÷ rate and only the rate changed. That is the whole reason allocation rate is the first lever: it is the one term you can change by a factor of ten with a code review, and it moves frequency proportionally.

The sentence: with a 150 ms time-to-safepoint, 96% of ZGC's advantage evaporates, because you are no longer paying for collection, you are paying to stop the threads — and both collectors pay that identically. So if your time-to-safepoint is bad, fixing it comes before changing collector, and choosing a low-pause collector without checking it is buying a sports car for a road with a 20 mph limit.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 The timer starts after the request has already waited. Concretely: 40 pods, a pool of 16 threads each, and a traffic spike takes arrivals past capacity. Requests now sit in the executor's queue for four seconds and are then handled in 40 ms. The Timer records 40 ms. p99 stays green all hour. [MODEL] at 340 requests/second against a pool of 16: in-handler p99 180 ms, arrival-to-answer p99 379 ms — and the gap widens as fast as the queue does (at the load in the README's scenario 8 it was 120 ms against 880 ms, 7.3×). The excluded time is time_in_queue, everything between arrival and dispatch. This is coordinated omission. Fix: measure at the edge, and add time_in_queue as its own metric.

6.2 The hidden assumption is that CPU samples are a proxy for elapsed time. They are not; they are a proxy for CPU time. [HAND] If the service is genuinely CPU-bound then 61% halved gives 1/((1−0.61) + 0.61/2) = 1.439×, a 44% improvement, so the "~30%" is at least the right order. But in a service where only 4.5% of elapsed time is on-CPU, that method is 0.61 × 4.5% = 2.7% of the request, and halving it gives 1/((1−0.027) + 0.027/2) = 1.0137× — a 1.4% improvement. Same profile, same 61%, same optimisation, a factor of 32 difference in the outcome, decided entirely by a number the profiler never printed.

6.3 Any of:

  1. Dead-code elimination — the return value is unused, so the JIT proved the whole call has no effect and deleted it. Rule it out by consuming the result (JMH's Blackhole) and checking the number changes.
  2. Constant folding — input never changes, so the result was computed once at compile time. Rule it out by varying the input from state the compiler cannot see through.
  3. Warmup / profile pollution — the loop was measured across the interpreted phase, or C2 compiled it with the knowledge that there is exactly one call site and one implementation, which will not be true in production. Rule it out with warmup iterations and forked JVMs.

A fourth worth knowing: escape analysis may have removed the very allocation you were trying to measure. All four are what JMH exists for.

6.4 F6. Run ZGC, whose collection pauses are genuinely under a millisecond, and have one thread inside a tight counted loop over a large primitive array that the JIT compiled with no safepoint check in it. Every pause now costs the collector's ~1 ms plus however long that loop takes to finish — [MODEL] longest freeze 181.4 ms and p99 2,189 ms, with the collector's own work unchanged. The GC log shows sub-millisecond pauses throughout and is telling the truth. The runbook should have named -Xlog:safepoint*, which reports reaching the safepoint separately from being at it. (Non-GC operations cause safepoints too — thread dumps, class redefinition — so the freeze may not be the collector's fault at all.)

6.5 Two failures:

  1. MaxGCPauseMillis=5 is a goal G1 pursues mainly by shrinking the young generation. Smaller young generation → more frequent collections → more total collector CPU → more premature promotion → a worse p99 than they started with, while the metric they asked for looks satisfied.
  2. -Xmx equal to the container's memory limit leaves nothing for thread stacks, metaspace, the code cache, GC structures and direct buffers. The process exceeds the limit, the kernel's OOM killer terminates it, and the container exits with code 137 and no stack trace at all — that is the one with no diagnostics. Use -XX:MaxRAMPercentage instead, and turn on -XX:+HeapDumpOnOutOfMemoryError for the case where it really is the heap.

6.6 Little's Law gives the average number of busy threads, and an average is not a maximum. Demand fluctuates around 8.6, so a pool of 9 spends a large share of its time with every thread busy and a queue forming — the pool is running at ρ ≈ 1, which queueing-theory showed is exactly where latency stops being finite. [MODEL] a pool of 8 pinned throughput at 174.7/s (never reaching 200), and a pool of 9 reached 196.7/s but with a p99 of 226 ms against the 173 ms available from 12 threads. The rule: L = λW gives the floor; the setting is λS ÷ ρ_target — at ρ 0.7 that is 8.6 ÷ 0.7 ≈ 13 threads. Then verify there is no upper cliff (F11) before going much beyond it.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

These are design discussions. What follows is one good answer, not the only one.

7.1 Retries turning a refusal into a collapse. Two retries with no delay means a refused request is offered three times, so your offered load triples exactly when you had least capacity. queueing-theory measured this precisely: a one-instant burst into a healthy 85%-utilised system, with clients retrying twice on a 600 ms timeout, produced a queue of 3,249 then 9,983 then 37,115 jobs after 20 minutes and still growing — while the identical burst without retries was absorbed inside four minutes. It also measured that 99% of server time went to clients that had already given up. The system does not recover when the trigger goes away, which is why turning traffic off and on is the standard remedy.

Server side, smallest change: drop requests whose deadline has already passed at dequeue time — check before you start work, not after. This costs one comparison and immediately stops you spending capacity on answers nobody will read. Second: refuse fast and hard rather than gently (that lab measured a shed to 0.85 recovering in 6 minutes where a timid shed to 0.95 took 18).

Client side, smallest change: a retry budget — cap retries at a small percentage of total requests, so retries can never multiply offered load — plus exponential backoff with jitter. And make the operation idempotent so retrying is safe at all (idempotency).

Which first: the server. You control it, it protects you from every client including ones you do not own, and it works immediately. The client fix is better but slower to land and only covers clients you can change.

7.2 Deployments that saturate a cold instance. The instance is slow for a compilation-count reason, not a time reason (F1), so time-based warmup waits are the wrong shape — a low-traffic instance stays cold much longer than a busy one.

  • (a) Ramp by weight, driven by the instance's own readiness. Start at a small share and increase as it earns it. Better still, use a load balancer with least-outstanding-requests rather than round-robin: it naturally sends less to an instance that is responding slowly, with no configuration at all. That single change fixes most of this.
  • (b) To keep the rollout fast, run the ramp in parallel across instances rather than serially, and cap the total fraction of the fleet in ramp state.
  • (c) A broken build is still detected quickly because health and error-rate checks are separate from latency checks — you gate on errors immediately and on latency only after the ramp.

The readiness signal: not "60 seconds elapsed". Use either the compiler's own state (JFR compilation events levelling off) or, more practically, a self-test: on startup, run a fixed number of synthetic requests through the real handler and report ready when their latency has stabilised. That measures the thing you actually care about and works regardless of traffic shape. What you trade: startup time, and the risk that your synthetic requests warm the wrong paths.

7.3 Hiding a 400 ms pause without shortening it. Make the pause happen somewhere the user is not waiting. Options:

  • Take the instance out of rotation before it collects. Some systems do this explicitly, and it is the cleanest version of the idea.
  • Hedged requests: send the request to a second replica if the first has not answered within, say, p95. A pause on one replica is then invisible unless both pause together. You trade extra load (a few percent of duplicated requests) for tail latency, and you require the operation to be idempotent.
  • Stagger the pauses across the fleet so they are never simultaneous — otherwise hedging does nothing.

What breaks if the pause rate doubles: the probability that both the original and the hedge hit a pause rises roughly with the square of the pause probability, so hedging degrades gracefully at first and then suddenly. And the extra load from hedging raises utilisation, which — queueing-theory — raises latency, which triggers more hedges. There is a feedback loop here; cap hedges as a percentage of traffic, exactly like a retry budget.

7.4 A downstream with p50 20 ms and p99 2,000 ms, against your 300 ms SLA. Cheapest first:

  1. Set a timeout well below your SLA (say 200 ms) and have a fallback: cached value, default, or a degraded answer. Cheap, immediate, and it converts a latency failure into an availability decision you control. Gives up: completeness of the answer.
  2. Cache it (caching), if the data tolerates staleness. Removes most calls entirely. Gives up: freshness, and you inherit that lab's invalidation problems.
  3. Hedge — a second call at p95. Gives up: extra load on a service that is already struggling. Only do this if they can take it.
  4. Make the calls concurrent if you make several. Turns a sum of latencies into a maximum. Gives up: burstier load downstream.
  5. Move it off the request path entirely — precompute, or make it asynchronous and return a reference. The largest win and the largest change.

The option that makes it worse: retrying on timeout. Their p99 is 2 seconds because they are struggling; a retry adds load to a struggling service and can tip it into the metastable failure in 7.1. If you must retry, use a budget.

Rung 8 — Design

Sketches. Yours should differ; it should not omit what is below.

8.1 — Observability for the payments service.

Measured at the edge (ingress), per route: request rate, error rate, latency percentiles p50/p95/p99/p99.9 — this is the SLA number and it is the only one that counts as truth. Rules out: nothing on its own; it tells you whether there is a problem, which is a different job.

Measured in the application:

  • time_in_queue (arrival → dispatch). Rules in or out: the waiting for a thread bucket, and by subtraction separates "too small" from "too slow". Highest value per line of code of anything on this list.
  • Pool gauges — active threads, queue depth, rejection count. Rules in: the same bucket, with the cause.
  • Downstream call duration and count, per dependency, per request. Rules in: the waiting on the database bucket, and catches N+1 patterns.
  • Lock/monitor contention from JFR. Rules in: the waiting for a lock bucket.
  • Allocation rate, collection count and pause duration, resident set size against the container limit. Rules in: the frozen bucket and F3.
  • Container CPU throttling counters. Rules in: the ready to run, no core free bucket, and F2. Direct measurement, not inference.

Logs on permanently: -Xlog:gc* to a rotating file (negligible cost), -XX:+HeapDumpOnOutOfMemoryError. On demand: -Xlog:safepoint*, and async-profiler.

Always-on JFR with a low-overhead profile, plus automatic capture triggered by symptom: a rolling recording dumped when p99 exceeds threshold for N seconds, or on OOM. The recording you did not take is the incident you investigate twice.

Alerts: on p99 against the SLA, on error rate, on queue-depth derivative (growing, not merely large), on spare capacity, and on throttling. Not on the mean; not on CPU utilisation alone.

At 3am, read in this order: (1) is it errors or latency? (2) time_in_queue — is the service too small or too slow? (3) the bucket decomposition — which block grew? Everything else follows from those three.

8.2 — Capacity and pool design.

Per request: 12 ms CPU + 3 × 30 ms sequential downstream = 102 ms holding a thread, of which 12 ms is CPU.

  • CPU limit per pod: 4 CPUs ÷ 12 ms = 333 requests/second of CPU capacity, absolute ceiling.
  • Pod count: 3,000 ÷ 333 = 9 pods at 100% — which is the ρ = 1 cliff, so target ρ ≈ 0.65: ≈ 14 pods, round up for failure domains. Per pod that is ~215 requests/second.
  • Pool size: Little's Law L = 215 × 0.102 = 21.9 threads busy on average. That is the floor; at ρ_target 0.7, ≈ 32 threads. On 4 CPUs this is fine precisely because threads are mostly asleep — 32 threads demanding 12 ms of CPU each is well inside 4 cores.
  • Queue capacity: queueing-theory's rule, maxQueue ≈ deadline/S − c = 400 ms / 102 ms − 32 — which is negative, and that is the answer, not a mistake: at this service time there is no useful queue at a 400 ms deadline. Set it small (8–16) purely to absorb jitter, and shed beyond it.
  • Timeout: below the SLA, and propagate the deadline so the downstream calls inherit what is left of it.

If downstream latency triples (30 → 90 ms): hold time becomes 282 ms. CPU demand is unchanged, so pod count is unchanged — the CPU was never the constraint. Little's Law re-derives itself: L = 215 × 0.282 = 60.6, so the pool must roughly triple to ~87 threads or throughput collapses. The queue capacity is now definitely zero and the timeout is now impossible — three sequential 90 ms calls at p50 cannot fit a 400 ms p99 SLA at all, so the sequential design itself has to change (parallelise the independent calls).

The general rule this illustrates: a concurrency limit re-derives itself when service time changes; a rate limit is correct at exactly one downstream latency. Prefer the former.

Platform vs virtual threads. Changes: the pool sizing arithmetic largely goes away — you can run thread-per-request and stop maintaining a number that is wrong whenever downstream latency moves. That is a real and large win here, because the tripling case above breaks a platform-thread design and does not break a virtual-thread one. Does not change: the CPU ceiling (still 333/s per pod), the pod count, the deadline arithmetic, the fact that three sequential 90 ms calls do not fit, or any lock contention. New obligation: the pool was providing backpressure for free; with virtual threads you must add a Semaphore or a concurrency limiter explicitly, or the queue simply becomes invisible. And check for pinning.

8.3 — The reply to the tuning proposal.

Thanks — worth taking seriously, and I want to make sure we spend the effort where it pays. Taking the four separately:

ZGC everywhere. Right when a service has a large heap and a strict tail requirement, and it genuinely does cut pause times enormously. It is not free: it does its collecting while our code runs, so it costs throughput, and the bill scales with how much garbage we make. Before switching I'd want, per service: what share of p99 is currently collector pauses? If it is under about 10%, we cannot win more than 10% and we may lose more than that in throughput.

8 GB heaps everywhere. A bigger heap reduces how often we collect and does nothing about how long each one takes — and it raises our memory bill across the fleet. I'd want the current allocation rate and collection interval first; if we are collecting every ten seconds, there is nothing to buy.

MaxGCPauseMillis=5. This one I'd push back on. It is a goal, not a guarantee, and G1 meets it mainly by shrinking the young generation — more collections, more promotion, and usually a worse p99. It also fights ZGC's own tuning if we do both.

Tripling every thread pool. Depends entirely on whether the pools are the constraint. Where a service is CPU-bound this makes it measurably slower — we have a case where 128 threads did 30% less work than 8 on the same hardware. I'd want queue depth and thread-busy numbers per service first.

What I'd suggest instead, in order. (1) Turn on GC logging and JFR everywhere and add time_in_queue — a week, and it tells us which of these four is even relevant. (2) Decompose p99 on the two worst services. (3) Then change one thing on one service and measure. My guess, and it is only a guess until we have the data, is that allocation rate on the ingestion path is the biggest single win and needs no flags at all.

Happy to be wrong on any of this — the measurements will settle it faster than we can argue.

8.4 — Three regions.

Survives: the seven buckets, the method, the CPU arithmetic, Little's Law, Amdahl, the pool sizing, the deployment ramp, the whole of 8.1's in-process instrumentation.

Must change: the deadline budget — 50 ms each way means a cross-region call costs 100 ms of your 400 ms before anything happens, so deadline propagation stops being good practice and becomes structural. Timeouts must be per-region. Caching becomes much more valuable and much more dangerous. Routing must be locality-aware, and "which region served this" becomes a required dimension on every metric — an aggregate p99 across three regions can hide one region being entirely broken.

The new bucket: waiting for consensus / cross-region replication — time spent waiting not for computation or for one downstream call, but for enough replicas to agree. It behaves differently from the other seven: it does not shrink with more CPU, more threads or less garbage, and its tail is set by the slowest required participant.

Labs that already cover the new failures: quorums-and-replication and raft/paxos for the consensus latency itself; pacelc for the fact that even with no partition you are trading latency for consistency; cap-theorem and split-brain for what happens during one; google-spanner and hybrid-logical-clocks for the clock side; tail-at-scale (not yet learned) for why a request touching many services is slow even when every service is fast.
