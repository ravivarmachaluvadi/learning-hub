Lab 9 · One Machine, Done Right

MVCC and VACUUM — why deleting a row deletes nothing, and why your 2 GB table is 40 GB

Tell a friend: Asha the clerk needs one second to record a land sale, and Ravi the auditor needs forty minutes to read the whole register without the pages changing under him. There is one book, so one of them has to wait. The way out is that Asha stops rubbing things out. She writes a fresh entry and pencils superseded in the margin of the old one, so Ravi still sees the register exactly as it stood when he began. Nothing is overwritten and nothing is erased, which means the bill arrives later: somebody must walk the book and strike out the entries no living reader could still want. That somebody is VACUUM, and every surprise in this lab comes from what it is not allowed to touch.

1. Why is a 2 GB table sitting in 41 GB of disk?

In one breath: A payments team is paged because the disk is nearly full. They delete three million old rows and the disk gets bigger. They run the cleanup command by hand and nothing at all comes back. Every one of those surprises is the intended behaviour of a design you are about to meet.

A payments team gets paged at 03:10. The alert is disk usage on the primary database. At 100% PostgreSQL stops accepting writes, and the payment API stops taking money.

The on-call engineer looks at the biggest table. It is called payment_attempts. The row count has not changed materially in a week. There has been no data import. Nobody added a column.

Read the middle column: it is what each figure on the alert page actually means.

Every one of those four is a direct, predictable consequence of one design decision, and that decision was made to solve a completely different problem: letting a report run for forty seconds without stopping anybody from taking payments.

By the end of this lab you will look at that incident and name the cause in one sentence. You will find the culprit in one query. And you will know which of four possible fixes actually returns the 38 GB.

2. What happens when Asha and Ravi need the same book?

In one breath: One clerk needs the ledger for a second. One auditor needs it for forty minutes. There is one ledger, it sits on one table, and that is the entire problem this lab solves.

Forget databases for ten minutes. We are in a district land registry office in 1970. There is one book: the register. Each page holds numbered entries, and each entry records who owns a plot.

Two people need the register right now.

Asha is a clerk. A sale has completed, so plot 12 must change hands from Meera to Vikram. Writing that down takes her about a second.

Ravi is an auditor. He is preparing a statement of every plot in the district. He must read all 400 pages, and it takes him forty minutes.

There is one book and it is on one table. That is the whole of the difficulty.

Two things must be true at once, and they fight each other.

The first is that Ravi's statement must be internally consistent. Suppose he reads plot 12 as Meera on page 3, and reads plot 12's mortgage as belonging to Vikram on page 290. He has now produced a document describing a state of the world that never existed. His report has to describe the register as of one moment.

The second is that Asha must not have to wait forty minutes to record a completed sale. The customer has already gone home.

Hold that scene in your head. Every design in this lab is an attempt to give both of them what they want.

Naming, once, so the rest reads cleanly. In this lab a reader is any transaction that needs a consistent view over time — a report, a pg_dump, a nightly export, an analytics query, a long SELECT. A writer is any transaction that changes rows. The tension is never reader-vs-reader.

3. Why can't we just let one person hold the book?

In one breath: Whoever is using the book holds it, and everybody else queues. It works. It also means a query that changes nothing can stop your business from taking money for thirty-seven minutes.

The first design any reasonable person proposes is possession. Asha wants to write, so she takes the book. Ravi wants to read, so he takes the book. Nobody else touches it until they put it down.

You have seen this before under a formal name. From isolation-levels this is two-phase locking (2PL), and its bill was stated there in three words: 2PL pays in waiting. You even know the refinement. Readers can share the book, because many people may read at once. A writer needs it exclusively. So a writer waits for every reader, and a reader waits for every writer.

Let us actually run the morning, because the failure is worse than "some waiting". Read the last column: it is who is standing in the corridor.

Time What happens Who is waiting
09:00 Ravi opens the register and starts his forty-minute pass nobody
09:03 Asha arrives with the plot 12 sale and needs exclusive access Asha
09:06 a second clerk arrives with another sale Asha, clerk 2
09:11 a third clerk arrives Asha, clerk 2, clerk 3
09:40 Ravi finishes; Asha finally records the sale nobody, 37 minutes late

Now say what that table means in production terms.

A read-only query stopped the business from taking money for 37 minutes.

Nothing was wrong. Nobody crashed. No network partitioned. A query that changes nothing, that has no side effects, that could in principle be run on a photocopy, halted every writer in the system. It did so because the only way it could guarantee itself a consistent view was to freeze the world.

That is not a tuning problem. It is structural. And it gets worse in the direction you would not guess. Reverse the order and the writers starve the reader instead. If clerks arrive every 20 seconds and each holds the book for a second, Ravi may never get a clean forty-minute window at all. His report never runs.

Ravi is read-only. He changes nothing. Is there any way to give him a consistent forty-minute view of the register that does not require him to stop Asha — while still using only the one physical book on the table?

Answer (open after you have committed)

Yes — and the narrow thing that breaks is worth naming precisely.

Ravi is not harmed by Asha adding information. He is harmed by Asha destroying information: specifically, by her erasing the value he still needs to see. If Asha writes Vikram on top of Meera, the value Meera is gone from the universe and Ravi can no longer produce a 09:00-consistent report.

So the requirement is not "stop Asha writing". It is:

stop Asha from destroying the past.

Those sound similar. They are enormously different in cost. Blocking writes costs you availability. Preserving the past costs you space, and space is a bill you can pay later, in the background, when nobody is watching.

If you guessed that Ravi must simply be given a photocopy, you were close to right and expensive. A photocopy of a 400-page register is a full copy of the database, taken per reader. The trick below gets the same effect for the cost of the few entries that actually changed.

That trade is the entire subject of this lab: latency now, for space and cleanup later. Everything else is consequences.

4. What exactly does Ravi need, stated in one sentence?

Every reader must be able to see a complete, unchanging picture of the data as it stood at one chosen moment, no matter how long that reader takes, and without preventing any writer from proceeding.

5. What if Asha never rubbed anything out?

In one breath: When something changes, do not change it. Write a new copy and pencil a note in the margin of the old one saying when it stopped being true. Now the past is still readable and nobody has to wait.

Here is the invention, in two sentences.

When a row changes, do not modify it. Write a brand-new copy of the row containing the new values, and mark the old copy with the moment it stopped being current.

Back at the registry, Asha stops erasing. Instead she does three things.

Entry 47 currently reads Plot 12 — owner Meera. Asha writes a new entry, number 92, on a fresh line: Plot 12 — owner Vikram. Then she goes back to entry 47 and writes in its margin: superseded at 09:03.

She has not destroyed anything. Entry 47 is still legible, still there, with a note saying when it stopped being true.

Now Ravi, who started at 09:00, has a rule he can apply on his own. He does not have to talk to anybody to apply it.

Ignore any entry created after 09:00. Ignore any entry that was already superseded before 09:00. What is left is the register as of 09:00.

Walk it with him. He reads entry 47 and sees "superseded at 09:03". That is after his 09:00, so the superseding had not happened yet as far as he is concerned, and he uses entry 47. He reaches entry 92, sees it was created at 09:03, after his 09:00, and skips it.

Asha did not wait. Ravi got a consistent 09:00 view. The only thing it cost was ink and paper.

This is Multi-Version Concurrency Control, and now the name decomposes exactly. Multi-version means more than one physical copy of each logical row exists at once. Concurrency control is the mechanism by which concurrent transactions are kept from interfering.

You knew the acronym MVCC coming in. What you now have is the reason. MVCC is the answer to "how do I stop destroying the past", and it exists because readers-blocking-writers was intolerable.

6. What are the four words the rest of this lab is written in?

In one breath: You have felt four distinctions already. Now they get their names: the physical copy, the family of copies, the birth stamp and the death stamp. Everything after this is built from those four.

Now that you have felt the distinctions, they can have names. These four are load-bearing. Everything else gets defined at first use and collected in the glossary in section 27.

What is the difference between a row and a tuple?

In PostgreSQL's own source code and documentation, a tuple means one physical version of one row. This is the single most important vocabulary correction in the lab, so say it out loud. A row is the logical thing your application talks about, such as "plot 12's owner". A tuple is one physical copy of it sitting in a page on disk. One row can be many tuples.

In the registry, plot 12 is the row. Entries 47 and 92 are two tuples.

(Warning, because this genuinely confuses people: in the SQL standard and in most textbooks, "tuple" just means "row". PostgreSQL uses it in the narrower physical sense above, and so does this lab, because that distinction is the thing being taught.)

The set of tuples that are all copies of the same logical row, ordered oldest to newest, is called a version chain. Plot 12's chain is entry 47 → entry 92.

What do the two margin notes become?

Asha wrote two things by hand: who wrote this entry, and when it stopped being current. Those are the two stamps every physical version carries, and they are the whole of MVCC's memory.

Read the middle column first: it is the plain-English name for each stamp, and the symbol only arrives in the last column.

The thing in the registry In words The symbol PostgreSQL uses
"written by clerk 701" on entry 92 the tuple's birth certificate: which transaction created it xmin
"superseded at 09:03 by clerk 701" on entry 47 the tuple's death certificate: which transaction ended it, by deleting it or replacing it xmax
a margin with nothing written in it nobody has ended this version, so it is the current one xmax = 0

Entry 47's death stamp and entry 92's birth stamp are the same transaction, because the single act that created 92 also ended 47.

Two consequences are worth noticing right now, because they will do a lot of work later.

An update is really an insert plus a stamp. Physically, telling PostgreSQL UPDATE plots SET owner='Vikram' WHERE id=12 writes a whole new tuple and puts a death stamp on the old one. In SQL words, an UPDATE is an INSERT plus a DELETE. There is no "modify in place" anywhere in this design. Updating a single boolean column on a 4 KB row writes a new 4 KB tuple.

A delete deletes nothing. A DELETE writes a death stamp on the existing tuple and returns. The bytes are all still there. That is the sentence in the lab title, and it is literally true.

Illustrative only — do not run this anywhere that matters, and note that the birth and death stamps are hidden system columns you can select but never write:

SELECT ctid, xmin, xmax, id, owner FROM plots WHERE id = 12;

On PostgreSQL this returns one row per visible tuple, so normally one row. The value of this snippet is that it is the seam where the physical model becomes observable. The first column, ctid, is the tuple's physical address written as (page, line-pointer), and it changes every time the row is updated, because the row moved.

7. What do the stamps look like on real numbers?

In one breath: Swap the clerks' names for numbers and run the same morning. Ravi reads a value that is fourteen minutes out of date, and that is exactly the correct answer.

Time to drop the registry for concrete machine values. We keep the people.

Transactions get an ever-increasing integer identifier. Think of it as the number stamped on a clerk's badge when they start a shift. In PostgreSQL it is called a transaction ID, universally abbreviated XID. Badge 700 started before badge 701. That is all it means, for now. Section 19 will demolish "that is all it means", but not yet.

Our table plots starts with two rows, both written long ago by transaction 500, which committed. Read the two stamp columns as birth and death.

tuple xmin (born by) xmax (ended by) id owner
A 500 0 (nobody) 12 Meera
B 500 0 (nobody) 13 Kavya

Ravi begins his report. He gets badge 700. He takes a snapshot, which for the moment we will treat as just "the moment 700 began". He starts reading.

Asha updates plot 12. She gets badge 701 and runs UPDATE plots SET owner='Vikram' WHERE id=12. Two physical things happen. A new tuple C is written, born by 701 and ended by nobody, holding Vikram. Tuple A gets a death stamp naming 701.

tuple xmin (born by) xmax (ended by) id owner
A 500 701 12 Meera
B 500 0 (nobody) 13 Kavya
C 701 0 (nobody) 12 Vikram

If you ever dump those two events in the engineer's own shorthand, this is what you see. Read the middle column first; the shorthand is only the last column.

The event In words In raw notation
tuple C appears born by 701, ended by nobody, plot 12 now owned by Vikram xmin=701, xmax=0, id=12, owner=Vikram
tuple A is stamped ended by 701 xmax=701
tuple A, both stamps together born by 500, ended by 701 (xmin=500, xmax=701)

Asha commits. Transaction 701 is now committed and durable.

Ravi, still in transaction 700, reads plot 12. He walks the tuples for id 12 and finds A and C. Which does he take?

He applies a rule of exactly two clauses:

A tuple is visible to me if two things hold. (a) Its birth stamp names a transaction I should be able to see. (b) Its death stamp is either absent, or it names a transaction I should not be able to see.

Work it through. Read the last column: it is the verdict.

tuple clause (a): the birth stamp clause (b): the death stamp verdict
C 701 started after Ravi's 700 began, so he must not see it not reached invisible
A 500 committed long before Ravi began, so it passes 701 is a transaction Ravi cannot see, so it passes visible

Ravi reads owner = Meera. Asha committed her change fourteen minutes ago and it is not there, which is exactly right, because Ravi is producing a 09:00 report. Meanwhile any transaction starting now gets badge 702, sees C, and reads Vikram.

Look at what just happened at the physical level. Two different transactions read two different bytes from two different places on disk, for the same logical row, at the same instant, with no locks and no coordination between them. That is the whole payoff. Visibility is a pure function of the tuple's stamps and your own snapshot. It requires no communication.

8. Why isn't "started before me" the same as "finished before me"?

In one breath: Comparing badge numbers looks like it decides who you can see. It does not, because clerks do not finish their shifts in the order they started them. Two extra structures supply the missing facts.

Version 1 of the rule quietly assumed something false. It assumed that "a transaction I should be able to see" can be decided by comparing badge numbers.

Here is the scenario that breaks it. Read the last column: it is what is true at 09:06, when Ravi takes his snapshot.

Badge Who What they did State at 09:06
710 Asha began a long update touching many rows still running
711 Vikram began a quick update and committed at 09:05 committed
712 Ravi began a report at 09:06 running, this is us

Now Ravi looks at a tuple whose birth stamp reads xmin = 710. Under the naive number rule, 710 is less than 712, so "it started before me, I see it". That is wrong, because 710 has not committed. Ravi would be reading uncommitted data. From isolation-levels you know the name for that result: a dirty read, forbidden at every level above read-uncommitted.

Now the reverse case. Ravi looks at a tuple whose birth stamp reads xmin = 711. Again 711 is less than 712, so the number rule says visible. Here that happens to be correct, because 711 committed before Ravi started. But now imagine a third transaction 715 that commits, and then Ravi, still in 712, looks at its tuple. 715 is greater than 712, so invisible, which is also correct. The number comparison got two of three right by luck.

The actual requirement is this:

I should see a transaction's work if and only if that transaction had already committed at the instant my snapshot was taken.

Two facts are needed, and neither of them is on the tuple. The first is whether that transaction committed, aborted, or is still running; the tuple header only carries a number. The second is whether it was already committed when I started; a number cannot answer this, because transactions do not commit in the order they start. Badge 710 started before 711 and will commit after it.

Which two structures supply the missing facts?

The registry office has an answer to the first question already. There is a board by the door where each clerk's shift is marked on duty, signed off, or walked out. PostgreSQL has the same board, and it is tiny.

The commit log is a separate array on disk with two bits per transaction ID, recording in progress, committed, aborted or sub-committed. To ask "did 710 commit?", you index into that array. It is small and almost always in memory. Its file is called pg_xact, and it is called clog in older versions and in most blog posts.

The second question needs Ravi's slip of paper. When a transaction needs a view, it captures a snapshot — not a copy of data, but a description of which transactions to believe. In PostgreSQL it is three fields. Read the middle column first.

Field In words What it decides
snapshot xmin the lowest badge still on duty when I took my slip anything below this is finished, so ask the commit log
snapshot xmax the next badge that had not yet been handed out anything at or above this began after me: always invisible
xip[] the badges that were on duty at that instant, between the two bounds always invisible, whatever they do later

(Careful: the snapshot's xmin/xmax fields and a tuple's xmin/xmax stamps are different things that unfortunately share names. Snapshot bounds describe transactions; tuple stamps describe one physical version. When it matters below, this lab writes "snapshot xmin" or "tuple xmin" in full.)

So Ravi's snapshot at 09:06 is roughly xmin=710, xmax=713, xip=[710]. Read it as a sentence: "Everything below 710 has finished — ask the commit log whether it committed. 710 was still running, so never believe it. 713 and above did not exist yet, so never believe them."

🔮 Predict #2. Asha's transaction 710 has been running for two hours and has updated 50,000 rows. She now types ROLLBACK. Those 50,000 new tuples are sitting in the table's pages on disk, physically written, some of them already flushed to disk by the background writer.

How much work does the rollback do to undo them? Give an order of magnitude: seconds? minutes? proportional to 50,000?

Answer

Essentially none. It is O(1) and it is instant. The rollback writes a single entry in the commit log, saying "transaction 710: aborted". That is two bits. Then it returns.

That is it. Nothing walks back over the 50,000 tuples. Nothing is erased. All 50,000 new tuples are still physically present in the pages, and all 50,000 old tuples still carry a death stamp naming 710, written xmax = 710.

They are simply now invisible to everyone forever, by the same rule that governs everything. Any reader that checks a birth stamp of 710 will consult the commit log, learn that 710 aborted, and skip the tuple. Any reader checking an old tuple's death stamp of 710 will learn 710 aborted, conclude that the deletion never happened, and treat the tuple as alive. The abort is retroactive and free.

If you predicted "proportional to 50,000", you were applying the model from acid, and you were right about every in-place engine on earth. There, undo was real work: before-images, a full undo pass, CLRs, undoNext, and a recovery process that could pin a CPU for an hour rolling back one huge aborted transaction. That was ARIES on an in-place storage engine, where the new value physically overwrote the old one, so getting the old value back meant copying it back.

PostgreSQL's heap never overwrites, so there is nothing to copy back. The undo work is zero.

And now the sentence that is really the thesis of the entire lab:

The work did not disappear. It moved. In an in-place engine you pay for old versions at rollback time and at read time. In a no-overwrite engine you pay for them later, in the background, in a completely different process, on a schedule you do not control.

That process is VACUUM. It is not a maintenance chore bolted onto PostgreSQL. It is the other half of the design, and you have just watched the bill being run up.

(One honest footnote: rollback is not quite free of consequence. The aborted transaction's changes were still written to the WAL, still dirtied pages, and still left 50,000 dead tuples. It is free of undo work, not free of cost.)

9. What is the visibility rule, exactly?

In one breath: Two questions about every tuple you touch. Was it born by somebody I believe? Was it ended by somebody I believe? Yes and no means you read it.

You now have every piece. Here is the actual rule, which PostgreSQL evaluates for every tuple it touches, on every scan. This is the intermediate model's centrepiece. Read it slowly once and it will serve you for years.

The shape first, so the tables afterwards land on something.

flowchart TD
    A["A tuple in front of me"] --> B{"Born by a transaction<br>I can believe?"}
    B -- "no" --> X["Skip it"]
    B -- "yes" --> C{"Ended by a transaction<br>I can believe?"}
    C -- "yes" --> X
    C -- "no" --> V["Read it"]

We are looking at a tuple whose two stamps we will call t.xmin and t.xmax, held by a reader whose snapshot we will call S. In words: the tuple's birth stamp, the tuple's death stamp, and my slip of paper. The pair (t.xmin, t.xmax) is just those two stamps side by side.

Step 1 — is the tuple's creator visible to me? Read the middle column: it is the question in plain words.

The check In words Result
it is my own transaction I wrote this myself visible, after a command-level check described below
t.xmin >= S.xmax it was born after my slip was issued not visible. Stop here
t.xmin ∈ S.xip[] its creator was still on duty when my slip was issued not visible. Stop here
otherwise, ask the commit log its creator had finished; did it sign off or walk out? aborted ⇒ stop, invisible. committed ⇒ go to step 2

Step 2 — has the tuple been ended by someone I can see?

The check In words Result
t.xmax = 0 nobody ever ended it VISIBLE. This is the current version
the death stamp is only a lock somebody is holding the row, not deleting it VISIBLE. See the note below
t.xmax >= S.xmax, or t.xmax ∈ S.xip[] somebody deleted it, but that deletion is in my future VISIBLE. This is exactly Ravi reading Meera
otherwise, ask the commit log did the deleter sign off or walk out? aborted ⇒ VISIBLE, the delete never happened. committed ⇒ not visible

Two refinements matter in practice.

A death stamp is not always a death. SELECT ... FOR UPDATE and foreign-key checks place a row lock, and PostgreSQL records row locks in the same xmax field, telling them apart with flag bits in the tuple header called the infomask. So a tuple can have a non-zero death stamp and be perfectly alive and current. If you ever inspect raw tuples and see a populated xmax on a row you know was never deleted, this is why.

Your own changes need a finer clock. Two extra fields, cmin/cmax, record the command number inside your transaction. Without them, UPDATE t SET x = x+1 would see the rows it is itself producing and loop forever. You will never tune this. Know it exists so the header layout makes sense.

The one-sentence version is worth memorising:

A tuple is visible to me if it was created by a transaction that had committed before my snapshot, and has not been deleted by a transaction that had committed before my snapshot.

Notice what is not in that rule. There is no mention of other readers, no lock, no coordination, no waiting. Visibility is decided entirely locally. That locality is why MVCC scales, and it is why "readers don't block writers, writers don't block readers" is true rather than aspirational.

10. Where does the registry picture lie to you?

In one breath: The book with margin notes is a good model and you should keep it. It lies to you in four places, and each lie is a section of this lab waiting to happen.

What is the simple model worth keeping?

The registry book. Nothing is erased. New entries go on fresh lines. Superseded entries get a margin note saying when they stopped being true. Every reader carries a slip of paper and applies the same two-clause rule. Periodically a clerk goes through with a ruler and strikes out entries that no reader in the building could possibly still need, freeing those lines for reuse.

That model gets you a long way and it is the one to keep in your head.

What is actually on disk?

A PostgreSQL table is a heap: a sequence of 8 KB pages. You met pages in btree-and-lsm, and it is the same object. Here is the inside of one page.

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ page header (24 B)                                          │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ line pointer array  →  [1][2][3][4][5] ...                  │  grows down ↓
│   each 4 bytes: offset, length, and a 2-bit STATE           │
│   state ∈ { UNUSED · NORMAL · DEAD · REDIRECT }             │

│                                                             │
│                  free space                                 │
│                                                             │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ tuple 5 │ tuple 4 │ tuple 3 │ tuple 2 │ tuple 1             │  grows up ↑
│                                                             │
│  each tuple = 23-byte header (xmin, xmax, cid, ctid,        │
│               infomask flags) + null bitmap + your data     │

Three details from that diagram each become a section of their own later.

The line pointer array is a level of indirection. An index does not point at a tuple's byte offset. It points at (page number, line pointer number), and that pair is the ctid. The line pointer then says where in the page the tuple actually starts. Indirection is what makes section 16's HOT updates possible.

Line pointers have their own lifecycle. A pointer sits in one of four states. Read the middle column: it is the state in plain words.

State In words Why it exists
NORMAL it points at a real tuple the ordinary case
DEAD the tuple is gone, but index entries may still point here vacuum, phase 1
UNUSED fully reclaimed and reusable vacuum, phase 3
REDIRECT it forwards to another line pointer in the same page HOT, section 16

Two of those four states exist purely because of vacuum.

The header is 23 bytes, padded to 24. Picture a table of narrow rows: an 8-byte integer key and a 4-byte value. The per-version bookkeeping is then twice the size of your data. MVCC is not free even when nothing goes wrong.

Where does the analogy break?

Four places, and each one matters.

Ravi's slip says a time; a snapshot does not. A snapshot is not "09:00". It is a set of transaction identities to believe. This is not pedantry. It is why a transaction that starts later can still see less than one that started earlier, and why wall-clock time never appears anywhere in the visibility rule. If you carry "snapshot = timestamp" in your head you will mispredict every overlapping-writer case in section 8.

The clerk with the ruler cannot make the book thinner. Striking out an entry frees the line for a future entry. It does not remove a page from the book, and it does not make the book lighter. Section 14 is entirely about this.

The register has no index; a real table has several, and they are separate books. Every tuple you create needs an entry in every index. Cleaning up a tuple means first finding and removing its pointers in every one of those books. This is why vacuum's cost is dominated by index maintenance, not by the heap.

The clerk's numbers run out. The registry could use 09:03 forever. A PostgreSQL badge number is a 32-bit counter, it wraps, and the consequence is the single most feared operational failure in the whole system. That is section 19, and it is the part of this lab that has woken people up at night.

11. What does one dead tuple actually cost?

In one breath: A tuple nobody can ever see again still sits on disk, still gets read by every full scan, and still takes up room in memory. The third one is the one that ruins your afternoon.

A dead tuple is a tuple that is invisible to every possible reader, now and forever. Nobody will ever look at it again. It is garbage. In the registry, it is an entry so old that no auditor in the building could still have a slip that reaches it. Not Ravi, and not anyone who walks in later.

Let us price it on a realistic table called sessions. Read the last column: it is where each number comes from.

Three costs land, and they are different from each other.

The first cost is disk. It is the obvious one and the least interesting. Disk is cheap.

The second cost is that every sequential scan reads all of it. SELECT count(*) FROM sessions must visit all 11 million tuples and evaluate the visibility rule on each one, in order to return 1,000,000. That is not 230 MB of I/O. It is 2.5 GB. Your query got 11× slower. The query plan did not change, the statistics did not change, and nothing in your code changed. This is what people mean when they say bloat hurts.

The third cost is cache pollution. PostgreSQL's shared buffers and the operating system's page cache hold pages, not rows. If 90% of every page is garbage, then 90% of your RAM is holding garbage. A working set that comfortably fit in 8 GB of cache now needs 88 GB and does not fit. So you start hitting disk on queries that used to be served from memory. Latency does not degrade gracefully here. It falls off a cliff when the working set crosses the cache size.

You met the general shape of the first two costs in btree-and-lsm under the names space amplification and read amplification. This is the same scorecard applied to a different engine. MVCC's answer to "when do you pay for sortedness" is "not sortedness — deadness, and you pay in a background process".

So somebody has to collect the garbage. That somebody is VACUUM, and it is the clerk with the ruler from Ravi and Asha's registry. Now the interesting part: the obvious way to write it is wrong.

12. Who is allowed to throw an old version away?

In one breath: The obvious cleanup rule deletes the very entry Ravi is about to read. The fix is a single number: the oldest slip of paper anybody in the building is still holding.

Here is version 1 of the cleanup rule, which everybody proposes first:

Scan the table. For each tuple, if its death stamp is set and that transaction committed, the tuple has been deleted — remove it.

It is wrong, and section 7 already contains the counterexample.

Recall the trace. Tuple A was born by 500 and ended by 701, which in raw notation is (xmin=500, xmax=701). Asha's 701 has committed, and Ravi is still sitting in transaction 700 producing his forty-minute report. Under rule v1, A qualifies for removal: death stamp set, that transaction committed, delete it.

Ravi reaches plot 12 and finds only tuple C, which reads owner = Vikram and was born by 701 — a transaction he cannot see. His snapshot says C is invisible, and A no longer exists. Plot 12 has vanished from the register. Ravi's report is missing a plot. Not stale. Wrong, and silently so.

We have violated the exact guarantee we set out to provide in section 4: "no matter how long that reader takes". Version 1 of vacuum silently converts a slow reader into a corrupt report.

What is the horizon?

So the rule must consult not only the tuple, but every snapshot alive in the system:

A dead tuple may be removed only when its deletion is visible to every snapshot currently held anywhere in the database — that is, only when its death stamp names a transaction that committed before the oldest snapshot still in existence.

Since snapshots are described by their xmin bound, this reduces to a single number. In the registry it is the earliest time written on any slip of paper still in the building.

the horizon = the minimum snapshot xmin over all snapshots currently held.

Every transaction with a badge below the horizon is finished and its outcome is settled for everyone. A dead tuple whose death stamp is below the horizon can never be wanted again. A dead tuple whose death stamp is at or above the horizon must be kept, no matter how obviously dead it looks.

PostgreSQL exposes this number. It is often written OldestXmin in logs, and it appears as backend_xmin in the pg_stat_activity view.

Read the horizon definition once more and notice its shape. It is a global minimum. Not an average. Not a percentile. One participant, anywhere, holding an old snapshot pins the horizon for the entire database — including tables that participant has never heard of.

That single structural fact generates most of section 20's failure scenarios, the 03:10 page in section 1, and about 80% of all PostgreSQL bloat incidents ever filed.

🔮 Predict #3. A developer opens psql, runs BEGIN; and a single SELECT count(*) FROM users;, gets the answer, and then goes to lunch without typing COMMIT. The session is now idle in transaction, holding an open read-only transaction. It holds no locks on any table — it is read-only and it finished its query.

Meanwhile the application updates the sessions table 10 million times over the next three hours, and autovacuum runs against it dozens of times, successfully, with no errors in the log.

How many of those 10 million dead tuples can vacuum remove?

Answer

Zero.

Every one of those 10 million dead tuples has a death stamp at or above the horizon, because the horizon is pinned at the developer's snapshot from three hours ago. Vacuum runs, walks all 11 million tuples, evaluates every one, removes none, reports success, and goes back to sleep. Then it does it again. And again.

Three hours later the table is 2.5 GB of which 230 MB is real. The developer comes back from lunch, types COMMIT (or their client times out), and the horizon jumps forward by three hours' worth of badge numbers. The next vacuum removes all 10 million dead tuples in one pass — and the table is still 2.5 GB on disk. Section 14 explains that last part.

This is worth internalising as a shape, because it recurs in every MVCC system ever built:

One idle session, holding nothing, blocking nobody, caused unbounded space growth across the entire database.

There are no locks involved. There is no error. pg_locks is clean. The only symptom is disk. This is why every experienced PostgreSQL operator sets idle_in_transaction_session_timeout. It is also why "who is holding the oldest xmin" is the first query you run on a bloat page. It is not "is autovacuum running", which was the on-call engineer's wrong first move in section 1.

If you predicted "a few", the mistake was almost certainly assuming the horizon is per-table, or that a read-only transaction is harmless. Neither is true. The horizon is global, and read-only is exactly the dangerous kind. A writing transaction that long would at least be visibly holding locks.

13. What are vacuum's five jobs, and which one hurts?

In one breath: "VACUUM removes dead tuples" is about a fifth of the truth. It has five jobs, four of them cause a production symptom you would otherwise misdiagnose, and the expensive one is the indexes.

Here is the whole job. A vacuum of one table proceeds roughly like this.

flowchart TD
    P1["1. Scan the heap<br>prune, mark pointers DEAD"] --> P2["2. Vacuum every index<br>the dominant cost"]
    P2 --> P3["3. Reclaim line pointers<br>DEAD becomes UNUSED"]
    P3 --> P4["4. Update the free space map<br>and the visibility map"]
    P4 --> P5["5. Freeze old tuples<br>maybe truncate the file end"]

Phase 1 — scan the heap and build a list of dead tuples. Read the table's pages, skipping the ones the visibility map marks all-visible (section 17). On each page, apply the horizon rule to every tuple. For each removable one, do an in-page prune: move its data out, and set its line pointer to DEAD. Collect its ctid into an in-memory list.

Why only DEAD and not UNUSED? Because indexes still point at that line pointer. Freeing the slot now would let a future tuple occupy it, and an old index entry would then point at an unrelated row. So the line pointer must be kept as a tombstone until every index has been cleaned.

Phase 2 — vacuum the indexes. For each index on the table, scan it and delete every entry whose ctid is in that list. This is usually the dominant cost of the whole operation: a full index scan per index. A table with 8 indexes pays 8 full index scans.

Phase 3 — reclaim the line pointers. Go back over the heap pages and set those DEAD line pointers to UNUSED. Now the slots and the space are genuinely reusable.

Phase 4 — update the free space map and the visibility map. Record how much free space each page now has, which is how future inserts find a home. Then set the all-visible bit on pages where every tuple is now visible to everyone.

Phase 5 — freeze old tuples, and possibly truncate empty pages off the end of the file. Sections 19 and 14 respectively.

Which four consequences can you now predict instead of discover?

(a) Vacuum is mostly an index operation. If a vacuum is taking hours, the first question is not "how big is the table" but "how many indexes does it have, and how big are they". Dropping an unused index makes every future vacuum faster, permanently.

(b) The dead-tuple list has a memory budget, and running out used to be brutal. The list lives in maintenance_work_mem. If it fills up, vacuum must stop scanning, do the entire index phase, come back, and resume. So a big table could pay for five or ten complete passes over every index.

On PostgreSQL 17 and later this changed significantly. The dead-TID list was rewritten to a compressed radix-tree structure called TidStore, which removed the old 1 GB cap and made multiple index passes far rarer. [Version-specific: check your major version. The old advice "raise maintenance_work_mem before a big vacuum" is strong pre-17 advice and much less critical after.]

(c) Vacuum is deliberately, configurably slow. It runs a cost-based delay loop. It accumulates notional cost points as it works, and when it exceeds a limit it sleeps. Read the middle column: it is what each knob counts, in plain words.

Setting In words Effect
vacuum_cost_page_hit points charged for a page already in cache cheapest
..._miss points charged for a page read from disk dearer
..._page_dirty points charged for a page vacuum modifies dearest
vacuum_cost_limit points allowed before a nap, default 200 the speed limit
vacuum_cost_delay how long that nap lasts the throttle

This is throttling by design: vacuum yields to your workload. It is also the single most common reason autovacuum "cannot keep up". It is not stuck. It is obeying a speed limit set for a machine much smaller than yours. The defaults have historically been extremely conservative; autovacuum_vacuum_cost_delay dropped from 20 ms to 2 ms in PostgreSQL 12, and on modern SSD hardware raising vacuum_cost_limit well above the default 200 is routine.

(d) A plain SELECT can clean up. Phase 1's in-page pruning also happens opportunistically whenever a page is read and looks like it would benefit. That is HOT pruning, and section 16 covers it. So a read query can dirty pages and generate WAL.

If you have ever wondered why a read-only workload produces write I/O, that is one of two reasons. The other is hint bits. The first reader to touch a tuple after its creating transaction committed writes a flag into the tuple header, caching "this birth stamp is committed", so the next reader need not consult the commit log. That flag write dirties the page. Both are the system doing your future self a favour, and both make "read-only query, why is it writing?" a non-mystery.

14. Why doesn't the disk space come back?

In one breath: Vacuum frees lines inside the book. It does not tear pages out of the book. So the healthy result of a vacuum is that the file stops growing, not that it shrinks.

This is surprise 3 and surprise 4 from the 03:10 incident, and it is the single most common misconception about vacuum.

🔮 Predict #4. The developer from section 12 finally committed. The horizon has jumped forward. Autovacuum now runs on sessions and successfully removes all 10 million dead tuples. The table has 1 million live rows, about 230 MB of real data, and the file on disk is 2.5 GB.

The vacuum reports success. What does du say the file size is now?

Answer

2.5 GB. Unchanged. Not one byte returned to the operating system.

If you predicted 230 MB, you have just made the same mistake the on-call engineer made at 03:10, and the next paragraph is the most useful one in the lab.

Ordinary VACUUM does not return space to the operating system. It makes space reusable inside the existing file.

Freeing tuple slots inside a page leaves the page where it is. The file has the same number of pages. du reports the same size. What has changed is that the free space map now says "page 4,201 has 6 KB free", so the next insert or update will land there instead of extending the file.

So the effect of vacuum is not shrinkage — it is that growth stops. The table reaches a steady-state size somewhere above its live size, and stays there. That steady state is the honest, healthy outcome. It is why a permanently-somewhat-larger-than-its-data table is not a bug.

There is exactly one case where plain vacuum does shorten the file: truncation. If the pages at the very end of the file are entirely empty, vacuum can lop them off and hand the space back. Note the two conditions. The empty pages must be contiguous and at the end.

Delete 3 million rows scattered through a 41 GB table, and you free space in the middle of thousands of pages and truncate approximately nothing. Truncation also needs a brief ACCESS EXCLUSIVE lock, which vacuum will give up on rather than block your workload. It can be disabled per-table with vacuum_truncate = off.

15. How does one field decide who wins a write conflict?

In one breath: Two clerks want the same entry. The death stamp is already claimed by one of them, and that claim is the conflict detector. No separate lock table is needed.

You learned in isolation-levels that snapshot isolation resolves concurrent writers by first-committer-wins. Here is the physical implementation, which is a two-line addition to what you already have.

When Asha (transaction 701) tries to update tuple A, she checks A's death stamp. If it is set to a still-running transaction, she cannot proceed, because someone else got there first. So she waits on that transaction's lock. When it finishes, one of two things happens.

If it aborted, Asha proceeds. If it committed, the answer depends on her isolation level. At READ COMMITTED she re-reads the newest version and re-evaluates her WHERE clause against it. That re-check is called "EvalPlanQual", and it is precisely why READ COMMITTED permits lost update while still not permitting dirty write. At REPEATABLE READ or SERIALIZABLE she instead gets ERROR: could not serialize access due to concurrent update, which is SQLSTATE 40001, the retry-loop error you already know.

So the entire write-conflict rule is one sentence: the xmax field is the conflict detector. No separate lock table entry is needed for row-level write conflicts, because the row version itself carries the claim.

16. What does every extra index cost, and how does HOT dodge it?

In one breath: Every new version of a row needs a new entry in every index, because the new version lives at a new address. Unless it does not move far, in which case PostgreSQL can skip the indexes entirely.

Return to a cost mentioned in passing. Every new tuple needs an entry in every index on the table. The reason is that the new version sits at a new ctid, which is a new physical address, and every index maps a key to a ctid.

So a table with 6 indexes, updated once, writes 1 new heap tuple plus 6 new index entries, even if you only changed one non-indexed column. Later, cleaning that tuple up costs 6 index scans. Index bloat is frequently worse than heap bloat, and unlike the heap it directly slows down the lookups you actually care about.

That is so expensive that PostgreSQL has a special case for it. The special case is worth understanding, because you can cause it or prevent it with schema decisions.

What is a heap-only tuple?

If both of these hold —

  1. the UPDATE changes no indexed column, and
  2. the new tuple fits on the same page as the old one —

then PostgreSQL performs a HOT update, where HOT stands for Heap-Only Tuple.

BEFORE                          AFTER a HOT update
page 42                         page 42
 lp[1] NORMAL ──▶ tuple A        lp[1] REDIRECT ──▶ lp[3]
                                 lp[3] NORMAL ────▶ tuple C   (new version)
index entry: 'Meera' → (42,1)   index entry: 'Meera' → (42,1)   ← unchanged!

The index entry still points at line pointer 1. Line pointer 1 becomes a REDIRECT that forwards to the new tuple within the same page. No index was touched. The chain of versions lives entirely inside one page, which is why it is called a heap-only tuple: a tuple no index points to.

The payoff compounds. HOT chains can also be pruned without an index scan, opportunistically, by any query that reads the page. So a HOT-heavy workload largely cleans up after itself, and autovacuum has far less to do.

🔮 Predict #5. A migration lands on sessions. It adds one index on last_seen_at, the column every request updates. The index itself is small and the dashboard query that needed it gets faster.

What happens to the table's bloat and to vacuum's cost per run? Same, a little worse, or much worse?

Answer

Much worse, and not because of the index's own upkeep.

The index is on the column that every update changes. Condition 1 for a HOT update is now violated on every single update of that table. Every update that used to touch no index at all now writes an entry in every index on the table. Every one of those entries must later be found and removed by vacuum's phase 2.

If you answered "a little worse, one more index to maintain", you used the right model for an index on a static column and the wrong one here. The cost of this index is not local to itself. It is charged to every update of the table, and then charged again to every vacuum.

This is the highest-leverage and least-known fact in PostgreSQL schema design, and it arrives in a migration diff that looks completely innocent.

What are the two ways to lose HOT?

Both of them are choices you make.

The first is indexing a column you update frequently. Adding an index on sessions.last_seen_at sounds harmless and even helpful. It converts every single session update from a HOT update into a full index-entry-per-index write. That is Predict #5 above.

The second is filling pages to 100%. Condition 2 needs free space on the page. A freshly-loaded table is packed full, so the first update of every row must go to a different page and cannot be HOT.

The lever is fillfactor. Running ALTER TABLE sessions SET (fillfactor = 85) tells PostgreSQL to leave 15% of each page empty at insert time, reserved for future in-page versions. You are spending 15% more disk to buy HOT updates. On an update-heavy table this trade is usually strongly positive. On an append-only table it is pure waste, which is why the default is 100.

(PostgreSQL 16 carries a related but distinct idea for indexes themselves, "bottom-up index deletion", introduced in PG 14. A B-tree page about to split first tries to delete entries it can prove are dead, which specifically targets the index bloat caused by non-HOT updates of unchanged index columns. Worth knowing the name; not worth tuning, as it has no knobs.)

17. Why does your "index-only scan" still read the table?

In one breath: An index entry does not know whether its row is visible to you. There is a two-bit-per-page cheat sheet that sometimes lets the index answer alone, and vacuum is the only thing that writes it.

Vacuum keeps a tiny side file per table called the visibility map, holding two bits per page. Read the middle column: it is what the bit promises.

Bit In words What it lets the system skip
all-visible every tuple on this page is visible to every possible transaction vacuum need not look at the page at all
all-frozen stronger still; nothing here can ever age out (section 19) even an aggressive vacuum can skip it

It exists to let vacuum skip pages. If a page has not changed since it was marked all-visible, vacuum need not look at it. That alone turns vacuuming a 1 TB mostly-static table from a 1 TB read into a small one.

But the map acquired a second job, and this is where it reaches into your query plans.

Consider SELECT count(*) FROM orders WHERE customer_id = 88, with an index on customer_id. In principle the index alone answers this, since it contains customer_id values. So why touch the table at all? Because of everything in section 9: the index entry does not carry visibility information. An index entry might point at a tuple that was deleted, or one created by a transaction you cannot see. Only the tuple header knows.

So the escape hatch is this. For each index entry, check the visibility map. If the target page is marked all-visible, then every tuple on it is visible to everyone, so this one certainly is, and the heap fetch can be skipped. If it is not marked, the heap must be visited to check.

Now the operational consequence, which surprises people badly.

An Index Only Scan in EXPLAIN is not a promise. It is a hope. Run EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS) and read the Heap Fetches: line. On a freshly-vacuumed table it says 0 and the query is fast. On a recently-written table it can equal the row count, and the "index-only" scan is doing exactly as much work as a plain index scan plus overhead.

Which yields a sentence connecting two things you would never have connected:

Whether vacuum ran recently is an input to your query performance, not just to your disk usage.

Ravi's reporting query can be 20× slower on Monday than on Sunday, because a bulk load on Sunday night cleared the all-visible bits on every page it touched and autovacuum has not caught up. Nothing in the plan changed. Nothing in the statistics changed. This is also why manually running VACUUM after a large bulk load measurably speeds up subsequent reads, even though there are no dead tuples to remove: you are setting visibility-map bits, not collecting garbage.

18. Who decides when to vacuum, and why did it skip your table?

In one breath: A background daemon decides, using one arithmetic rule. That rule waits until a fifth of your table is garbage, which is fine on a small table and ruinous on a huge one.

Nobody runs VACUUM by hand in production. A background daemon does it. Knowing its decision rule is what lets you predict why it did not run on the table you cared about.

An autovacuum launcher wakes every autovacuum_naptime, which defaults to 1 minute. It picks databases and starts up to autovacuum_max_workers worker processes, which defaults to 3. A worker examines each table's statistics and vacuums it if a threshold is crossed.

Here is the trigger:

dead_tuples  >  autovacuum_vacuum_threshold  +  autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor × reltuples
                          (default 50)                    (default 0.2)

Read that as a sentence: "vacuum when 20% of the table is dead." Read the last column: it is what that rule costs at each table size.

Table size Dead tuples before autovacuum starts What that means
100,000 rows about 20,000 fine; a small vacuum, often
100,000,000 rows about 20,000,000 the table is 20% bloated before anything starts

On the big table the vacuum, when it finally comes, is enormous. It takes hours, during which another few million tuples die.

The single most common autovacuum tuning change, and the one to reach for first: lower the scale factor on your big, hot tables, per table.

ALTER TABLE payment_attempts SET (
  autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor = 0.01,   -- 1%, not 20%
  autovacuum_vacuum_threshold    = 1000
);

Small, frequent vacuums instead of rare, huge ones. The total work is similar; the peak is a fraction, and the bloat ceiling drops from 20% to 1%. Some shops go further and set scale_factor = 0 with a fixed threshold, making the trigger purely absolute.

Why did an insert-only table never get vacuumed at all?

There is a second trigger, for inserts only, added in PostgreSQL 13. It uses autovacuum_vacuum_insert_threshold, which defaults to 1000, and autovacuum_vacuum_insert_scale_factor, which defaults to 0.2.

It exists because an insert-only table generates no dead tuples at all. It therefore never crossed the first trigger, and so it never got its visibility map set or its tuples frozen. Append-only tables historically sat untouched until they hit an anti-wraparound emergency, and every index-only scan on them fetched the heap. If you run an older major version, this is a real gap you must cover with a scheduled manual VACUUM.

When does autovacuum refuse to get out of the way?

Autovacuum is a polite guest, with one exception that will bite you. A normal autovacuum worker holds only a SHARE UPDATE EXCLUSIVE lock, which does not block reads or writes. It also cancels itself the moment somebody requests a conflicting lock, such as an ALTER TABLE or an index build. That is why routine autovacuum is invisible.

The exception is an anti-wraparound autovacuum, which is section 19's subject. It does not yield. It cannot, because the alternative is the database shutting down.

So the shape of a classic outage is this. A migration takes ACCESS EXCLUSIVE. It queues behind a six-hour anti-wraparound vacuum. Every query on that table then queues behind the migration. The table is up, the vacuum is working correctly, and the application is completely down.

If a DDL statement is hanging, check pg_stat_activity for an autovacuum worker on that table, and look at whether its query text says (to prevent wraparound). Killing it is usually the wrong move. It will simply restart, and the wraparound clock is still ticking.

19. What happens when the transaction counter runs out?

In one breath: Badge numbers are 32 bits, so they run out and start again. A row whose badge falls too far behind stops looking old and starts looking like the future, and a row in the future is invisible to everybody.

Everything so far treated badge numbers as plain increasing integers. They are 32-bit, which gives about 4.29 billion values, and a busy system can burn through that in weeks. So they wrap around to the beginning.

Now look at the visibility rule again and notice that it is built entirely on comparisons of the form "is this badge older than that one?". With a wrapping counter, "older" cannot mean "numerically smaller". PostgreSQL therefore compares badge numbers modulo 2³². From where you stand, the 2 billion badges behind you are the past, and the roughly 2 billion ahead of you are the future.

That is a circle, not a line. And it has a lethal property.

If a tuple's birth stamp is allowed to fall more than 2 billion transactions behind the current badge number, it silently crosses over and starts appearing to be in the future.

A tuple in the future is invisible to everyone.

The row disappears. No error. No log entry. It is simply not in your table any more, and it is not in your backups either if they are logical dumps.

Meera's plot, recorded correctly in 2019 and never touched since, would simply cease to exist. This is the worst failure mode in PostgreSQL, and the whole freezing machinery exists to make it impossible.

🔮 Predict #6. A lookup table was loaded once, in 2019, and has never been written to since. It has no dead tuples, no updates, and no deletes. The database around it is busy and burns 300 million transactions a month.

Does that table need vacuuming? And if it never gets one, what does SELECT * FROM lookup eventually return?

Answer

Yes, it needs vacuuming, and eventually the query returns nothing at all.

If you answered "no — there is no garbage to collect", you were using the only model this lab has given you so far, and it is exactly half the truth. Vacuum has a second job that has nothing to do with garbage.

The rows in that table carry birth stamps from 2019. The current badge number keeps climbing. Once a stamp is more than 2 billion transactions behind, the modular comparison flips its sign, and the row starts looking as though it was written by a transaction that has not happened yet. Every reader skips it.

Nothing errors. Nothing is logged. The table is simply empty, and a logical dump of it is empty too.

That is why vacuum must eventually visit every page of every table, including tables that are never written to, contain no dead tuples, and belong to a schema nobody has looked at in years.

What is freezing?

Freezing marks a tuple as "created so long ago that it is visible to everybody, unconditionally, forever". Once frozen, its birth stamp is never compared again, so it can never cross to the far side of the circle.

(Mechanically: PostgreSQL 9.4 and later set a flag bit in the tuple's infomask rather than overwriting the birth stamp with a magic value, so the original badge number stays readable for forensics. Older versions really did overwrite it.)

Freezing is a second, independent reason vacuum must visit pages. It is the reason vacuum must eventually visit every page of every table, including tables that have never been updated and contain no dead tuples at all. A read-only lookup table loaded once in 2019 still needs to be frozen.

Which four ages govern it?

Read the middle column: it is the age in plain words, before the setting name.

Which second counter did you not know existed?

There is a multixact ID, also 32-bit, also wrapping, with its own autovacuum_multixact_freeze_max_age, which defaults to 400 million.

A multixact is allocated when more than one transaction holds a lock on the same row at once. That happens routinely from SELECT ... FOR SHARE and, much more often than people realise, from foreign-key checks, which lock the parent row.

Picture a tenants or accounts table where every child row points at the same parent. A schema with a heavily-referenced parent like that can burn multixacts far faster than badge numbers, and hit a wraparound emergency on a counter nobody was monitoring. If you have ever seen pg_multixact grow to tens of gigabytes, this is why.

How much of this changes between versions?

Everything above describes 32-bit transaction IDs. There is long-running work on 64-bit transaction IDs, which would delete this entire class of failure. It has been proposed and reworked in the PostgreSQL community for many years. As far as this lab can state, it has not landed in a released major version as of PostgreSQL 18.

Treat that as a claim to verify against your version's release notes rather than a fact to rely on. It is exactly the sort of thing that changes. The freezing machinery, the ages, and the monitoring query above are stable across every version you are likely to run.

In one breath: Every failure below is the same event in a different costume. Either the horizon stopped moving, or cleanup lost the race. The skill is telling the costumes apart in under a minute.

Now the ten shapes, told as short stories. Ravi's forty-minute report is the honest version of several of them; the dishonest versions are the ones that cause outages.

1 — the session that went to lunch. A session runs BEGIN, does something small, and stops. This is Predict #3, and it is the most common cause by a wide margin. Correctness is never at risk; only space is. In an ORM it is usually a transaction opened around an HTTP request that then makes an external API call and waits.

2 — a genuinely long query. A four-hour analytics job, or a pg_dump, which holds one snapshot for its whole run by design. This is Ravi, doing his job. Distinguishing it from shape 1 matters, because the fix is the opposite: you cannot just kill it, the work is real. Run it on a replica, accept a defined bloat budget for its duration, or make it incremental.

3 — Ravi's report, running on a different machine. A long query on a physical replica with hot_standby_feedback = on is the nastiest of the family. The cause is on a machine the on-call engineer is not looking at. The replica tells the primary "do not remove anything my queries still need", so the primary's horizon is pinned by a report running elsewhere.

The tradeoff here is explicit and you must choose a side. With feedback on, the primary bloats. With it off, the replica's long queries get cancelled with ERROR: canceling statement due to conflict with recovery. There is no setting where both are happy. This is a genuine PACELC-shaped "somebody pays" choice, not a misconfiguration.

4 — an abandoned replication slot. A logical replication or CDC consumer, such as Debezium, a decommissioned read-replica, or a failed upgrade, has a slot that nobody reads. The slot pins the WAL and, for logical slots, the horizon. That is why it often presents as two alarms at once, because it fills your disk with WAL as well. Drop the slot — and note the sharp edge from change-data-capture: dropping a slot that a real consumer still needs loses its position permanently.

5 — an orphaned prepared transaction. Somebody ran PREPARE TRANSACTION, which is two-phase commit, covered in two-phase-commit, and the coordinator died before resolving it. It survives restarts, holds locks and the horizon forever, and appears in no ordinary session view. It can go undiagnosed for days, because almost nobody runs the query that finds it. If max_prepared_transactions = 0, which is the default, you can rule this out instantly.

6 — autovacuum falling behind on a hot table. No horizon problem at all. Cleanup is simply slower than damage. The signature is n_dead_tup not dropping to near zero after each run. Fix it in this order.

  1. Lower that table's autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor.
  2. Raise vacuum_cost_limit.
  3. Drop unused indexes.
  4. Consider fillfactor, plus removing an index on a hot-updated column, to restore HOT.
  5. Raise autovacuum_max_workers last. Workers share the cost budget, so more workers each go slower unless you raise the limit too.

7 — autovacuum starved by locks. On a table receiving frequent DDL or LOCK TABLE, autovacuum politely cancels itself every time and never completes a pass. Find the DDL. This one silently escalates into shape 8.

8 — the anti-wraparound vacuum that will not yield. The inverse of shape 7, described in section 18. A migration hangs behind a vacuum that cannot be polite. Do not kill it. The wraparound clock does not stop, and it will restart.

9 — mass DELETE with no space returned. Section 14, and the 03:10 incident. Accept the reuse and do nothing, or reach for pg_repack. The proper answer is to partition, so the operation becomes a DROP.

10 — wraparound emergency. Section 19. Find and clear whatever is pinning the horizon, since shapes 1 to 5 are the usual causes, then let the failsafe vacuum run. Do not raise autovacuum_freeze_max_age to make the warning go away. That is turning off the smoke alarm.

21. What did you actually buy, and what did you not?

In one breath: You bought correctness that never bends, and you paid for it in space, speed and availability. Every failure in this lab is one of those three, and none of them is ever a wrong answer.

Here is what MVCC guarantees, as described in this lab.

Snapshot stability. Within one snapshot, a given row's visible version never changes, however long you take. Ravi's repeated reads return the same answer at 09:00 and at 09:39.

Readers never block writers; writers never block readers. Structurally, not by tuning. Visibility is a local function of data plus snapshot, so there is no shared resource to contend on for reading.

Dirty reads are impossible. Not "forbidden at this level" — impossible, because the visibility rule consults the commit log. This is why PostgreSQL's READ UNCOMMITTED silently behaves as READ COMMITTED, a fact you already recorded in isolation-levels and can now explain mechanically.

Rollback is O(1), and it leaves no correctness debt.

Cleanup is never visible. Vacuum only ever removes tuples that no snapshot could reach, so its actions are logically invisible. That is exactly the invariant you met as compaction-is-logically-invisible in btree-and-lsm. Same invariant, different engine.

And here is what it explicitly does not guarantee.

Not serializability. Snapshot isolation permits write skew, because two transactions writing different rows never touch each other's death stamp. You proved this in isolation-levels. PostgreSQL's SERIALIZABLE adds SSI on top of everything in this lab: it keeps the same version machinery and adds read-tracking and pivot detection. MVCC gives you snapshot isolation; SSI is a separate layer.

Not bounded space. There is no upper bound on table size as a function of live data. The bound is a function of the oldest snapshot, which is controlled by client behaviour you may not own.

Not bounded cleanup latency. Vacuum is best-effort and throttled.

Not freshness. A long reader sees old data, by design. Ravi reading Meera at 09:39 is the feature working.

The guarantees rest on three assumptions. Read the last column: it is which failure shape breaks it.

Assumption In words Violated by
every snapshot is eventually released somebody eventually puts their slip down shapes 1 to 5
tuples are frozen before they age 2 billion old entries get stamped "always visible" in time shape 10
cleanup throughput exceeds garbage production the clerk with the ruler keeps up, on average shape 6

Notice the shape of that list. All three assumptions are about liveness, none about safety. MVCC's correctness is never at risk from any failure in this lab. What is at risk is space, speed, and availability.

That is the trade you made back in section 3 when you chose not to make Asha wait. It is why an MVCC database fails by getting slower and fatter, rather than by getting wrong.

22. Where do the other four engines put the garbage?

In one breath: Every engine here lets Ravi read while Asha writes. They differ on exactly one decision: where the old copy physically lives. Whether they need a VACUUM at all falls out of that single choice.

Every engine on this page implements MVCC. Every one of them lets readers see old versions without blocking writers. They differ on one decision: where does the old version physically live? Everything else, including whether they need a VACUUM at all, falls out of that one choice.

PostgreSQL — old versions live in the table

What you have just learned. New versions are appended to the heap alongside the current ones, so the table is the version store.

Rollback is free. Reading an old version is free, because it is right there, in the same page, often in the same cache line's neighbourhood. Reading the current version pays for every dead tuple in the way. Cleanup is a separate, throttled, failure-prone background process, and it is called VACUUM. The bill lands as space in your tables and indexes.

MySQL and InnoDB — old versions live in the undo log

InnoDB updates the row in place in the clustered index, and writes the before-image into an undo log record in an undo tablespace.

Each clustered index record carries two extra fields. Read the middle column first.

The field In words What it points at
DB_TRX_ID which transaction last modified this row the writer's identity
DB_ROLL_PTR where the previous version of this row is kept one undo record

Old versions are therefore a linked list running backwards out of the table into the undo log. InnoDB's name for a snapshot is a read view. A reader holding an old read view that finds a too-new DB_TRX_ID follows DB_ROLL_PTR. It then reconstructs the older version by applying undo records, one hop at a time.

So Ravi, in InnoDB, gets his consistent view by walking backwards. That changes every entry on the scorecard. Rollback is expensive, because it is real undo work, exactly the ARIES shape from acid. Reading the current version is fast, because the table contains only current rows. Reading an old version is slow, and slower the older it is. Every hop is a random read plus a reconstruction. So a long transaction gets progressively more expensive as the chain it must walk grows.

Cleanup is done by purge threads, controlled by innodb_purge_threads, which defaults to 4. They discard undo records no read view needs, and physically remove delete-marked index entries.

The bill lands as undo tablespace growth and purge lag, measured by History list length in SHOW ENGINE INNODB STATUS. That is a noun you already recorded in the acid lab. You can now read it as "the number of undo records the oldest open read view is preventing purge from discarding." Watching that number climb is watching a PostgreSQL bloat incident in another language.

Two structural notes are worth carrying. InnoDB's secondary indexes have no per-row transaction ID. Instead each index page carries a PAGE_MAX_TRX_ID, and if that is old enough relative to the read view, the index can answer without touching the clustered index. That is InnoDB's visibility map, at page granularity, doing the same job as section 17.

And InnoDB transaction IDs are 48-bit counters, so there is no wraparound, no freezing, and no anti-wraparound vacuum. That entire family of PostgreSQL failure modes simply does not exist there. (MySQL 8.0 also finally made undo tablespaces truncatable, which earlier versions could not do — the direct analogue of "VACUUM does not shrink the file".)

Oracle — undo segments, and a different answer to slow readers

Structurally Oracle is close to InnoDB: in-place update, before-images in undo tablespaces, and consistent reads reconstructed by applying undo to a block as of your SCN.

The interesting difference is what happens when a reader is too slow. Oracle keeps undo for UNDO_RETENTION and then overwrites it. A long-running query that later needs a before-image which has been recycled does not get a wrong answer, and does not get a slow answer. It gets this:

ORA-01555: snapshot too old: rollback segment too small

Read that as a design statement, not an error message. Oracle has chosen to bound the space and sacrifice the reader. PostgreSQL has chosen to guarantee the reader and let the space grow without bound. Those are the only two options, and there is no third. This is the central tradeoff of the entire lab, stated as cleanly as it ever gets. In Oracle, Ravi's forty-minute report can be killed. In PostgreSQL it cannot, and Asha's table grows instead.

PostgreSQL once offered an opt-in switch to Oracle's side of that trade, called old_snapshot_threshold. It raised a snapshot too old error rather than retaining versions forever, and it was removed in PostgreSQL 17 after long-standing correctness problems. [Version-specific; verify against your release notes.] Which means that on modern PostgreSQL, you cannot opt out of unbounded retention. Your only lever is client discipline.

SQL Server — versions in a side store, in a different database

Snapshot behaviour is off by default. You enable it per database with READ_COMMITTED_SNAPSHOT ON, a switch you already flagged in isolation-levels as one that changes application behaviour with no code change. Or you enable it per transaction with SET TRANSACTION ISOLATION LEVEL SNAPSHOT.

Row versions get a 14-byte pointer appended, and the old versions are copied into the version store in tempdb.

The failure mode is therefore "tempdb filled up", and its cause is, again, one long-running transaction preventing version-store cleanup. Same disease, third pathology report. (SQL Server 2019's Accelerated Database Recovery moved this to a Persistent Version Store inside the user database, partly to fix exactly this.)

CockroachDB and Pebble — versions in the key encoding

You have this one already from cockroachdb. Every key is stored as key/timestamp → value, so versions are literally adjacent entries in the sorted keyspace. Old versions are removed by MVCC garbage collection once they are older than gc.ttlseconds, which defaults to 14400, or 4 hours.

Which puts CockroachDB firmly in Oracle's camp. Retention is bounded by time, and a read older than the GC threshold fails rather than being served. And the thing that stops GC for a backup or a changefeed is called a protected timestamp. That is a noun you already have. You can now recognise it as an explicit, named, revocable handle on the horizon, which is arguably what PostgreSQL is missing.

🔮 Predict #7. InnoDB has no VACUUM, no freezing and no anti-wraparound emergency. A colleague concludes that moving to MySQL would make the 03:10 incident impossible.

Answer

They are half right, and the half they are wrong about is the half that caused the incident.

The wraparound family really is gone: 48-bit transaction IDs do not wrap in any practical timescale, so no freezing, no anti-wraparound vacuum, and no migration hanging behind one.

But the lunch problem is untouched. The developer's read view pins purge exactly as it pins the horizon. Undo records pile up in the undo tablespace instead of dead tuples piling up in the table, and History list length climbs instead of n_dead_tup. If you answered "yes, no VACUUM means no bloat", you swapped one bill for another and did not read the invoice. The version store moved. The horizon did not.

In one breath: How much history a system must keep is decided by its oldest reader, and that reader is a client the database does not control. Find the horizon; find who holds it.

Strip away the storage layouts and every row of that table says the same thing:

In any MVCC system, the amount of history that must be retained is determined by the oldest snapshot still alive — and that snapshot is held by a client the database does not control.

Ravi is not an attacker. He is not even careless. He is an auditor doing a job that takes forty minutes, and the whole database's disk usage is a function of how long he takes.

Which yields a rule you can apply to any versioned system you meet for the rest of your career, including ones that do not exist yet:

Find the horizon. Find who holds it. That is your bloat, your undo growth, your tempdb, your GC queue, your S3 lifecycle backlog, your Kafka log retention, your git pack size. The mechanism differs; the shape never does.

A long-running transaction is not a slow query. It is a resource leak with a global scope, and it is the only kind of leak that a completely idle, read-only, unprivileged client can cause.

25. Which models in your head are wrong?

In one breath: Ten beliefs that sound right, each with the exact moment in this lab where it fell over. If you only remember one, remember that a successful vacuum removing nothing is normal.

Which beliefs about space are wrong?

"DELETE frees space." It writes a death stamp and returns. It consumes space, in WAL records and dirtied pages, and creates work for later. The counterexample is section 1's three-million-row delete, after which disk usage went up. The correct model: DELETE marks, VACUUM reclaims within the file, and only TRUNCATE, DROP, VACUUM FULL or pg_repack return bytes to the operating system.

"VACUUM shrinks tables." It makes space reusable inside the existing file. Its normal, healthy outcome is that growth stops, not that size falls. The counterexample is the manual vacuum in section 1 that completed successfully and freed zero bytes.

"Bloat means autovacuum isn't running." Usually autovacuum is running perfectly and is forbidden from removing anything, because the horizon is pinned. The counterexample is Predict #3 in section 12: dozens of successful vacuums, zero tuples removed, no errors. The first query is pg_stat_activity, not last_autovacuum.

"Adding a column with a default rewrites the table, so it is like an UPDATE." Since PostgreSQL 11 a non-volatile default is stored in the catalog and no rows are rewritten. This is one of the few schema operations that does not generate a table's worth of dead tuples. Worth knowing precisely, because the older folk wisdom is now wrong.

Which beliefs about concurrency and query plans are wrong?

"A read-only transaction is harmless." A read-only transaction is precisely the dangerous kind. It holds a snapshot while holding no locks, so every lock-based diagnostic shows a clean system. The counterexample is the developer at lunch in section 12.

"MVCC means transactions never block each other." Readers never block writers and writers never block readers. Writers absolutely still block writers. Two transactions updating the same row serialise on that row's death stamp, which is section 15. MVCC removes one of the three contention pairs, not all three.

"MVCC gives me serializability." It gives snapshot isolation. Write skew survives it, and you measured that in isolation-levels. PostgreSQL's SERIALIZABLE is SSI layered on top of the machinery in this lab. Oracle's SERIALIZABLE is snapshot isolation and permits write skew.

"An Index Only Scan doesn't touch the table." It touches the table for every row whose page is not marked all-visible. Read Heap Fetches in EXPLAIN (ANALYZE). Its performance is a function of how recently vacuum ran, which is section 17.

"Wraparound is a legacy problem that modern versions fixed." The failsafe in PG 14 and better freezing make it much harder to reach. But badge numbers are still 32-bit, and the shutdown behaviour is still there. It is rarer, not gone.

"An extra index just costs a bit of write amplification." On a frequently-updated table, an index on the updated column also destroys HOT for every update on that table, multiplying heap bloat and vacuum cost. That is section 16 and failure 11. Its cost is not local to itself.

What is this commonly confused with?

MVCC versus optimistic locking (optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking). MVCC is how the engine stores and shows versions. Optimistic locking is an application-level conflict policy. You can run either policy on an MVCC engine.

VACUUM versus LSM compaction (btree-and-lsm). Both discard shadowed data in the background. Compaction rewrites and merges files, and reclaims to the filesystem. Vacuum frees slots in place, and does not.

VACUUM versus garbage collection (jvm-garbage-collectors). These are strikingly close, and the difference is the interesting part. A GC decides death by reachability from roots: it never finds the dead, it defines them as everything it did not reach. Vacuum decides death by visibility relative to the oldest snapshot, which is a horizon, not a graph walk.

Both suffer the same pathology from the same cause. A long-lived reference keeps garbage alive, and that reference is a GC root in one system and an old snapshot in the other. In both, the cost is paid in a background process competing with your application. If it helps: the oldest snapshot is vacuum's GC root.

Snapshot (MVCC) versus snapshot (backup). Unrelated meanings of the word. A filesystem or volume snapshot is a point-in-time copy of bytes.

27. Glossary — what does each word mean?

In one breath: Every term this lab earned, grouped by where it lives in the machine. Each group is small enough to read in one go.

What are the words for the physical layout?

  • Tuple — one physical version of one row (PostgreSQL's usage). One row may be many tuples. Earned in section 6.
  • Version chain — all tuples that are copies of the same logical row.
  • Heap — the table's main data file: a sequence of 8 KB pages. Drawn in section 10.
  • Line pointer (ItemId) — a 4-byte slot at the top of a page holding a tuple's offset and one of four states: NORMAL, DEAD, UNUSED, REDIRECT.
  • ctid — a tuple's physical address, (page, line pointer). What every index entry points at. Changes on every non-HOT update.
  • infomask — the tuple header's flag bits (xmin committed, xmax is a lock, frozen, HOT-updated, …).

What are the words for the stamps and the snapshot?

  • xmin (tuple) — the XID of the transaction that created this tuple; the birth stamp of section 6.
  • xmax (tuple) — the XID of the transaction that deleted or superseded it; 0 if none. May instead record a row lock, distinguished by flag bits.
  • XID / transaction ID — a 32-bit, wrapping counter identifying a transaction; the badge number of section 7.
  • Commit log (pg_xact, formerly clog) — two bits per XID recording in-progress / committed / aborted. The board by the door in section 8.
  • Snapshot — (xmin, xmax, xip[]): the set of transactions a reader is allowed to believe. Not a timestamp.
  • Snapshot xmin / xmax — the snapshot's bounds; distinct from tuple stamps of the same name.
  • xip[] — the list of XIDs in progress when the snapshot was taken.
  • Visibility rule — created-by-a-committed-transaction-before-my-snapshot and not-deleted-by-one. Stated in section 9.
  • cmin / cmax — command numbers within a transaction, so a statement does not see its own output.
  • Hint bits — cached commit status written into a tuple header by the first reader after commit; why read queries can dirty pages (section 13).

What are the words for cleanup and its knobs?

  • Dead tuple — a tuple invisible to every possible snapshot; garbage.
  • Bloat — the gap between a table's live data size and its file size.
  • Horizon (OldestXmin, backend_xmin) — the minimum snapshot xmin across the whole system. Nothing at or above it may be removed. Section 12.
  • VACUUM — the process that removes dead tuples, cleans indexes, updates the free space and visibility maps, and freezes old tuples.
  • Pruning — in-page removal of dead tuples, done by vacuum and opportunistically by ordinary reads.
  • Free space map (FSM) — a per-table fork recording free space per page, so inserts can find reusable room.
  • Visibility map (VM) — a per-table fork with two bits per page: all-visible and all-frozen. Lets vacuum skip pages and lets index-only scans skip heap fetches. Section 17.
  • Cost-based delay — vacuum's deliberate self-throttling (vacuum_cost_limit, vacuum_cost_delay).
  • maintenance_work_mem — the memory budget for vacuum's dead-tuple list; overflowing it caused repeated index passes (much improved in PG 17).
  • VACUUM FULL — a full table rewrite into a new compact file. Takes ACCESS EXCLUSIVE; needs ~2× the space.
  • pg_repack / pg_squeeze — extensions that achieve the same compaction with only a brief lock.
  • Truncation — vacuum returning trailing empty pages to the OS; the only case where ordinary vacuum shrinks a file. Section 14.
  • Autovacuum — the daemon: a launcher plus up to autovacuum_max_workers workers, triggered by threshold + scale_factor × reltuples. Section 18.

What are the words for indexes and HOT?

  • HOT (Heap-Only Tuple) — an update that touches no indexed column and fits on the same page, so no index entry is created; the old line pointer becomes a REDIRECT. Section 16.
  • fillfactor — the percentage of a page filled at insert time; free space left behind enables HOT.
  • Bottom-up index deletion — a B-tree's attempt (PG 14+) to delete provably dead entries instead of splitting a page.
  • Index-only scan — an index scan that skips the heap fetch when the target page is marked all-visible. Heap Fetches in EXPLAIN tells you how often it failed to.

What are the words for freezing and the counter?

  • Freezing — marking a tuple as unconditionally visible to everyone, so its XID is never compared again. Section 19.
  • Anti-wraparound vacuum — a forced vacuum at autovacuum_freeze_max_age that does not yield to conflicting lock requests.
  • Transaction ID wraparound — the failure where an unfrozen tuple's xmin ages past 2 billion and appears to be in the future, making the row vanish.
  • relfrozenxid / datfrozenxid — the oldest unfrozen XID in a table / database; age() of it is your wraparound budget consumed.
  • Failsafe (vacuum_failsafe_age) — PG 14+ emergency mode: skip index cleanup and throttling to freeze fast.
  • Multixact — a second wrapping 32-bit counter, allocated when multiple transactions lock the same row (notably via foreign-key checks).

What are the words for whoever is holding the horizon?

  • idle in transaction — a session inside an open transaction running nothing; the most common horizon holder. Failure 1 in section 20.
  • Replication slot — a server-side position marker for a consumer; an inactive one pins both WAL and (for logical slots) the horizon. Failure 4.
  • Prepared transaction — a PREPARE TRANSACTION awaiting resolution; survives restart and pins the horizon indefinitely. Visible only in pg_prepared_xacts. Failure 5.
  • hot_standby_feedback — a replica telling the primary not to remove versions its queries need; trades primary bloat against replica query cancellations. Failure 3.
  • max_standby_streaming_delay — the other side of that same dial: how long a replica delays applying changes before it cancels a conflicting query.

What are the words the other engines use?

  • Undo log / rollback segment — where InnoDB and Oracle put old versions instead of the table. Section 22.
  • DB_TRX_ID / DB_ROLL_PTR — InnoDB's per-row transaction stamp and pointer into the undo chain.
  • Read view — InnoDB's name for a snapshot.
  • Purge thread — InnoDB's vacuum.
  • History list length — InnoDB's bloat metric: undo records not yet purged.
  • ORA-01555 "snapshot too old" — Oracle's choice to fail the slow reader rather than retain versions forever.
  • old_snapshot_threshold — PostgreSQL's removed (PG 17) opt-in to that same behaviour.
  • Version store — SQL Server's tempdb area holding row versions under RCSI/snapshot isolation.
  • Protected timestamp — CockroachDB's explicit, named handle preventing MVCC GC below a point, for backups and changefeeds.
  • gc.ttlseconds — CockroachDB's time-bounded retention (default 4 hours).

Solutions — MVCC and VACUUM

Worked answers. For the design rungs these are a line of reasoning, not the only one; what matters is whether your answer names the right mechanism and the right bill.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 (one acceptable answer)

Changing a row in place destroys the value that used to be there. That is fine if only one person is looking, but a long report reading the table needs the table to hold still for as long as it runs. If the only way to make it hold still is to stop everyone else from writing, then a read-only report can halt the business for forty minutes. So instead of destroying the old value, the database writes the new one somewhere else and makes a note against the old one saying when it stopped being current. Readers that started earlier can still find the value they need, and writers never have to wait for them. The price is that the table now contains copies nobody needs any more, and something has to come along later and clear them out.

Self-check: the words you were forbidden are the names; the paragraph above earns each of them without using any.

1.2 The right half: autovacuum genuinely does cause I/O and CPU, and on an under-tuned system the spikes are real — usually because the default autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor of 0.2 lets 20% of a large table die before anything happens, so each vacuum is enormous.

The wrong half: turning it off does not remove the work, it defers and compounds it. Three things then happen. Dead tuples accumulate with no ceiling, so every sequential scan and every cached page gets progressively more expensive. The visibility map stops being maintained, so index-only scans start fetching the heap. And most seriously, freezing stops — so eventually autovacuum_freeze_max_age is crossed and an anti-wraparound autovacuum starts regardless of your setting, does not throttle itself the way a normal one does, and does not yield to lock requests. You have converted a predictable continuous cost into an unpredictable one that arrives at the worst moment.

The actual fix for their observation is the opposite: make vacuum run more often on those tables (lower the scale factor) so each run is small, and raise vacuum_cost_limit so the runs finish instead of dragging.

1.3 All five are responses to the same thing: in an MVCC system somebody must retain old row versions until the oldest live snapshot no longer needs them, and the amount to retain is controlled by a client the database does not control.

The decision that splits them: is retention bounded? PostgreSQL, InnoDB and SQL Server retain whatever the oldest snapshot demands and pay in unbounded space — bloat, history list length, tempdb growth. Oracle and CockroachDB bound retention (UNDO_RETENTION, gc.ttlseconds) and pay by failing the slow reader — ORA-01555, must be after replica GC threshold. There is no third option: either the space is unbounded or the reader can be refused. (InnoDB sits nearer the first camp; Oracle nearer the second, though both use undo.)

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 The reader returns Q.

  • R is eliminated by the xip clause of step 1: xmin = 155 is in the in-progress list, so its creator is invisible, full stop.
  • P is eliminated by step 2: its xmax = 140 is below the snapshot's xmax, not in xip, and committed — so the deletion is visible to this reader, and a tuple whose deletion you can see is not there for you.
  • Q passes both: xmin = 140 is committed and visible (step 1), and xmax = 155 is in xip, so the deletion is not visible (step 2) — from this reader's point of view Q has not been deleted.

Note what that last line means physically: another transaction is right now deleting Q, and this reader is serenely reading it with no lock and no wait.

2.2 pid 9104 is the horizon holder — idle in transaction, 6h41m, backend_xmin age of 9.1 million transactions against pid 8812's 18 thousand. The query column shows SELECT 1, which is the giveaway: that is the last statement it ran, not what it is doing. It is doing nothing.

Confidence from this output alone: high for "9104 is the oldest of the three", but not high for "9104 is the oldest in the system", because pg_stat_activity only shows backends. The three horizon holders it cannot see:

holder query
a replication slot (a CDC consumer, a decommissioned replica) SELECT slot_name, active, xmin, catalog_xmin FROM pg_replication_slots;
a prepared transaction left by a failed 2PC coordinator SELECT * FROM pg_prepared_xacts;
a replica's query, via hot_standby_feedback SELECT application_name, backend_xmin FROM pg_stat_replication;

The third is the cruel one: the cause is on a different machine.

2.3 Heap Fetches: 40873 against ~41,000 rows means the index-only scan fetched the heap for essentially every row — it did all the work of a plain index scan plus the visibility-map lookups, for nothing.

The one fact it tells you: the pages holding those rows are not marked all-visible in the visibility map, which means vacuum has not processed them since they were last written. The table has recently been written to (or bulk loaded) and autovacuum has not caught up.

What to do: run VACUUM orders (not VACUUM FULL) and re-check. If it recurs, lower that table's autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor, and — if the table is insert-mostly on an older major version — schedule a periodic manual VACUUM, because before PostgreSQL 13 an insert-only table never crossed autovacuum's dead-tuple threshold at all.

The transferable lesson: whether vacuum ran recently is an input to query performance, not only to disk usage.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 A waits, blocking on B's transaction until B ends. This is first-committer-wins implemented physically: A tries to stamp row 5's xmax, finds a live claim there, and waits on the lock.

Because B has already committed by the time A tries, what happens next depends on the level, and the difference is exactly the one you measured in isolation-levels:

  • READ COMMITTED: A re-reads the newest committed version, re-checks its WHERE clause against it, and proceeds. This succeeds — and it is precisely the behaviour that permits lost update.
  • REPEATABLE READ / SERIALIZABLE: A gets ERROR: could not serialize access due to concurrent update (SQLSTATE 40001) and must retry the whole transaction.

So: same physical mechanism, two different policies layered on it.

3.2 Predict:

  1. Removable tuples: ~0 — or a small number from before the developer's session began.
  2. "nonremovable" / "dead but not yet removable": ~5,000,000, with a line naming the oldest xmin.
  3. Pages removed / truncated: 0.

The verbose output tells you this directly with a phrase like "N dead row versions cannot be removed yet, oldest xmin: 12345678" — that number is the horizon, and it is the number to take to the incident channel.

3.3 du before ≈ 60 GB. After ≈ 60 GB. Vacuum removed 24 GB worth of tuples and returned essentially nothing to the filesystem, because the freed space is scattered through pages in the middle of the file and only trailing empty pages can be truncated.

The next 24 GB of inserts: they consume almost no new disk, because the free space map directs them into the reclaimed slots. That is the whole point — the correct outcome of vacuum is that the table stops growing, not that it shrinks.

If you actually need the 24 GB back today: pg_repack (brief lock, needs ~2× space) or VACUUM FULL (long ACCESS EXCLUSIVE lock, needs ~2× space). If you need it back every month, the answer is not a tool, it is partitioning — make the operation a DROP of a partition.

3.4 The cause: the bulk load wrote 20 million rows, which cleared the all-visible bit on every page it touched, and autovacuum has not yet re-set them (and on a pre-13 major version, an insert-only load may not have triggered autovacuum at all).

Heap Fetches will be large — roughly equal to the number of rows the scan returns, where yesterday it was 0 or near it.

The fix is a plain VACUUM events at the end of the load job. Note that it removes no dead tuples whatsoever — its entire value here is setting visibility-map bits.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Per-table horizon. What it fixes: the worst property of the current design — that a transaction touching one table pins cleanup on tables it has never referenced. The developer at lunch would only bloat what they actually looked at.

What it costs and what must become true: a snapshot would have to record which relations it can still reach, and that is the hard part — a transaction can issue an arbitrary new query at any moment, so the database cannot know in advance that it will never touch sessions. To make it safe you would need either (a) transactions to declare their relation set up front and be refused if they exceed it, or (b) a mechanism to detect that a transaction has been pinned out of a relation and abort it if it later tries — which is Oracle's ORA-01555 in a new costume, i.e. you have moved from the unbounded-space camp to the fail-the-reader camp.

PostgreSQL has taken partial steps in this direction (notably distinguishing the horizon for regular tables, catalog access, temporary tables and replication slots) rather than a full per-relation horizon. Check your version's notes; the detail here moves.

4.2 fillfactor default 80.

  • Strictly better: an update-heavy table with no index on the updated columns — every update that fits becomes HOT, so no index entries are written, index bloat stops, and vacuum can clean the chains inside the page with no index scan.
  • Strictly worse: an append-only table (events, logs, audit). Every page is 20% empty forever, for no benefit at all: 20% more disk, 20% more pages per sequential scan, 20% less useful cache.
  • The metric: the ratio of n_tup_upd to n_tup_ins in pg_stat_user_tables, and specifically n_tup_hot_upd — if HOT updates are a small fraction of total updates on an update-heavy table, lowering fillfactor is likely to pay. That is a per-table decision, which is why the default is 100: the default should be the one that is never actively harmful.

Rung 5 — Trace

5.1

after tuples (row 12) T60 reads T61 reads T62 reads T63 reads removable
t3 A(50,61) · B(61,0) Meera Vikram — — 0
t6 A(50,61) · B(61,62) · C(62,0) Meera — Priya — 0
t7 same Meera — Priya — 0
t8 A(50,61) · B(61,62✗) · C(62✗,0) Meera — — — 0
t9 same Meera — — Vikram 0
t10 same (committed) — — Vikram 0
t11 same — — — — 2

(✗ marks a stamp written by a transaction that aborted.)

Three things worth extracting:

  • At t8 the rollback resurrected B. B's xmax = 62 still sits in the tuple header, physically unchanged; it simply stopped meaning anything the moment 62's commit-log entry said "aborted". Nothing walked back over the data.

  • At t9, T60 and T63 read different values simultaneously and both are right. T63 reads Vikram because 61 committed before it began and 62 aborted; T60 reads Meera because 61 began after it did.

  • The t10 cell is the trap. Most people write "now T60 has committed, so the horizon moves and A becomes removable." It does not. T63's snapshot recorded xmin = 60 when it began, because 60 was running at that moment, and T63 is still holding that snapshot. The horizon is the minimum over snapshots, not over live transactions — so T60's exit changes nothing until T63 also releases. Only at t11 does the horizon jump to 64, making A (xmax 61, committed) and C (xmin 62, aborted) both removable: 2.

    (Nuance worth knowing: at READ COMMITTED a new snapshot is taken per statement, so a mostly-idle T63 would refresh its snapshot on its next statement and release the horizon then. At REPEATABLE READ it holds for the whole transaction. Same trap, different duration.)

5.2 At t10, with the horizon still at 60:

  • Phase 1 (scan): reads every page, evaluates every tuple, finds 0 removable. Not a no-op — it did all the reading.
  • Phase 2 (index): the dead-TID list is empty, so 0 entries are deleted — but a vacuum still has to decide there is nothing to do, and in the general case this is where the full index scans happen. This is the phase that makes "vacuum ran and did nothing" expensive rather than free.
  • Phase 3 (reclaim): genuine no-op.
  • Phase 4 (freeze): tuple A has xmin = 50 against a current XID around 64 — an age of ~14, nowhere near vacuum_freeze_min_age. No tuple is frozen.

File size at the end: still 1 page. Nothing was removed, nothing was freed, nothing was truncated, and the vacuum reported success. That is exactly the shape of the incident in README §1 — and it is why the first query on a bloat page is pg_stat_activity, not last_autovacuum.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 Smallest failing scenario: two transactions.

T1 begins and takes a snapshot. T2 updates row 5 and commits. Now row 5's old tuple has a committed xmax, so the rule removes it. T1 reads row 5: the new version is invisible to it (created after its snapshot), and the old version no longer exists. T1 sees no row 5 at all — a row that has existed continuously throughout.

Guarantee violated: snapshot stability — "every reader sees a complete picture of the data as it stood at one moment, no matter how long it takes". Note that the failure is not staleness; it is a missing row, and a report that silently omits a payment is worse than one that is an hour old.

The missing clause is exactly the horizon: xmax must have committed before the oldest live snapshot, not merely committed.

6.2 The flaw: it handles creation and ignores deletion.

Smallest failing scenario: a row created by an ancient transaction (xmin = 100, long committed) and deleted yesterday (xmax = 900, committed). The index entry is still present, because index entries are only removed by vacuum's phase 2, which has not run. A reader with a current snapshot checks the stored xmin = 100, finds it comfortably older than its snapshot, declares the entry visible, and returns a deleted row.

There is also a second, subtler failure even for live rows: an index entry pointing at a tuple whose creating transaction later aborted would be returned, since xmin alone says nothing about the outcome.

This is precisely why the visibility map answers a different question. It does not ask "is this entry visible?" — it asks "is this whole page visible to everybody?", which is a claim about all tuples on the page, deletions included, and which only vacuum is in a position to make.

6.3 What it catches: a table that autovacuum has stopped visiting entirely — starved by locks (§20 failure 7), or with autovacuum = off set on it.

What it misses, all of which are more common:

  • Vacuum running perfectly and removing nothing, because the horizon is pinned. last_autovacuum updates every few minutes and looks healthy while the table triples in size. This is the single most common real incident.
  • Wraparound, which has nothing to do with vacuum frequency and everything to do with age(relfrozenxid).
  • Vacuum falling behind: it runs constantly and still loses, so last_autovacuum is always fresh while n_dead_tup climbs.
  • Big tables where the 20% scale factor means "recently" still allows enormous bloat.

The four metrics that do cover it: n_dead_tup per large table, max(age(relfrozenxid)), the oldest backend_xmin age, and the count of inactive replication slots.

6.4 The weakened rule removes a tuple whose xmax committed more than an hour ago even if a snapshot older than that still exists.

Smallest situation: a reporting transaction begins at 09:00 and takes 90 minutes. At 09:05 a row is deleted and committed. At 10:06 vacuum removes it under the "older than one hour" clause. At 10:20 the report reaches that row's table.

The user sees: a report that is internally inconsistent and silently wrong. Rows the report already counted in an earlier query are missing from a later one; a SUM of balances does not match the SUM of the transactions that produced them; a reconciliation fails with no error anywhere and no way to reproduce it, because by the time anyone looks the tuple is gone and the state that would explain the discrepancy no longer exists.

That is the difference between stale and wrong, and it is why the horizon rule has no time component at all.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 Three genuinely different approaches:

  1. Move the dump off the primary. Take it from a physical replica with hot_standby_feedback = off. Cost: the dump can be cancelled by recovery conflicts and needs retry logic; you need the replica. Does not fix: if you turn feedback on to stop the cancellations, you have moved the problem back to the primary unchanged.
  2. Stop using pg_dump for this. Use a physical/base backup (pg_basebackup, or a filesystem/volume snapshot with WAL archiving). A physical backup does not hold a long MVCC snapshot in the same way, and it is usually far faster for 800 GB. Cost: it is an all-or-nothing cluster-level artifact, not a selective logical dump; if compliance specifically requires a logical export, this does not satisfy it. Does not fix: the need to prove restorability, which you must now test differently.
  3. Shrink what has to be dumped. Partition the large tables and dump only the partitions that changed, or move cold data to a separate database. Cost: a schema migration and more moving parts. Does not fix: the hot partitions still take time.

A fourth, weaker option: accept the bloat and budget for it — lower the scale factor and raise the cost limit so the 12% is cleaned up promptly each morning, and size the disk for the sawtooth. This is a legitimate answer if the growth is bounded and reclaimed daily.

What none of them fixes: as long as one process needs a consistent view of 800 GB for six hours, something retains six hours of history. You are only choosing where.

7.2 The frame: hot_standby_feedback decides which machine pays for the replica's long snapshots.

  • On: the primary retains versions the replica's queries need. Buys: analytics queries always complete. Pays: primary bloat, proportional to query duration × primary write rate.
  • Off: the primary cleans up on its own schedule; when replay needs to remove a version a replica query still needs, that query is cancelled. Buys: a clean primary. Pays: a third of the analytics work is thrown away.

Two other levers that change the shape rather than picking a side:

  • max_standby_streaming_delay — how long replay is allowed to wait before cancelling. Raising it buys the queries more time at the cost of replica lag (and therefore a longer failover recovery). This is a third position, not a compromise between the first two.
  • A dedicated replica. Point analytics at a replica with feedback off and a large standby delay, kept out of the failover set. Its lag no longer matters.
  • Logical replication or a separate warehouse. Removes the coupling entirely and is the real answer if this workload is permanent.

What I would do: dedicated analytics replica, hot_standby_feedback = off, generous max_standby_streaming_delay, excluded from failover — and start planning the warehouse, because 40-minute queries against an OLTP schema is a signal about architecture, not about vacuum.

7.3 Runbook.

Check, in order:

  1. pg_stat_activity — confirm the autovacuum worker on payment_attempts and confirm its query text says (to prevent wraparound).
  2. SELECT age(relfrozenxid) FROM pg_class WHERE relname = 'payment_attempts' — how urgent is it, really? Past 1 billion is serious; near 2 billion is an emergency.
  3. pg_stat_progress_vacuum — which phase, how far through. This is the difference between "20 more minutes" and "6 more hours".
  4. pg_locks — confirm the migration is what everything else is queued behind.

Do:

  • Cancel the migration, not the vacuum. pg_cancel_backend() on the DDL releases the queue immediately, and every normal query resumes — a normal autovacuum holds only SHARE UPDATE EXCLUSIVE and blocks nothing you care about.
  • Then let the anti-wraparound vacuum finish. On PostgreSQL 14+ check whether vacuum_failsafe_age has kicked in; if not and it is urgent, raising vacuum_cost_limit (or setting the delay to 0) for this table speeds it up substantially and can be changed live.
  • Afterwards: find out why it reached the anti-wraparound threshold. That is the actual incident. It is nearly always a pinned horizon (§20 failures 1–5) or a table where autovacuum was disabled or starved.

Do not:

  • Kill the vacuum. It will restart within a minute, having lost its progress, and the wraparound clock has not stopped. You have made it strictly worse.
  • Raise autovacuum_freeze_max_age to make the situation go away. That is disabling the alarm, not the fire, and it shortens the time you have left.
  • Restart the database hoping to clear it. Recovery does not freeze anything, and the anti-wraparound vacuum starts again immediately.

7.4 No long-running transaction means the horizon is fine, so this is failure 6: cleanup throughput below garbage production, not a horizon problem. n_dead_tup falling overnight confirms it — vacuum wins when the write rate drops, so it is not blocked, just outrun.

Ordered fixes:

  1. Lower that table's autovacuum_vacuum_scale_factor to ~0.01 with a fixed threshold. Cheapest, safest, per-table, reversible. Small frequent vacuums instead of rare huge ones.
  2. Raise vacuum_cost_limit (globally or per table). The default 200 is a speed limit set for hardware two decades old; vacuum is very often not stuck but throttled.
  3. Restore HOT. Check n_tup_hot_upd against n_tup_upd in pg_stat_user_tables. If the HOT fraction is low: drop any index on a frequently-updated column, and set fillfactor to 80–90. This attacks the production of garbage rather than its removal, which is strictly better.
  4. Drop unused indexes (pg_stat_user_indexes, idx_scan = 0). Every index dropped removes one full index scan from every future vacuum, permanently.
  5. Raise autovacuum_max_workers — last, and only together with the cost limit, because the cost budget is shared among workers: more workers on the same budget simply means each goes slower.
  6. Structural: if the row is updated on every request purely to record something like last_seen_at, consider whether that write belongs in the database at all, or in a cache flushed periodically. The cheapest dead tuple is the one never created.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 payment_attempts.

  • Schema: range-partition by month (or week) on created_at, using declarative partitioning. Indexes become per-partition.
  • Retention: the nightly DELETE ... WHERE created_at < now() - 90 days becomes DROP TABLE payment_attempts_2026_05 (or DETACH then DROP). This returns the file to the filesystem instantly, creates zero dead tuples, generates almost no WAL, needs no vacuum, and does not require 2× free space. Every symptom in §1 is designed out rather than tuned around.
  • Monitor: n_dead_tup on the current partition only (the old ones are read-only and static); age(relfrozenxid) across all partitions — note the old partitions still need to be frozen, and on PostgreSQL 13+ the insert-based autovacuum trigger handles that, while on older versions you need a scheduled VACUUM (FREEZE); oldest backend_xmin; partition creation succeeding ahead of time.
  • New problems, honestly: partition maintenance is now a job that can fail (a missing future partition means failing inserts — use pg_partman or a tested scheduled task); queries that do not filter on created_at now scan every partition, so the planner's pruning must be verified on your real queries; unique constraints must include the partition key; and cross-partition foreign keys have limitations. Partitioning is a real cost, paid for a real return.

8.3 Choosing the engine on the MVCC bill.

The requirement — 2–4 hour consistent reads against the primary, never cancelled — is a requirement about retention, so read it as: "I need the system whose retention is bounded by the reader, not by a setting."

  • PostgreSQL-style (versions in the heap). The reader is guaranteed to complete; there is no snapshot too old on modern versions, so this maps onto the requirement exactly. The bill: 2–4 hours of every table's garbage is retained, every table, whether or not the query touches it — plus the visibility-map and index-only-scan side effects. Failure when the assumption breaks: disk fills; the database stops accepting writes. Loud, and recoverable by adding disk.
  • InnoDB/Oracle-style (versions in undo). The current table stays compact, so the OLTP side is unaffected by the long reader — genuinely attractive here. But the long reader is reconstructing old versions by walking undo chains that grow for the whole four hours, so the analytics query itself gets progressively slower, and on Oracle it can be killed outright by ORA-01555 if undo is recycled. That directly contradicts the stated requirement. On InnoDB you would be watching History list length climb for four hours, and purge lag has its own cliff.

On the MVCC bill alone, PostgreSQL fits the stated requirement better, because the requirement is "never cancel the reader" and PostgreSQL is the camp that never cancels the reader.

What to measure before committing: the primary's write rate during the analytics window (that, times four hours, is the retention bill in bytes — if it is 400 GB the answer changes); how much of the workload the analytics query actually touches; whether the consistent view genuinely must be the primary rather than a replica or a warehouse; and, if the answer is yes, whether partitioning can keep the churn away from the tables being read.

8.4 MVCC with no background cleanup.

You must give up unbounded retention — that is forced, because the only alternative to cleaning up later is not accumulating in the first place, or bounding how much can accumulate.

Three coherent designs:

  1. Clean up inline. Every reader that walks past a provably-dead version removes it on the spot. PostgreSQL already does a version of this (opportunistic HOT pruning, and hint bits). Given up: predictable read latency — reads now do write work and can dirty pages — and it only cleans what someone happens to read, so cold garbage lives forever.
  2. Bound retention by time and refuse late readers. Keep versions for T seconds; a reader that outlives T is aborted. Given up: the guarantee that a reader always completes. This is Oracle's ORA-01555 and CockroachDB's gc.ttlseconds, and it is the most widely shipped answer.
  3. Never delete anything; make history the product. An append-only, immutable store where every version is addressable forever, with cold data tiered to cheap storage. Given up: bounded storage, and the ability to actually delete data — which in a world of retention regulation is a substantive, not theoretical, loss. Datomic is the well-known system built on this trade; append-only event logs and event-sourcing are the same idea at the application layer.

The meta-answer, and the one worth keeping: the cleanup process is not an implementation wart you could design away with more cleverness. It is where the cost of "never destroy the past" is recognised. Remove the process and the cost reappears as a refused reader, a slower read path, or an unbounded disk bill.
