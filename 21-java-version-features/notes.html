BEGINNER MODEL — stages 1 to 9

1. Why does a language everybody likes start to die?

In one breath: Java 8 was loved, and the next version took three and a half years to arrive. In that gap, upgrading became frightening, so teams stopped doing it. A platform that nobody upgrades stops being able to fix anything for anybody.

Asha is the platform lead at Peepal Pay, a payments company in Pune. She owns one decision that everybody else's work depends on: which version of Java the company's forty services run.

For six years her answer was "eight". Not because Java 8 was best. Because moving off it was a piece of work nobody could find room for, and it got bigger every year they waited.

She was not unusual. She was the normal case, and the normal case is the subject of this lab.

Java 8 came out in March 2014. It was enormously popular. Lambdas and streams changed how people wrote Java, and the whole industry moved to it happily.

The next version, Java 9, came out in September 2017.

Read those two dates again, because the gap between them is the entire problem.

The release When it arrived Gap since the one before
Java 8 March 2014 —
Java 9 September 2017 three and a half years

In that gap something bad happened, and it had nothing to do with Java's technical quality. Teams stopped upgrading. Years after Java 11 shipped, surveys of production Java still found version 8 in the majority of deployments. An enormous share of the world's backend software was sitting on a version whose free public updates had ended.

That is the problem this lab is about. It is not "Java lacked feature X". It is that the way Java shipped releases made upgrading feel dangerous.

And a platform nobody upgrades slowly dies. Its security fixes reach nobody, because nobody takes the new version. Its new work reaches nobody, for the same reason. The platform can still be excellent and still be irrelevant.

Everything below is the story of how that got fixed, and of the features that fell out of the fix.

2. What is wrong with a shuttle that waits for everybody?

In one breath: Picture the office shuttle that will not leave until the last colleague has boarded. One late colleague delays everyone. Worse, when it finally arrives, ninety-one people get off at once and nobody can tell whose ticket jammed the barrier.

Forget compilers for a page.

Peepal Pay runs one shuttle from the office to the train station. It is the only shuttle. Its rule is simple and, on a normal day, sensible:

The shuttle leaves when the last passenger has boarded.

On a normal day everybody arrives, everybody boards, the shuttle goes. Fine.

Then comes the day Priya is late. Priya is bringing a large and complicated piece of luggage, and she has been running late for a while now.

Everybody already aboard sits and waits. Meera is aboard with a laptop bag. Dev is aboard with nothing at all. Sam has a small backpack. Wei is carrying nothing. Forty other people who arrived on time are in their seats. Every one of them is ready. Their journeys are trivially simple. They wait anyway, because the rule says the shuttle leaves when the last passenger has boarded, and Priya has not boarded.

Priya eventually arrives. Three and a half years late.

Now here is the part that matters, and it is not the delay.

When the shuttle finally pulls in at the station, ninety-one people get off at once. The station staff who have to check every ticket now face a crowd. If anything is wrong with anybody's ticket, it is wrong in the middle of ninety-one people, and nobody can tell whose ticket caused the jam.

3. Why not just cut the late feature?

In one breath: The obvious fix is to hold the date and drop whatever is not ready. Every engineer proposes it within ten seconds. It is also how most software teams already work.

Dev says it out loud in the retro, and he is right to.

Hold the deadline. Drop whatever is not finished. If Priya's luggage is not packed by the departure time, the shuttle goes without her and she catches the next one. If the module system is not done by the date, ship Java 9 without it and put it in Java 10.

Reasonable. Standard practice. And it does not work here. The next section is why.

4. Why does cutting the late feature not help either?

In one breath: It fails twice. Under a slow schedule, "catch the next one" means "wait three more years", so the deadline always loses. And even a release with the late feature cut still arrives as a crowd.

The shallow reason first.

Under a schedule where releases are years apart, "put it in the next one" means "delay it by three more years". Nobody agrees to that. So the pressure to hold the shuttle is enormous, and it wins every time. A deadline everybody knows will slip is not a deadline.

The deep reason is the interesting one.

Cutting the late feature does not fix the crowd at the ticket barrier. Suppose Java 9 had shipped on time in 2016 without the module system, carrying only its other ninety features. That is still a two-and-a-half-year gap from Java 8. It is still ninety changes arriving at once. Asha's upgrade is still a project with a plan, a risk register and a name.

And here is the trap that closes behind her:

An upgrade that is a project gets scheduled against other projects. It loses. So you skip it. So the next upgrade is even bigger. So it loses even harder.

Engineers call a mechanism like that a ratchet: it only turns one way. Every skipped upgrade makes the next one more expensive, which makes skipping it more likely, which makes the one after that worse again.

Asha's team did not get stuck on Java 8 because they liked Java 8. They got stuck because by the time they looked up, the jump was too big to justify — and it grew every year they waited.

🔮 Predict: Java's answer was to release more often — every six months instead of every few years. But that means more upgrades for Asha, not fewer. How does adding upgrades make upgrading less painful?

Reveal

If you guessed "it doesn't, they just made peace with the pain", you are reading upgrade cost as if it adds up. It does not.

Upgrade pain is not linear in the number of upgrades. It is superlinear in the size of each one. Twelve small steps cost far less in total than one step containing the same total content. Three reasons:

  1. You can tell what broke. When 4 things changed and Meera's test suite goes red, she can find which of the 4 did it. When 400 things changed, she cannot, and that debugging is what actually eats the schedule.
  2. It stops being a project. A change small enough to absorb inside normal maintenance never gets scheduled against a feature. It just happens. That is the ratchet running backwards.
  3. Warnings get runway. Small frequent releases let the platform warn you in one release, warn louder two later, and only remove five later. You always meet a removal you were told about twice. You will see that exact three-step ladder again and again below; it is the single most important pattern in this lab.

The bet Java made in 2017 was that frequency is a safety feature. The rest of this document is that bet being paid off — and the places where it was not.

5. What has to be true for a thirty-year-old platform to keep moving?

In one breath: Strip the story away and three things must hold at the same time. Steps must be small and land on a date. It must be possible to ship a language feature and still change it later. And there must be a version a conservative bank can sit on.

Asha does not need a story. She needs a list of conditions. Here they are.

First, each step must be small enough to absorb, and must arrive on a date she can plan around. Not "when it's ready". A date. Asha schedules people against calendars, not against hopes. That is condition one.

Second, it must be possible to ship a language feature and still change it afterwards. That is condition two. It is the hard one, and it looks impossible.

A library method that turns out wrong can be deprecated and eventually removed. A piece of language syntax that turns out wrong is in a hundred million source files forever. Java is still carrying Object.finalize(), Java serialization and java.util.Date — mistakes from the 1990s that can never be taken back.

Getting language design right requires real user feedback. Real user feedback requires shipping. Shipping, under Java's compatibility promise, means committing forever. Those two requirements appear to contradict each other, and section 6 is where the contradiction gets broken.

Third, there must be a version a conservative organisation can sit on. Call that condition three. A bank that will not move every six months needs a target that keeps receiving security fixes for years. Without it, condition one relocates the problem: Asha would be choosing between upgrading twice a year forever and running unpatched.

The rest of this lab is three inventions, one per condition.

6. What are the three inventions?

In one breath: The shuttle leaves on the timetable whether or not Priya boarded. Some passengers wear a badge saying "I may be redesigned". And a few routes keep running for years after the next shuttle departs.

Invention one — the release train. The shuttle leaves in March and in September, every year, whether or not Priya has boarded. A feature that is not ready rides the next one, six months later. Because six months is a short wait, "it isn't ready, hold it" becomes a cheap and ordinary decision instead of a disaster.

Invention two — preview features. A feature can ship complete and finished but explicitly not permanent. You must pass a flag to both compile and run it, and the platform marks the compiled output so the feature physically cannot escape into production unnoticed. This is how condition two stops being a contradiction. The feature is in real users' hands generating real feedback, while the design is still legally changeable.

Invention three — long-term support. Every so often one of the trains is designated as the one vendors keep patching for years. Everyone else's train stops receiving updates the moment the next one departs, six months later.

Those three, together, are the entire mechanism. Everything else in this lab is consequence.

7. What do engineers call all of this?

In one breath: Six words appear in every Java release note. You have now met the problems each of them names, so each one is a short story rather than a definition.

Asha opens the release note for a new JDK. Six words she half-knows are in the first paragraph. Each one below is introduced here because you have already met the problem it names. The precise definitions are collected in the glossary in section 16.

What is a JEP, and why does everyone quote numbers?

Somebody wants to change Java. Before any code is written, they write a numbered public document proposing that one change. It gets discussed in the open, it gets a number, and that number becomes the permanent record of the change. That document is a JEP, a JDK Enhancement Proposal.

The process itself is defined by JEP 1, and the release process by JEP 3. Java is a platform that describes itself in its own numbering scheme.

A JEP moves through a fixed set of stages and is targeted at a specific release. Read the middle column as "what it means when you see this in a tracker".

Stage of a JEP's life What it means Where it matters to you
Draft somebody is writing it up; nothing is promised ignore it
Candidate accepted as worth considering early signal only
Proposed to Target aimed at a named release this is when a date first appears
Targeted accepted into that release's plan plan around it, carefully
Integrated the code is in the build it will very likely ship
Closed / Delivered it shipped in that release this is what section 10's tables record

Why does Java need this word at all? Because it needed a way to discuss a change publicly and durably before building it, separate from the legal specification process. That legal process is the JCP, and its documents are JSRs. A JEP is engineering intent; a JSR is specification change. Big JEPs feed a JSR. Most JEPs never need one.

Concretely: JEP 444 is "Virtual Threads", delivered in JDK 21. When somebody in a Java conversation says "444", they mean exactly that document, and you can read it at openjdk.org/jeps/444.

You have already used this vocabulary without knowing the map it lives on. The java-virtual-threads lab is JEP 444. The scoped-values lab is JEP 506. This lab is that map.

What does GA mean, and what happens just before it?

GA stands for General Availability: the day a version is officially released for production use.

It is the final milestone of the schedule JEP 3 defines. Before it come two "rampdown" phases, in which the bar for accepting a fix rises twice, and then two release candidates. Nothing new gets in during rampdown; the release is being made boring on purpose.

The word exists to separate a release Asha may run in production from the early-access builds that precede it by months. Early-access builds of the next Java exist continuously, and she can download one today.

Why is LTS not a property of the build?

LTS stands for Long-Term Support: a release that keeps getting security fixes for years.

Here is the part people get wrong, and it is worth getting right before Asha sits in a vendor meeting. LTS is not a technical property of the build. There is no code in JDK 21 that makes it LTS. There is no flag, no marker, no different compilation.

It is a support commitment made by vendors. The OpenJDK project ships every release identically. Oracle, Adoptium, Amazon, Azul, Red Hat and others then choose which ones they will keep patching. They have converged on the same ones, which is why it feels like a property of the release.

The word exists to give condition three a name. Without it, Asha's only choices are "upgrade every six months" or "run unpatched", and most enterprises would quietly pick unpatched.

🔮 Predict: A colleague asks Asha: "so what is technically different about a JDK 25 build that makes it LTS?" What should she say?

Reveal

"Nothing." That is the whole answer, and it surprises people.

If you guessed that LTS builds get extra hardening, more testing, or a different branch, you are describing how LTS works in some other ecosystems, not in Java. The JDK 25 artifact is built the same way JDK 24's was.

The follow-up that matters commercially: because LTS is a promise rather than a build, different vendors make different promises about the same release. Two teams can both say "we're on LTS 21" and have different end dates. Asha has to check her actual vendor, not a table she found online.

What makes a preview feature different from an unfinished one?

A preview feature is a finished language or VM feature that is deliberately not yet permanent, so its design can still change based on what users report.

"Finished" is the surprising word. A preview feature is fully specified and fully implemented. It is not a beta and not a half-built thing. What it lacks is permanence.

The rules come from JEP 12. The feature is disabled by default. To use it you pass --enable-preview to javac, along with --release <current>, and to java at runtime. A preview feature may change, or be removed entirely, in any later release, with no deprecation cycle at all.

Why does Java need this? Because of condition two. Without preview, the six-month train would be actively dangerous for language features. Asha would be watching the platform commit permanently to a design every six months instead of every three years, which is worse, not better. Preview is what makes fast releases safe for language design.

Dev can watch the mechanism work on his own machine. Structured concurrency is JEP 505, a fifth preview in JDK 25. He compiles a program that uses StructuredTaskScope:

$ javac --enable-preview --release 25 R.java
Note: R.java uses preview features of Java SE 25.

Then he runs it without the flag:

$ java R
Error: LinkageError occurred while loading main class R
  java.lang.UnsupportedClassVersionError: Preview features are not enabled for R
  (class file version 69.65535). Try running with '--enable-preview'

Why does an incubating API need a different word?

An incubator module is the same idea as preview, but for a whole new API rather than a language change.

The rules come from JEP 11. The API ships in a module named jdk.incubator.*, and you must explicitly add it with --add-modules jdk.incubator.foo. It is not on the module path by default, and code compiled against it produces a warning.

So why is this a separate word rather than a synonym? Because the two are enforced by completely different machinery, and confusing them causes real mistakes. Preview is a language or VM thing, gated by a compiler and runtime flag, and marked inside the class file. Incubator is a library thing, gated by module resolution, and marked by the package name.

That last detail has a sharp edge. An incubating API that graduates changes its package name. jdk.incubator.foreign became java.lang.foreign, so every import in every early adopter's code broke on graduation. That was not an accident. It is the design.

Concretely: the Vector API entered as an incubator in JDK 16 as JEP 338, and was still incubating in JDK 25 as JEP 508 — its tenth incubation. Ten. Section 10 explains why, and it is the most instructive entry in the whole dataset.

Why can a garbage collector not be previewed?

An experimental feature is a VM feature that is present but switched off, because it is not yet trusted in production. It is gated behind -XX:+UnlockExperimentalVMOptions. This is purely a HotSpot concept, with no language or API involvement at all.

The reason it needs its own gate is mechanical. Garbage collectors and JIT compilers cannot be "previewed" the way syntax can, because there is nothing to compile differently. Your source code does not mention the collector. So the gate has to be a runtime switch instead.

Concretely: ZGC arrived as experimental in JDK 11 as JEP 333, and became a production feature in JDK 15 as JEP 377.

Which gate is which?

Three gates, three mechanisms, three enforcement points. If you remember nothing else from this section, remember that these are not synonyms.

8. Which two pictures should you carry?

In one breath: Carry the shuttle timetable in your head for talking to people, and the feature pipeline for reasoning about releases. Then learn the three places the shuttle picture lies to you, because each lie costs money.

The simple picture: a timetable

Shuttles leave in March and September. Some routes keep running for years; the rest terminate as soon as the next shuttle departs. Passengers who are not ready catch the next one. Some passengers ride wearing a badge that says "I may get off and be redesigned".

That is the picture to carry around in your head, and it is the one Asha uses when she explains the plan to a product manager.

The engineering picture: a pipeline

Three properties of that pipeline are worth stating precisely, because they are exactly what the visualization lets you watch.

A tick advances at most one step. Six months of real elapsed time per transition. That is why "how many previews did it take" is a question answered in years, not in iterations.

A feature can hold. Structured concurrency was a preview in JDK 21, 22, 23, 24 and 25. That is five ticks. The API was substantially redesigned in the middle of that run.

A feature can leave the pipeline without ever reaching final. This is the property that justifies the whole design, and it has actually been exercised. String templates, JEP 430 and then JEP 459, previewed in JDK 21 and 22 and were then withdrawn, removed from JDK 23 with no replacement. That is the system working correctly, not failing.

Where does the shuttle story lie to you?

Three places. Each one costs somebody money.

Lie one: a late passenger is unchanged when they board the next shuttle. A held feature is not. This is the biggest lie in the whole analogy. The point of holding a feature for another preview is precisely that it gets redesigned in between.

Take StructuredTaskScope. In JDK 21 you used static factory methods called ShutdownOnFailure and ShutdownOnSuccess. By JDK 25 the whole shape had changed to StructuredTaskScope.open() with a joiner policy. Same passenger name, different passenger. If Dev learned the JDK 21 API, he has to relearn it. The pipeline has a mutation step the shuttle has no room for.

Lie two: an LTS release is not a different vehicle. JDK 25 is not built differently from JDK 24. Nothing in the artifact is special. LTS is a promise made about it afterwards, by vendors, and different vendors make different promises. The analogy makes LTS look like a bigger, sturdier bus. It is the same bus with a longer maintenance contract.

Lie three: shuttles are independent; releases are cumulative. Missing the 3pm shuttle costs Priya thirty minutes. Skipping JDK 22 costs her nothing at the time, and then appears in full when she jumps from 21 to 25, because 25 contains everything 22, 23 and 24 did.

That third lie has a consequence Asha needs on a slide. The train departs every six months, but the delta you actually swallow is set by how long you waited, not by the cadence. Frequent releases do not reduce your migration cost. Frequent upgrading does. The cadence only makes frequent upgrading possible.

10. How does a feature actually travel, and why does it look so strange?

In one breath: We are going to build the release mechanism the way a smart engineer would, and let each version break. Every strange detail of the real system is the scar of one of these failures.

Asha and Dev are now going to design the thing themselves. Five attempts. Each one fails in a specific place, and the fix for that place is a feature of real Java that otherwise looks arbitrary.

v1 — what if you just release every six months?

The simplest possible answer to condition one. March and September, fixed dates, whatever is ready ships.

Trace it on a real case. Switch expressions are the change that lets you write int x = switch (day) { case MON -> 1; ... }; instead of a statement switch full of break. Suppose Java ships it, final, in the first train that has it working: JDK 12, March 2019.

Now feedback arrives. In the version that shipped, producing a value from a multi-statement branch used break value;:

// The design that shipped in this hypothetical
int n = switch (day) {
    case MON -> { int t = compute(); break t; }   // 'break' with a value
    default  -> 0;
};

Users report that overloading break this way is confusing. break already means "leave the loop". Now it also means "return this value". Inside a loop inside a switch, you cannot tell which is meant.

Where v1 breaks. It has shipped. It is final. break value; is now in a hundred thousand source files. Java's compatibility promise means it can never be removed.

Java is now stuck with a syntax everybody agreed was wrong, forever. That is exactly the Object.finalize() disease from section 5 — except it now arrives twice a year instead of every three years. Version one has made condition two worse.

v2 — what if a flag on the compiler is enough?

So add a flag. javac --enable-preview lets you use the impermanent feature. Without it, a compile error. Done?

Trace it. It is 2019. Dev is enthusiastic about switch expressions. He adds --enable-preview to the build, writes break value;, compiles orders.jar, and ships it to Peepal Pay's artifact repository. The jar runs fine on JDK 12 in production.

Where v2 breaks. The jar either misbehaves subtly or crashes in a way that points at nothing. The compile-time flag protected the compiler. It did nothing to protect the artifact.

And the artifact is the thing that travels. Into repositories, into containers, into other teams' dependency trees — long after everybody has forgotten that --enable-preview was ever passed.

v3 — how do you stamp the artifact itself?

The fix is to put the mark in the class file, and make the JVM refuse to load it unless the conditions are exactly right.

Every Java class file starts with the same eight bytes. Read the diagram as four magic bytes, then a two-byte minor number, then a two-byte major number.

ca fe ba be   <minor:2>  <major:2>
└─ magic ─┘

cafebabe is the magic number that says "this is a class file". Then comes a two-byte minor version and a two-byte major version.

The major version is the Java release, offset by 44. Here are the four you will actually meet.

Major version Java release
52 Java 8
61 Java 17
65 Java 21
69 Java 25

Dev dumps the first eight bytes of a normal class compiled on JDK 25:

$ javac T.java && xxd -l 8 T.class
00000000: cafe babe 0000 0045
                     ^^^^ ^^^^
                     minor  major = 0x45 = 69 = Java 25

[RAN]

And now the same class compiled with the preview flag:

$ javac --enable-preview --release 25 R.java && xxd -l 8 R.class
00000000: cafe babe ffff 0045
                     ^^^^ ^^^^
                     minor = 0xFFFF = 65535   major = 69

[RAN]

One value changed: the minor version went from 0 to 65535. That is the entire mechanism. The JVM's class loader has exactly two rules about it.

Rule In words What it prevents
1 if the minor version is 65535, refuse to load unless --enable-preview was passed running preview code by accident
2 if the minor version is 65535, refuse to load unless the major version is exactly this JVM's own version carrying preview code across a JDK upgrade

Rule 1, observed on Dev's machine:

$ java R
Error: LinkageError occurred while loading main class R
  java.lang.UnsupportedClassVersionError: Preview features are not enabled for R
  (class file version 69.65535). Try running with '--enable-preview'

$ java --enable-preview R
42

[RAN]

Rule 2 is the one that closes the hole v2 left open. A normal class file compiled for Java 8 has major version 52, and it runs on every later JVM. That is the compatibility promise. A preview class file compiled on JDK 25 has major 69 and minor 65535, and JDK 26 will refuse it, because 69 is not 70. [CITED — JEP 12; not executed here, since only one JDK is installed for this lab.]

The consequence, stated plainly, because it is the single most practically important fact in this section:

A jar containing preview-compiled classes runs on exactly one Java version and nothing else. It cannot be published to a shared repository and used by others. It cannot survive a JDK upgrade. This is not a bug or an inconvenience. It is the designed property that makes preview features safe to ship. The mechanism deliberately makes it impossible for a preview feature to quietly become load-bearing infrastructure.

Which is why "we'll just use the preview API and deal with it later" is never a viable plan for a library. It is at most a viable plan for an application you control end to end and rebuild on every upgrade. Dev meets the consequence in person as failure F5.

v5 — why did the LTS gap shrink from three years to two?

The original plan was one long-support release every three years. Java 11 in 2018, then Java 17 in 2021.

Where v5 broke. Three years is six releases. An 11-to-17 jump swallows a great deal at once. The module system's encapsulation tightening, sealed classes, records, pattern matching for instanceof and text blocks all arrive together. So do three removals: Nashorn, RMI activation and the CMS collector. And a good deal more besides.

The encapsulation change in particular broke a large fraction of the ecosystem's reflective libraries at once. The crowd at the ticket barrier was back. Not as bad as 8 to 9, but bad enough that "we'll wait for the next LTS" became a common decision again — which is the ratchet from section 4 restarting.

The refinement. The cadence moved to two years: 17 in 2021, 21 in 2023, 25 in 2025, 29 in 2027. Four releases per jump instead of six.

It is the same argument as the original six-month decision, applied one level up. Smaller deltas, more often. Look back at the support tables in section 7 with this in mind. The change from three-year to two-year gaps is visible in the last column, and it is the only structural change to Java's release process since 2017.

11. What actually breaks when Asha's team jumps versions?

In one breath: Twelve things. Eleven of them fail loudly and cost an afternoon. One fails silently and reaches customers. Almost all of them were announced years in advance and ignored.

Everything so far has been the happy path. Now the failures. These are not hypothetical. They are the things that make a real upgrade take three weeks.

Before the individual stories, here is the pattern that connects most of them.

The three-step removal ladder. Java almost never removes something in one step. It deprecates it, which is a compile-time warning. Then it terminally deprecates it with @Deprecated(forRemoval=true), a louder warning that says removal is now committed. Then it removes it. Frequent releases are what make this affordable: each step gets its own release, so you get years of notice. Most upgrade pain is somebody having ignored steps one and two and meeting step three as a surprise. It was not a surprise.

Each failure below belongs to a member of Asha's team, because in a real upgrade each one lands on somebody's desk on a different day. Tab 4 of the visualization is a scanner over exactly this list, and you can filter it by your own from-version and to-version.

F1 — why does Priya's service refuse to start on JDK 9?

Priya (who owns the oldest service, ledger) puts two jars on the module path. Both contain classes in the same package. One is an old javax.annotation jar sitting alongside the JDK's own copy. Two shaded copies of the same library do it just as well.

The service does not start. At startup she gets java.lang.LayerInstantiationException, saying the package javax.annotation is in both module A and module B. At compile time she would instead get a module resolution error naming both modules.

The reason is the module system's core rule: a package belongs to exactly one module. That is not a style preference. It is what makes reliable configuration possible, because the runtime can decide which module exports a given package without ambiguity. Java 8's flat classpath allowed duplicates and silently picked whichever appeared first, which is where a decade of "works on my machine" classpath bugs came from.

The platform's answer is a hard failure at resolution time, deliberately not a warning. The whole value of the rule is that it holds.

What Priya actually does is nothing at all — and this is the single most useful fact in this section. The classpath is exempt. Everything on the classpath lands in the unnamed module, where split packages are still tolerated. That is why the vast majority of applications moved to Java 9 and beyond without ever writing a module-info.java. You do not have to adopt modules to use modern Java. The JDK itself is modular; your application does not have to be.

F2 — why does Arjun's SOAP integration stop compiling on JDK 11?

Arjun owns the integrations that talk to banks over SOAP. His code imports javax.xml.bind.* for JAXB. On JDK 11 it stops compiling with package javax.xml.bind does not exist — a plain compile error, as if he had made a typo.

The same thing happens for javax.xml.ws.* (JAX-WS), javax.annotation.*, javax.activation.*, javax.transaction.* and org.omg.*. All of them went in JDK 11, under JEP 320.

The reason is that these were Java EE specifications that had been bundled into Java SE. Java EE moved to the Eclipse Foundation and became Jakarta EE. Shipping a frozen copy inside the JDK meant every Java SE user was carrying a stale implementation of somebody else's specification.

The ladder ran here too. JDK 9 laid the groundwork and deprecated them for removal, JDK 9 and 10 both warned, and both offered --add-modules java.xml.bind as a temporary bridge. JDK 11 removed them.

Arjun's fix is to add the artifacts as ordinary dependencies: jakarta.xml.bind:jakarta.xml.bind-api plus a runtime such as org.glassfish.jaxb:jaxb-runtime. Note the package rename from javax.* to jakarta.* in current versions. That rename is a separate migration caused by a trademark constraint, not by the JDK. In a Spring codebase it is the same javax.* to jakarta.* move that Spring Boot 3 required.

F3 — why does Arjun's ORM throw InaccessibleObjectException on JDK 17?

This is the big one. It is the failure that made 8 to 17 hard for the entire ecosystem, so read it twice.

Arjun's ORM reflects into a JDK internal field. Reduced to two lines, that is:

Field f = String.class.getDeclaredField("value");
f.setAccessible(true);

On JDK 25 it throws:

java.lang.reflect.InaccessibleObjectException: Unable to make field
private final byte[] java.lang.String.value accessible: module java.base
does not "opens java.lang" to unnamed module @2b2fa4f7

[RAN]

Why does Java care? setAccessible(true) is deep reflection: it lets code read and write private fields of classes it does not own. Half the Java ecosystem was built on it — serialisation frameworks, mocking libraries, ORMs, dependency injection containers.

It also means the JDK can never safely change any private field of any class, because somebody out there is reaching into it. The module system's answer is that a module must explicitly opens a package for deep reflection into it, and java.base opens nothing.

The platform's response here is the ladder at its most careful, stretched over five years. Read the second column as what Arjun's two lines did in that release.

Release What setAccessible(true) on a JDK internal did
9 Allowed, with a one-time warning per offending package. --illegal-access=permit is the default.
10–15 Same. Warning stays. Five releases of notice.
16 (JEP 396) Default flips to --illegal-access=deny. Now it fails — but --illegal-access=permit gets the old behaviour back.
17 (JEP 403) The --illegal-access option is removed entirely. There is no global escape hatch left.
25 As above. Only per-package --add-opens works. [RAN]

The escape hatch that remains is explicit and narrow:

$ java U
BLOCKED: java.lang.reflect.InaccessibleObjectException: ...

$ java --add-opens java.base/java.lang=ALL-UNNAMED U
setAccessible OK

[RAN]

Arjun has three moves, in order of preference. First, upgrade the library. Every major framework fixed this years ago; if he is hitting it, he is usually running a 2019 version of something. Second, add the narrowest --add-opens he can to the launch flags, and treat each one as tracked technical debt with a ticket, not a permanent setting. Third, if the code is his own, replace deep reflection with VarHandle (JEP 193, JDK 9), MethodHandles.privateLookupIn, or the Class-File API from JDK 24.

One trap Meera watches for: --add-opens hidden in JAVA_TOOL_OPTIONS or MAVEN_OPTS is a common way teams hide this from themselves. Grep the CI config for it before claiming you are clean.

F4 — why is sun.misc.Unsafe warning in Meera's build?

Meera's build log grows a warning she did not add. Some dependency is calling Unsafe.objectFieldOffset and Unsafe.putInt, as a great deal of high-performance Java does indirectly.

At compile time:

W.java:1: warning: Unsafe is internal proprietary API and may be removed in a future release
import sun.misc.Unsafe;

At run time:

WARNING: A terminally deprecated method in sun.misc.Unsafe has been called
WARNING: sun.misc.Unsafe::objectFieldOffset has been called by W (file:...)
WARNING: Please consider reporting this to the maintainers of class W
WARNING: sun.misc.Unsafe::objectFieldOffset will be removed in a future release
unsafe wrote x=7

[RAN — note the code still ran and produced x=7]

sun.misc.Unsafe is a 2004-era internal class that became the foundation of the JVM's performance ecosystem. Netty, Cassandra, Hazelcast and many serialisers sit on it. It offers unchecked memory access with no safety net at all; a wrong offset corrupts the heap or crashes the VM.

It could not be removed until supported replacements existed for everything it does. They now do. VarHandle from JDK 9 covers the atomic and field-access uses. The Foreign Function & Memory API, final in JDK 22, covers the off-heap memory uses. That is why the eight-year FFM saga in section 10 mattered.

The ladder again: JDK 23 (JEP 471) terminally deprecates the memory-access methods, JDK 24 (JEP 498) adds the runtime warning above, and a future release removes them. As of JDK 26 that removal is not yet scheduled. [CITED]

The warning names the calling class, so Meera reads it. It is usually a dependency, not her code. If it is hers, field access goes to VarHandle and off-heap memory goes to java.lang.foreign.MemorySegment and Arena. She adds -Xlog:deprecation style checks to CI, so a new offender fails the build instead of quietly accumulating.

F5 — why does Dev's preview jar run on exactly one JDK?

Dev builds with --enable-preview, publishes the jar, and the JDK gets upgraded. This is the story from section 10, now happening to a real artifact.

He sees either UnsupportedClassVersionError: Preview features are not enabled when the flag is missing, or a class-version mismatch when the JDK moved. The mechanism is the one from v3: minor version 65535, plus an exact major-version match.

The platform refuses to load the class. Loudly, at class-load time, not subtly at runtime.

What Dev should do is never publish preview-compiled artifacts to a shared repository. For an application he fully controls, it is defensible if and only if he rebuilds on every JDK bump. That means the build must pin the JDK version, and CI must fail if it drifts.

F6 — why does Ben see wrong characters in a report on JDK 18?

Ben is on call. A customer report comes back with mangled names in it. Nothing threw. Nothing logged. This is the failure that reaches customers.

The trigger is any API that uses the default charset: new FileReader(f), new String(bytes), Files.readAllLines(p) and their relatives, on a platform whose default charset was not UTF-8. Most commonly that is Windows with a windows-1252 or Shift_JIS locale.

What Ben sees is nothing. No exception, no warning. Text that used to decode correctly now decodes to mojibake, or the reverse. A test comparing strings fails somewhere far away from the read.

Before JDK 18, the default charset came from the operating system locale, so the same program produced different results on different machines. That is the classic "it works on Linux CI and fails on a developer's Windows laptop". JEP 400 made UTF-8 the default everywhere.

The platform's response was a silent change, and that is the important contrast with every other failure here.

-Dfile.encoding=COMPAT restores the JDK 17 behaviour as a temporary bridge. The real fix is to stop using default-charset APIs entirely and always pass an explicit StandardCharsets.UTF_8. That advice predates JEP 400 by fifteen years.

🔮 Predict: F1 through F5 all fail loudly. F6 fails silently. Which class is more dangerous in a version upgrade, and what does that imply about how Asha should test one?

Reveal

The silent ones, by a wide margin, and it is not close.

If you guessed the loud ones because they stop the service, you are pricing the outage and not the detection. A loud failure is found by compiling and starting the application. It costs an afternoon and it is impossible to miss. Every hazard in this section that throws is, in practice, cheap.

A silent behaviour change is only found by a test that asserts on the changed behaviour, or by a customer. If Meera's suite does not cover the encoding of one particular file read, JEP 400 ships to production and shows up two weeks later as corrupted names in a report.

So: compiling and booting on the new JDK proves almost nothing. What Asha actually needs is the full behavioural test suite running on the new JDK. And specifically, tests that assert on output content, not just on "it returned 200".

The single most valuable practice is to run the existing test suite against the next JDK in a non-blocking CI job, continuously, starting long before anyone plans to upgrade. That converts the upgrade from a project into a stream of small known-in-advance fixes — the ratchet from section 4, run backwards.

The other members of the silent class, for the record: the removal of biased locking (F8 below), garbage collector default changes, and any performance characteristic at all.

F7 — why does Omar's rules engine throw a NullPointerException on JDK 15?

Omar owns the scripted business rules — the ones written in JavaScript and evaluated at runtime. On JDK 15 his code calls new ScriptEngineManager().getEngineByName("nashorn").

It returns null. His next line throws NullPointerException. He gets a null return, not a helpful error, and the stack trace mentions nothing about JavaScript.

Nashorn, the JavaScript engine inside the JDK, was removed in JDK 15 under JEP 372. Maintaining a full ECMAScript implementation inside the JDK was a large ongoing cost that only a small fraction of users needed, and JavaScript was evolving faster than the JDK release cycle could track. It was deprecated in JDK 11 under JEP 335 and removed in 15. The ScriptEngineManager service interface itself remains.

Omar's fix is to add org.openjdk.nashorn:nashorn-core as a dependency. That is the same engine, now maintained as a standalone project. His other option is to migrate to GraalVM's JavaScript. This one is worth knowing because scripted business rules in older enterprise Java frequently sit on Nashorn, and the failure surfaces as an NPE in a rules engine rather than as anything that mentions JavaScript.

F8 — why did Priya's throughput drop with no code change on JDK 15?

Priya's service acquires an uncontended synchronized lock in a hot loop, single-threaded. After the upgrade, nothing functional changes. Throughput regresses.

Biased locking was an optimisation that let the first thread to acquire a monitor keep it cheaply, on the assumption that most locks are never contended. It was hugely effective on 2000s-era code. But revoking a bias required expensive global safepoint operations. Its complexity was also blocking work on the VM's object header. That work became compact object headers: JEP 450 in JDK 24, and product feature JEP 519 in JDK 25. It was disabled and deprecated in JDK 15 under JEP 374, and removed later.

The platform's response was silent, with a deprecation warning on the flag only.

Nothing about synchronized semantics changed. Nothing in the java-memory-model lab's happens-before rules is affected. What Priya lost is a performance characteristic that was never promised.

Her fix is to measure before and after. If it bites, the answer is usually to stop locking in the hot path rather than to chase the flag. In practice that means java.util.concurrent atomics and ConcurrentHashMap.

One related change matters more than the regression. Since JDK 24 under JEP 491, synchronized no longer pins a virtual thread to its carrier. The pinning problem the java-virtual-threads lab describes is fixed as of 24, which changes the advice that lab gives for JDK 21.

F9 — why does Omar's Security Manager stop working on JDK 24?

Omar calls System.setSecurityManager(...), or starts the JVM with -Djava.security.manager=.... On JDK 24 he gets UnsupportedOperationException, or the JVM simply refuses to start, depending on how it was set.

The Security Manager was designed in the 1990s for untrusted applets. It was never effective for server-side sandboxing, because it protects against accidental misbehaviour rather than a determined attacker inside the same JVM. It imposed a permission check on every sensitive operation, and it was blocking platform work. It was deprecated for removal in JDK 17 under JEP 411 and permanently disabled in JDK 24 under JEP 486. Seven years of notice.

Everything else Omar relies on survives — TLS, the crypto providers, deserialisation filters from JEP 415. What is gone is in-JVM sandboxing.

The replacement is not another in-JVM sandbox. It is process-level isolation: containers, seccomp, separate JVMs, least-privilege service accounts. If Omar was relying on the Security Manager for a real security boundary, the honest reading is that he had less protection than he thought even before it was removed.

F10 — why does Meera's shared library warn about --release 8?

Meera builds a library that Java 8 consumers still use, on a JDK 25 toolchain:

$ javac --release 8 T.java
warning: [options] source value 8 is obsolete and will be removed in a future release
warning: [options] target value 8 is obsolete and will be removed in a future release
warning: [options] To suppress warnings about obsolete options, use -Xlint:-options.
3 warnings

$ javac --release 7 T.java
error: release version 7 not supported

The full supported set on JDK 25 is 8 through 25. [RAN]

The reason is bookkeeping. javac keeps a table of historical platform APIs for --release, and maintaining every one of them forever is unbounded work. The policy is to keep roughly the last several LTS-era releases. Warning first, then removal. That is the ladder again.

Note the asymmetry, because it catches people out. Meera can still run Java 8 bytecode, major version 52, on JDK 25. That is the core binary compatibility promise and it is not going anywhere. What expires is the ability to compile for arbitrarily old targets. Running old bytecode is safe; compiling for old targets is what expires. For Meera, this warning is her notice to plan the baseline bump.

F11 — why does Ben's profiler print a warning on JDK 21?

Ben attaches a profiler to a running JVM to investigate a latency spike. That is what profilers, APM tools such as Datadog, New Relic and Dynatrace, and some mocking libraries all do.

He gets: WARNING: A Java agent has been loaded dynamically ... If a serviceability tool is in use, please run with -XX:+EnableDynamicAgentLoading to hide this warning.

An agent can redefine any class at runtime, which is a complete bypass of the encapsulation that F3 spent five releases establishing. JEP 451's title is the interesting part: "Prepare to Disallow the Dynamic Loading of Agents". It is a JEP whose entire content is a warning, filed to start the ladder. It warns now; a future release will require the flag.

Agents loaded at startup with -javaagent: are unaffected and always will be. What is going away is silent attach-to-a-running-JVM.

Ben passes -XX:+EnableDynamicAgentLoading explicitly when he genuinely wants a profiler to attach. Better, he treats the warning as an inventory tool: if it fires and nobody knows why, something is instrumenting the JVM and somebody should find out what.

F12 — why does Asha's JVM refuse to start on JDK 24?

Asha's launch flags include -XX:+UseZGC -XX:-ZGenerational, copied from a blog post years ago. On JDK 24 the JVM fails to start, or the flag is rejected.

ZGC's generational mode arrived as JEP 439 in JDK 21 and was better on essentially every workload. Maintaining two modes doubled the testing surface. JDK 23 (JEP 474) made generational the default. JDK 24 (JEP 490) removed the non-generational mode. Default flip first, removal second.

-XX:+UseZGC still works and is still a low-pause collector. What is gone is the specific non-generational implementation.

Asha deletes the flag. More generally, GC flags are the most common source of upgrade failures that have nothing to do with your code. That line belongs in her upgrade checklist. She audits JVM flags on every LTS jump. A flag copied from a 2016 blog post is a liability. -XX:+PrintFlagsFinal and -XX:+IgnoreUnrecognizedVMOptions are her inventory tools.

12. What is guaranteed, and what did Asha pay for it?

In one breath: Java's willingness to change is bounded by promises that are precise. Knowing exactly which promise covers each of your dependencies is what turns "upgrading is scary" into "upgrading is planned work".

Pull the twelve failures together. Four things are promised, and the promises are worth reading slowly.

Old bytecode keeps running. Code compiled against the java.* and javax.* public API on an old JDK loads and runs on a newer JVM. Class file major version 52, which is Java 8, still loads on JDK 25. This is the strongest promise Java makes, and it is the reason the platform is worth building on at all. F10 is the demonstration: the output of --release 8 runs fine.

Old source keeps compiling, subject to the removal ladder. Code that compiled on one release generally compiles on the next. Exceptions exist, such as a new keyword or a new method on an interface you implemented. They are rare, announced, and usually mechanical.

Specified behaviour stays specified. If the specification says a method returns something, it returns it.

The ladder itself is a promise. Nothing in the specified API is removed without deprecation, then terminal deprecation, then removal, across multiple releases. You are always told twice.

Is there a one-sentence test?

There is, and Asha keeps it on a slide.

If the thing you depend on is specified in java.* or javax.* public API, you are covered by the strongest promise in mainstream software. If it is anything else, you are on your own, and you were told. "Anything else" means an internal class, a reflective reach-around, a performance characteristic, a GC default, or a preview feature.

Almost every painful Java upgrade in the last decade is an instance of the second half of that sentence.

"Pick a target JDK" Your JDK is chosen by the intersection of: your framework's supported range, your build tool's, your container base image's, your APM agent's, and your vendor's support dates. Spring Boot 3.x requires 17+; Spring Boot 4 requires 25. The framework usually decides for you.

What is the playbook?

Six phases. Asha runs them in this order, and the order is the point.

Phase 0 — before planning anything, find out what you already know. The platform has probably been warning her for years. Turn the warnings on:

javac -Xlint:all -Werror ...          # deprecation, removal, preview, options
java  -Xlog:deprecation ...           # what your dependencies are doing
jdeps --jdk-internals your-app.jar    # every JDK-internal API you touch, and its replacement
jdeps --multi-release 25 ...          # for multi-release jars

jdeps --jdk-internals is the single highest-value command in this lab. It names each internal API you depend on and the supported replacement, and it sees through your dependencies rather than only your own code. Run it before writing an upgrade plan, because it usually changes the plan.

Phase 1 — get the new JDK into CI before committing to anything. Add a non-blocking CI job that builds and runs the full test suite on the next JDK. Non-blocking is the important word: it must not be able to break the team's day, or somebody will delete it. Then let it run for weeks.

That turns the upgrade from a project into a stream of small known fixes. Projects lose to other projects, as section 4 showed. It is also the only defence against the silent failures F6 and F8. Compiling and booting proves almost nothing.

Phase 2 — upgrade the tooling first, in this order. Build tool, then build plugins, then test framework and bytecode libraries, then application dependencies, and only then your own code. Most "the new JDK is broken" reports turn out to be a five-year-old plugin.

Phase 3 — compile with --release, not -source and -target.

javac --release 21 ...       # correct
javac -source 21 -target 21  # subtly wrong

--release N also restricts you to the API of release N. -source and -target only affect language level and bytecode version. So you can compile against a JDK 25 method, emit JDK 21 bytecode, and get a NoSuchMethodError in production. --release catches that at compile time. This distinction has existed since JDK 9 under JEP 247, and it is still the most common build misconfiguration in Java.

Phase 4 — audit the JVM flags, every LTS jump. Asha dumps what the JVM actually resolved with -XX:+PrintFlagsFinal -version, then deletes anything nobody can justify. -XX:+IgnoreUnrecognizedVMOptions will get a JVM to start across a version boundary, but it hides exactly the signal Asha needs. Use it to survive the first boot, then remove it.

Phase 5 — measure, don't assume. GC changes, JIT changes and object header changes all move performance in both directions. Get a before-and-after on the real workload.

What does Ben see at 3am?

Upgrade-related incidents in Java have a small number of signatures. Ben knows them by shape.

  • UnsupportedClassVersionError: ... class file version 69.0 — something was compiled on a newer JDK than the one running it. Subtract 44 from the major version to get the Java version: 69 is Java 25, 65 is 21, 61 is 17, 52 is 8. A minor version of 65535 means preview, which is F5.
  • InaccessibleObjectException / does not "opens" — F3. Find the library, upgrade it, or add the narrowest --add-opens.
  • NoClassDefFoundError: javax/xml/bind/... — F2. Add the dependency.
  • NoSuchMethodError on a method that exists in the IDE — phase 3; somebody used -target instead of --release.
  • getEngineByName returned null, then an NPE — F7, Nashorn.
  • Latency changed and nothing else did — GC or JIT. Check the flags first (F12), then the collector, then compare -Xlog:gc output either side.
  • Mojibake in output, no exception at all — F6, the charset default. This is the one that reaches customers.

What do you get for free, and what is it worth?

Two things cost Asha nothing and are often misattributed to something else.

Class-Data Sharing has been on by default since JDK 12, under JEP 341, "Default CDS Archives". It memory-maps a pre-parsed archive of core JDK classes instead of parsing them at every startup. Her services have been getting this without asking since 2019.

Here is what it looks like on a small program that builds a 2000-entry TreeMap, streams over it, formats currency and does some date arithmetic:

$ java -Xlog:class+load:file=cl.txt Boot
1003 classes loaded, of which 956 came from the shared archive

[RAN]

Ahead-of-Time class loading and linking extends that idea to your classes, and to the linkage work, using a training run. It is JEP 483 in JDK 24, with ergonomics from JEP 514 and method profiling from JEP 515 in JDK 25.

$ java -XX:AOTCacheOutput=app.aot -cp . Boot     # training run, writes the cache
$ java -XX:AOTCache=app.aot -cp . Boot           # subsequent runs read it

[RAN — 8 runs each, wall clock]

Configuration Mean startup Classes from the archive
Default (CDS only) 0.1050 s 956 of 1003
With AOT cache (12.6 MB) 0.0862 s 1039 of 1048

That is 18% faster startup on this toy program. Be careful how you read that number.

It is small in absolute terms, just 19 ms, for two reasons. The baseline was already getting CDS. And a 1000-class program has very little to accelerate. Published figures for large frameworks are much larger, because a Spring Boot application loads tens of thousands of classes and does far more linking. The honest claim from this lab's own run is "18% on a tiny program"; anything bigger is a claim not tested here.

Here is why this matters for the story. The release train is what let AOT ship as three separate JEPs across two releases, instead of waiting until it was complete. Those were JEP 483 in JDK 24, then JEP 514 and JEP 515 in JDK 25. Under the old model it would still be unreleased.

Which four things get confused most often?

Four pairs, and in each one the difference is a mechanism rather than a shade of meaning. Each bullet names the mechanism.

  • Preview vs incubator vs experimental. Preview is a language or VM feature behind --enable-preview, stamped into the class file. Incubator is an API in a jdk.incubator.* module behind --add-modules, and its package name changes on graduation. Experimental is a VM feature behind -XX:+UnlockExperimentalVMOptions.
  • LTS vs GA. GA is the day a release becomes available, and every release has one. LTS is a vendor's promise to keep patching it afterwards. Only some releases get one, and the promise differs per vendor.
  • --release vs -source / -target. --release N also restricts you to release N's API. So it catches at compile time the NoSuchMethodError that -source and -target let you ship.
  • Module path vs classpath. Code on the module path is in a named module with enforced boundaries. Code on the classpath lands in the unnamed module, which reads everything and tolerates split packages. Most applications are still, correctly, on the classpath.

Solutions — Java 9 → 25

Attempt EXERCISES.md first. Rungs 7–8 have no single right answer; what follows is a worked position with its reasoning exposed, so you can disagree with the reasoning rather than the conclusion.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Java used to release "when the big feature was done." Java 9's big feature took three and a half years, and ninety other finished features sat waiting for it. When it finally shipped, everyone had to absorb ninety-one changes at once, which made upgrading a project rather than a chore — and projects get scheduled against other projects, and lose. So teams skipped it, which made the next jump bigger, which made skipping more likely. Java broke that loop by shipping on a fixed date twice a year with whatever is ready, so each step is small enough to absorb without planning it.

1.2 Java 9 was not big because the team was unusually productive — it was big because it was three and a half years of accumulated work released in one go. Java 10 covered six months and shipped six months' worth. The rate of work was roughly the same; what changed was the batch size. And smaller batches are the point, because upgrade cost grows faster than linearly with batch size: when four things change and your tests go red you can find the cause, and when four hundred do, you cannot.

1.3

  • Preview is a language or VM feature, gated by --enable-preview at both compile and run time, and enforced by a stamp written into the class file.
  • Incubator is an API, shipped in a jdk.incubator.* module you must add with --add-modules, and enforced by the module system plus the package name — which changes when it graduates.
  • Experimental is a VM feature (a collector, a JIT), gated by -XX:+UnlockExperimentalVMOptions, and enforced by nothing but that flag.

1.4 LTS means some vendors have committed to keep publishing security fixes for that release for years after it is superseded; non-LTS releases get about six months. To the follow-up: nothing is technically different about the build. OpenJDK ships every release the same way. LTS is a support commitment made about a release, not a property of it — which is also why different vendors publish different LTS dates, and why some support releases Oracle does not.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 (at JDK 21) Final: switch expressions, text blocks, records, pattern matching for instanceof, sealed classes, pattern matching for switch, record patterns, virtual threads, sequenced collections, the HTTP client. Still impermanent: structured concurrency (preview 1), scoped values (preview 1), string templates (preview 1), compact source files (preview 1), the FFM API (preview 3), the Vector API (incubator 6).

2.2 String templates. Preview in 21, second preview in 22, then withdrawn in 23 — removed from the JDK entirely, no replacement, no successor JEP. It is the only row that reverses, and it is the event that makes preview mean something.

2.3 The Vector API. Incubator 1 in JDK 16 through incubator 10 in JDK 25 (and 11 in JDK 26) — the state never changes. It is waiting on Project Valhalla's value types, because the API is designed around value semantics the language does not have yet.

2.4 LTS releases are 8, 11, 17, 21, 25, with 29 next. Gaps: 8→11 is 4.5 years (the old model), 11→17 is 3 years, 17→21 is 2 years, 21→25 is 2, 25→29 is 2. The change is at 17→21: the cadence moved from three years to two, because a three-year jump was still big enough to make teams wait for the next one — the ratchet restarting one level up.

2.5 The minor version — bytes 5 and 6, immediately after the cafebabe magic number. Normally 0000; a preview class file has ffff, i.e. 65535.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 No, it does not load. A preview class file's major version must match the running JVM exactly. Built on 25 (major 69), it runs on 25 and nowhere else. The --enable-preview flag on JDK 26 does not help — it satisfies a different rule.

3.2 Yes, it loads. This is Java's core forward-compatibility promise: ordinary bytecode compiled for an older release runs on every newer JVM. Java 8 bytecode (major 52) still runs on JDK 25.

The answers differ because preview class files are deliberately exempt from the compatibility promise. That exemption is not an oversight — it is the mechanism that makes it impossible for an impermanent feature to quietly become load-bearing infrastructure in somebody else's dependency tree.

3.3 Team B (8→17), by a wide margin. 8→17 crosses split packages, the version-string change, the Java EE module removal, JavaFX and Web Start, Nashorn, biased locking, and both halves of strong encapsulation. 17→25 crosses the charset default, dynamic agent loading, sequenced collections' default methods, the string-template withdrawal, Unsafe warnings, the Security Manager, non-generational ZGC and the JNI warning — real work, but almost all of it either mechanical or a flag change.

3.4 Scoped values finalised first — JDK 25 (JEP 506) — despite entering the pipeline a release later than structured concurrency, which is still previewing in JDK 26.

The explanation is the useful part: both are Project Loom, same team, adjacent scope. Scoped values have a small API surface whose semantics were settled early — bind a value, read it beneath, it is gone when the block exits. Structured concurrency had to settle how a scope decides it is done, and that joiner design took several attempts; the JDK 21 shape (StructuredTaskScope.ShutdownOnFailure) is not the JDK 25 shape (StructuredTaskScope.open(joiner)). Time in the pipeline measures design risk, not team velocity.

3.5 Yes, something breaks — and this is the trap worth internalising. The flag stamps the class file whether or not any preview feature was used. So a build with a leftover --enable-preview produces artifacts with minor version 65535, which require the flag at runtime and refuse to load on any other JDK version — for no benefit whatsoever. Check the tab: "Only final features" plus the flag still yields ffff.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Dev enables the flag on JDK 12 to use switch expressions, writes break value;, builds orders.jar, publishes it to the internal repository. Three other teams add it as a dependency. Six months later the platform team upgrades production to JDK 13, where that construct's semantics changed. The jar is unchanged, and Java's compatibility promise says an unchanged artifact keeps working — so nobody suspects it. It either misbehaves subtly or throws something that points at nothing.

The compile-time flag protected the compiler. It did nothing for the artifact, and the artifact is the thing that travels — into repositories, into containers, into other teams' dependency trees, long after everyone has forgotten the flag was passed. Hence the class-file stamp: put the mark on the thing that moves.

4.2 A hypothetical 17→23 jump: 18(9) + 19(7) + 20(7) + 21(15) + 22(12) + 23(12) = 62 JEPs. The real jumps: 17→21 = 9+7+7+15 = 38, and 21→25 = 12+12+24+18 = 66. So the two-year jumps are not uniformly smaller in JEP count — 21→25 is larger than the hypothetical, because JDK 24 was unusually big.

That is the honest answer and it is more interesting than the expected one. JEP count is a poor proxy for migration cost. What actually matters is how many breaking changes are in the window, and there the two-year cadence wins clearly: 17→21 contains one significant hazard, while 11→17 contained the entire strong-encapsulation transition. The argument for two years is about where the breakage falls, not about counting features.

4.3 Better: a feature reaches users sooner, and a design mistake is caught sooner.

Worse, first: the feedback window per preview shrinks. Real feedback on a language feature comes from people using it in anger, which takes months. Three-month previews would mostly produce previews with no feedback in them, which is the appearance of iteration without the substance.

Worse, second: the support surface grows. Every release is a branch a vendor may have to patch, and every release is another class-file version every bytecode library must handle. Doubling the rate doubles that cost for the entire ecosystem, not just for the JDK team.

4.4 For throwing: silent behaviour changes are the ones that reach customers; an exception would have converted an invisible correctness bug into a loud, findable one.

Against: there is no well-defined "ambiguous decode." Most byte sequences are valid in both windows-1252 and UTF-8 and simply mean different things — there is nothing for the JVM to detect. Enforcing this would mean rejecting input that is legal by every applicable specification, on the basis of a guess.

Committed answer: against. The change was correct and could not have been made loud without breaking correct programs. The right mitigation is not in the JDK — it is -Dfile.encoding=COMPAT as a bridge, and, permanently, never using default-charset APIs. That advice predates JEP 400 by fifteen years, and the people bitten by JEP 400 are exactly the people who had ignored it.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 The sequence: the library is built on JDK 21 with --enable-preview and published internally. Three teams consume it. The platform team upgrades one service to JDK 22 — a routine, low-risk bump. That service now fails at class load with UnsupportedClassVersionError, and the failure is in a dependency, so the team debugging it has no reason to suspect a flag in someone else's build. Meanwhile no team can upgrade independently: the library has silently coupled all of them to one exact JDK version.

And the harder failure: if the previewed feature is withdrawn (as string templates were), the library cannot be rebuilt at all without a rewrite.

The violated guarantee is binary forward compatibility — deliberately waived for preview class files. "We control all our JDKs" is not the relevant claim; the relevant claim would be "no service will ever upgrade independently," which nobody can promise.

6.2 The UTF-8 charset default (JEP 400, JDK 18). It compiles — no API changed. It boots — nothing throws. The health endpoint returns 200 — it does not read any affected file. And text read with a default-charset API now decodes differently, so customer names with non-ASCII characters come out corrupted, and it surfaces two weeks later in a report.

The mechanism is that the change is semantic, not structural: no signature changed, no exception is thrown, only the bytes-to-characters mapping moved. The only thing that catches it is a test asserting on output content. Biased locking's removal (JDK 15) has the same shape.

6.3 The reasoning treats time-in-pipeline as a proxy for team performance. It is not: the Vector API's own JEP states it will remain in incubation until Project Valhalla delivers value types, because the API is built on value semantics the language does not yet have. Finalising now would freeze a design against a language about to change underneath it — the exact mistake that produced java.util.Date.

What would actually support the conclusion: evidence about Valhalla's progress, since that is the blocker. What would refute it: Valhalla delivering. The evidence to gather is one project over, not in the incubation count.

For contrast: string templates left the pipeline after two previews, by being withdrawn. That is what a design failure looks like, and it looks nothing like ten patient incubations.

6.4 First: "supported until 2030" is Oracle's extended support for Java 8, which is a paid tier — the free public updates ended in 2019, and whether you have it depends entirely on your vendor and contract. Many teams saying this are not actually covered by the date they are citing.

Second, and larger: support dates say nothing about the cost of the eventual jump, which grows every year you wait. That is the ratchet from the README. A team that waits until 2030 will be doing an 8→33 migration under time pressure with nobody left who remembers why the code does what it does. The question is not "are we supported" but "what will this cost when we can no longer avoid it, and is it cheaper now."

6.5 What it bought: the service starts. What it cost:

It opens java.lang reflectively for the entire application, permanently and invisibly — the setting is in an environment variable, not in code, so it does not appear in any review, and nobody will find it when they wonder why their JDK 25 upgrade behaves oddly. It removes the pressure that would have caused the actual fix (upgrading the library that needs it), so the debt compounds silently. And because it is global, a second library that starts needing deep reflection will now work by accident, and nobody will ever know.

The better outcome: find the specific offender with jdeps --jdk-internals, upgrade it if possible, and if not, add the narrowest possible --add-opens in the launch configuration where a reviewer will see it, with a ticket attached.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

Worked positions, not the only answers.

7.1 The 40-service plan.

Route: 11 → 17 → 25, in two waves. Do not attempt 11→25 directly: essentially all the risk is concentrated in 11→17 (strong encapsulation), and isolating it means that when something breaks you know which transition caused it.

CI first, before any migration work: add a non-blocking job to all 40 repositories that builds and runs the full test suite on JDK 17. Non-blocking is not a detail — a job that can break the team's day gets deleted. Let it run for two to three weeks and collect the failures. This converts the migration from an unknown into a list.

Order within a service: build tool → build plugins → test and bytecode libraries (Mockito/ByteBuddy, JaCoCo, Lombok) → application dependencies → application code. Most "the new JDK is broken" reports are a five-year-old plugin.

Before writing the plan, run jdeps --jdk-internals on every service. It names each JDK-internal API used and its supported replacement, and it sees through dependencies. It will usually change the plan.

Readiness criterion — and this is where most plans are too weak: not "it compiles and boots." A service is ready when its full behavioural test suite passes on the target JDK, including tests that assert on output content. That is what catches JEP 400 and the biased-locking class of change.

Sequencing across services: pick two or three low-traffic services with good test coverage first, absorb the surprises, write them down, then fan out. Group the remainder by shared framework version — services that share a Spring version will hit the same problems, and the second one costs a tenth of the first.

The abandoned library: four options in order — (1) check for a community fork, which usually exists; (2) check whether the JDK now provides what you used it for (VarHandle, FFM, the Class-File API, java.util.Base64 and the HTTP client have all absorbed abandoned libraries); (3) vendor the source into your codebase and fix it yourself — realistic, and the honest cost is maintaining it forever; (4) contain it: isolate the dependency behind an interface in a service that stays on 17 while everything else moves, and schedule its replacement. Do not add a global --add-opens and call it done.

Second quarter: repeat for 17→25, which is much lighter.

7.2 Preview policy.

  • Who: anyone, in a spike or a prototype. That freedom is the point of preview features existing.
  • What kind of artifact: applications you own end-to-end, never a library, and never anything published to a shared repository. The rule is mechanical: if another team consumes the artifact, no preview features.
  • What must be true of the build: the JDK version is pinned in the build file (toolchain, not "whatever is installed"), CI fails if the JDK drifts, and the --enable-preview flag is declared in exactly one place with a comment naming the JEP and the feature.
  • Enforce it, don't trust it: add a CI check that greps published artifacts for class files with minor version 65535. This is cheap and it is the only control that actually works, because the flag will otherwise be copied into a build file by someone who does not know what it does (see 3.5).
  • On withdrawal: the owning team rewrites within one release cycle. The policy should say this in advance, so that when it happens it is a scheduled task rather than an argument about whether it was a good idea.

7.3 Diagnosing the wrong-characters report.

Order matters — cheapest and most likely first.

  1. Suspect the charset default before anything else. You moved across JDK 18, and this symptom is JEP 400's signature: wrong characters, no exception.
  2. Confirm cheaply: run the old and new JDK against the same input file and diff the decoded output. If they differ, you are done diagnosing.
  3. Locate the read. Grep for default-charset APIs: new FileReader(, new String( with one argument, new PrintWriter(File, getBytes() with no argument, new InputStreamReader( with one argument.
  4. Bridge, then fix. -Dfile.encoding=COMPAT restores JDK 17 behaviour and stops the bleeding. The fix is an explicit StandardCharsets.UTF_8 at every call site.
  5. Check the data. If files were written by the old service, they are in the old encoding and need converting — the code fix alone leaves you reading old files wrongly.

The test to add: a round-trip test with genuinely non-ASCII content — not "test", but something with accents, a non-breaking space, and an emoji — that asserts on the exact decoded string. Put it in the shared test module so every service gets it. The general principle: your upgrade safety net is tests that assert on output content, because those are the only ones that catch a silent semantic change.

7.4 Responding to JEP 500 (final means final).

Find out if you are affected, first — most of this is in dependencies, not your code. Grep your own source for setAccessible followed by a write to a final field, but expect little. The real exposure is frameworks: some serialisation libraries reconstruct objects by writing final fields directly, and some DI and test frameworks do the same for injection and mocking.

Then: run on a JDK 26 early-access build in the non-blocking CI job you already have from 7.1 — this is exactly what that job is for, and it is why it should exist permanently rather than being deleted after each migration. JEP 500 is a preparation JEP, so it warns before it forbids; the warning names the caller, which gives you the inventory for free.

Then: upgrade the named frameworks, most of which will have moved to VarHandle, MethodHandles.privateLookupIn or the Class-File API. For your own code, records and constructor injection remove the need entirely.

The pattern to notice: JEP 451 (agents), JEP 472 (JNI) and JEP 500 (final) are the same move — a release whose entire content is a warning, filed to start a ladder. When you see one, that is your notice, and acting on it during the warning phase costs a fraction of acting on it during the removal phase.

Rung 8 — Design

Worked positions.

8.1 Version policy for 200 services.

Permitted versions: exactly two at any time — the current LTS and the previous LTS. Two, not one, because a hard cutover across 30 teams is not achievable; three or more and you are maintaining a museum.

Who decides: a platform team owns the list and the dates. Individual teams do not get to choose their JDK, for the same reason they do not choose their TLS version — the cost of divergence is borne by everyone.

Introducing a version: the moment a new LTS reaches GA, it goes into every repository's CI as a non-blocking job. Not when migration starts — at GA. This is the single highest-leverage item in the whole policy, because it turns migration into a stream of small known fixes instead of a project.

Retiring one: announce the date at the same time you introduce the new one — two years of notice, matching the LTS cadence. Then actually enforce it, or the policy is decoration.

Automated: the CI job; a build-time check that the toolchain version matches the declared one; a scan for preview-stamped class files in published artifacts (see 7.2); a dependency bot. Automate the detection, not the migration — attempts to automate the fix produce changes nobody understands.

Monitored: one dashboard, one number per service — which JDK it runs in production. Distribution, not average. The number that matters is how many services are on the oldest permitted version, because that is your exposure when it is retired.

Stopping the ratchet: the ratchet is "upgrading is a project, projects get scheduled, this one loses." You break it by making the upgrade not a project — continuous CI, a fixed cadence nobody re-litigates, and a default that moving is what happens unless someone objects.

The two most likely failures. First: the non-blocking CI job gets ignored and then deleted. It is red for months, everyone stops looking, someone cleans it up. Mitigation: give one named person responsibility for its aggregate status, and review it monthly — the review is what keeps it alive, not the job. Second: two or three services get a permanent exemption, for good reasons at the time, and the exemption never expires. Mitigation: every exemption gets an expiry date and a named owner at the moment it is granted, and an exemption without a date is refused.

8.2 Library version policy.

Minimum JDK: the previous LTS. On this timeline that means 21 — supported until at least 2028, and it gives you records, sealed types, pattern matching and virtual threads. Going lower costs you real expressiveness for users who, in practice, are not going to adopt a new version of your library anyway. Requiring the current LTS excludes too many.

When to raise it: on a major version of your library, on the LTS cadence, and announced one minor version in advance. Never in a patch release — a minimum JDK bump is a breaking change even though it does not look like one, and shipping it in a patch is how you end up in someone's incident review.

Multi-release jars: only for a genuine optimisation, never for API surface. The legitimate use is "there is a much faster path available on JDK N+" — for example, using the Vector API or FFM when present and falling back otherwise. The illegitimate use is offering different APIs per version, which doubles your test matrix and confuses every user. Cost: your build and test complexity roughly doubles for the versions you cover, so cover as few as possible.

Handling a user base spread from 8 to 26: be explicit, in the README, in a table, with dates. If you must serve Java 8, do it with a separate, frozen, maintenance-only branch, not with compatibility contortions in the main line. And compile with --release, never -source/-target, so the compiler catches an accidental use of a newer API instead of your user discovering it as a NoSuchMethodError.

Never use preview features. Non-negotiable for a library — see 6.1.

8.3 Is the preview mechanism worth it?

Yes, and the strongest case against is worth stating properly first.

The case against: string templates consumed years of design, two full implementations, two release cycles of documentation and tooling support, and was then deleted. That is real engineering that produced nothing shippable. Multiply by every feature that has previewed more than twice, and the mechanism looks expensive.

Why it is still worth it. Compare against the actual alternative, which is not "get it right first time" — it is shipping it permanently. Java is still carrying Object.finalize(), Java serialization and java.util.Date, each of which has cost the ecosystem more than string templates cost, every year, for thirty years. finalize() alone has produced two decades of resource leaks and GC pathologies, and it can never be removed.

Two concrete features that make the case:

Switch expressions. Shipped in JDK 12 preview with break value;, which overloaded break ambiguously. Feedback arrived, and the JDK 13 preview replaced it with yield. Cost: one release, behind a flag, affecting only opt-in users. Under the old model that syntax would be permanent, and every Java programmer would be explaining it to newcomers forever.

String templates. The apparently-damning example is actually the proof. The mechanism's value is precisely that withdrawal is possible, and a mechanism whose escape hatch is never used is not known to work. The cost of those years is the insurance premium; the payout is that Java did not permanently acquire a syntax its own designers concluded was too complex.

The honest qualification: the mechanism is not free for users either, and the lab's F5 is the evidence — anyone who used STR."..." had working code become uncompilable. The design's answer is that this is disclosed and enforced up front rather than discovered later, which is the most you can ask of an impermanence mechanism.

8.4 Designing this for your own platform.

Take a shared internal library as the example.

What "preview" means there: an API you may call, that will not be removed without notice only if you are in the list of registered callers, and that carries no compatibility promise across minor versions. Concretely: a @Preview annotation, a separate package (com.acme.lib.preview.*), and its own section of the changelog.

What plays the role of the class-file stamp — the enforcement question, which is the whole design problem. The stamp works because it is mechanical, attached to the artifact, and checked automatically by something the developer does not control. Candidates in a normal organisation:

  • A build-time check that fails the build if a non-exempt module imports the preview package. This is the closest analogue and it is genuinely effective, because it runs on every build without anyone remembering.
  • A linter or ArchUnit rule in the shared build configuration.
  • For a service API: a required header or API version parameter on preview endpoints, so a caller cannot use one by accident, and the gateway can enumerate who is calling.
  • For a database schema: a naming convention plus a migration check — _preview suffixed tables that the schema linter refuses to reference from stable code.

If nothing can play that role — and often nothing can, because you cannot modify the consumer's build — then do not call it preview. An impermanent thing with no enforcement is just an undocumented promise, and it will become load-bearing within one quarter; the failure mode is precisely the one in 6.1. The honest alternatives are: (a) don't ship it until it is final, or (b) ship it final and accept that you now maintain it forever, and price that in.

The general lesson, which is the point of the exercise: impermanence needs a mechanism, not a label. Java put the mark on the artifact rather than on the developer's intention, because the artifact is the thing that outlives the intention. Any impermanence scheme you design should be judged by the same test: what physically stops this from becoming something people depend on?
