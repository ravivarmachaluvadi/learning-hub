Where you are: BEGINNER MODEL — stages 1–9.

1. WHY does this exist?

Every distributed system stamps things — writes, messages, log lines, MVCC versions — and the stamp has two jobs that pull in opposite directions. Job one: order — if this event caused that one, its stamp must be smaller, always, or replicas apply effects before causes and debugging becomes archaeology of a fictional history. Job two: meaning — a stamp should answer human questions: what did the system look like at 5 p.m.? delete everything older than 30 days. Wall clocks do the second and betray the first; the counters you met in the causal-consistency lab do the first and know nothing of the second. HLC exists because real databases need one number that does both.

2. THE PROBLEM, as a story

A news agency has two bureaus. Asha in Mumbai stamps everything with the newsroom wall clock — which runs 5 minutes fast. Ben in London stamps with his — 5 minutes slow. (NTP in real life keeps machines within milliseconds-to-seconds of each other; nobody's clock is right.)

Zoe, the editor, has two needs. First, the archive must never show an effect before its cause — a correction filed before the article it corrects makes the archive fiction. Second, she assembles editions: "give me everything as of 5:00 p.m., exactly, from both bureaus" — and that cut through history must be whole: if it contains Ben's receipt of Asha's story, it must contain Asha's sending of it.

Order, and meaning. Watch both fail.

3. THE NAIVE SOLUTION

Stamp with the wall clock — it's right there, it survives restarts, NTP keeps it roughly honest, and everyone understands "17:42:03.114".

4. THE FAILURE — twice, because we own two clocks

Failure one: the wall clock files effects before causes. Scene 1 (java src/Scenes.java 1), the whole thing:

tick 10: Asha publishes an article and wires it to Ben. Her clock stamps it 15.
tick 12: Ben receives it. HIS clock stamps the receipt 7.

Sent at 15, received at 7 — the receipt precedes its own cause by 8 ticks. Fuzzed against a vector-clock ground truth (experiment 1): at skew 8, 65 inversions among sampled happens-before pairs; at skew 20, 341. And Zoe's editions? 152 of 200 candidate cut times produce a torn edition — a receive inside, its send outside (experiment 5).

🔮 Predict: Set the skew to zero — clocks perfectly synchronized. Is the wall clock safe now?

Reveal No — it stops inverting and starts **tying**: 79 happens-before pairs per run that it cannot order at all, because two events inside the same clock tick get the identical reading. A clock has finite resolution, and a busy server does many things per millisecond. Even a perfect wall clock is too coarse to be an order. (Experiment 1, skew-0 row: 0 + 79.)

Failure two: the fix you already own burns the map. The causal-consistency lab gave you the Lamport clock — counter = max(own, heard) + 1 — and it is flawless at job one: 0 inversions, 0 ties, every run. But run scene 2: after one busy afternoon, Asha's counter reads 41 while her wall says 18; fuzzed, the counter strays up to +460 ahead / −13 behind wall time, and events sharing one counter value span 34 ticks of real time. Ask "the newsroom at counter 20" and that moment is mid-afternoon in Mumbai and next week in London. Ask "at 5 p.m." and there is no answer at all — the counter never heard of 5 p.m. Consistent cuts, yes — at values no human can name.

So: two clocks, each perfect at the other one's failure. The deeper problem is now visible — we don't need a better clock, we need one stamp carrying both kinds of time without letting them corrupt each other.

5. THE REAL REQUIREMENT

One stamp, small enough to put on every MVCC version and message header, such that:

  1. Order: if e happened-before f, then stamp(e) < stamp(f) — no inversions, no ties, ever, regardless of skew.
  2. Meaning: the stamp stays within a known, bounded distance of wall time, so "as of 5 p.m." and "older than 30 days" are real questions.
  3. Size: O(1) — 64 bits, not a vector that grows with the cluster (the causal-consistency lab already showed you a vector clock's width is your replica count).

6. THE KEY IDEA

Never let logical time masquerade as physical time. Keep the wall part l as exactly "the freshest actual wall-clock reading anywhere on my causal path" — a number some real clock really displayed — and keep a separate counter c for ordering the events that l cannot tell apart.

l can then never stray further from your own clock than the clocks themselves disagree — the bound. And c gives the strict order within a tick — the correctness. Two numbers, two jobs, one stamp: (l, c), compared lexicographically.

7. TERMINOLOGY

Hybrid logical clock (HLC). A clock producing stamps (l, c) where l tracks the maximum physical-clock reading seen on the causal path and c is a logical counter reset whenever l advances. From Kulkarni, Demirbas et al., 2014. "Hybrid" because it is literally a physical clock and a Lamport clock fused, each doing the job the other can't.

l — the wall part. Invariant: l is always a reading some node's physical clock actually produced, and l ≥ your own current reading. Example from scene 3: Ben's wall reads 8, but he received a message stamped 16 — his l becomes 16, a reading Asha's clock really took.

c — the logical part. Counts events that share one l. Resets to 0 the moment l moves. Example: three edits inside one tick at wall 15 → stamps 15, 15.1, 15.2; next tick → 16 (c back to 0).

Drift. l − pt: how far a stamp's wall part sits ahead of the local clock's actual reading. The number that separates every design in this lab: unbounded for Lamport (+460) — and, as stage 10 shows, for the obvious first fix too — but bounded by the clock spread for HLC (measured: 15 at spread 16).

Clock spread / skew. How far apart the physical clocks are (fastest minus slowest). The honest input to every promise HLC makes — and the thing NTP controls in production.

Causality token. An HLC stamp carried by a client between requests and absorbed by each server through the ordinary receive rule — the implementation of the causal-consistency lab's session token (afterClusterTime). No new machinery: a token is a message with no payload.

Consistent cut at a wall time (revisited from distributed-transactions): the set of events with stamp ≤ (T, ∞). HLC's gift is that T is an actual time, not a counter value.

Minor terms at first use later: one-way property (recalled), uncertainty restart, max-offset.

8. TWO MENTAL MODELS

The simple model: dated pages, numbered lines. The newsroom writes in a shared style of notebook. Every entry lands on a page dated with a real calendar date — and the rule is: use the latest date you have seen on any calendar, yours or one mentioned in an incoming letter, never a made-up one. Many entries on the same page get line numbers: date 15, line 0; date 15, line 1... Turn to a new page (a later date appears — your calendar caught up, or a letter mentioned one) and line numbering starts over at 0. Order of any two entries: compare dates, then lines. "The archive as of the 15th" = every page up to 15, whole by construction.

The engineering model. Per node: (l, c). Local event or send at wall reading pt: l' = max(l, pt); c' = (l'==l) ? c+1 : 0. Receive of message stamped (ml, mc): l' = max(l, ml, pt), and c' = (tie with both → max(c, mc)+1) / (tie with own → c+1) / (tie with message → mc+1) / (own wall won outright → 0). Compare (l, c) lexicographically. Invariants: happens-before ⟹ strictly smaller stamp; l ≥ pt always; l − pt ≤ clock spread.

Simple model Engineering model

Where the analogy breaks. (1) The page date can come from someone else's calendar — after Ben absorbs Asha's stamp, his pages are dated by her fast clock even though his says three days earlier; "the 15th" on a page is at most the clock spread away from anyone's calendar, but it is not your 15th. (2) Notebook dates never decrease and neither does l — but real wall clocks DO jump backwards (NTP corrections); the max means HLC glides through a backward step while a raw wall-clock stamp would happily file new events in the past. (3) Comparing two entries tells you their order but never whether they were causally related or merely concurrent — line numbers and dates are as one-way as Lamport's counter; detecting concurrency still costs a vector clock.

10. THE ACTUAL MECHANISM — one funeral, then the rules

v1 — the obvious hybrid: one merged value

Any smart engineer's first sketch (it is also the strawman in the HLC paper itself): keep a single value that never falls behind either clock or causality:

local/send:  l = max(l + 1, pt)
receive:     l = max(l + 1, m.l + 1, pt)

Ordered? Perfectly — the +1s are a Lamport clock. Near wall time? It starts there...

🔮 Predict: Does the merged value stay near wall time under load?

Reveal No — and the failure is structural, not a tuning matter. Every event adds `+1` to a number that is *supposed to be a wall reading*. Any burst of events inside one clock tick pushes `l` ahead of `pt`, and `max` means it **never comes back down** — the excess compounds forever. Experiment 3, same workload, growing run length:

| run length | NAIVE maxDrift | HLC maxDrift | |---|---|---| | 150 | 118 | 15 | | 300 | 244 | 15 | | 600 | 470 | 15 | | 1200 | 898 | 15 |

By tick 1200 the naive clock is 898 ticks into the future and climbing — as unmoored as the Lamport counter it secretly is, but disguised as a time, which is worse: "delete older than 30 days" now deletes live data. And you cannot even measure how bad it is from inside, because physical and logical ticks are fused into one indistinguishable number.

The funeral teaches the design: the corruption came from +1 landing on the wall-valued number. So forbid it. l may only ever be assigned a value some physical clock actually read — and everything +1-shaped moves into a second number.

v2 — the real HLC

Local event or send (node's wall reads pt):

l' = max(l, pt);                      // l: only ever a real wall reading
c' = (l' == l) ? c + 1 : 0;          // wall stuck -> borrow a counter tick
                                      // wall advanced -> counter owes nothing

Receive of a message stamped (ml, mc):

l' = max(l, ml, pt);
if (l' == l && l' == ml)  c' = max(c, mc) + 1;   // tie with both sides
else if (l' == l)         c' = c + 1;            // own l leads
else if (l' == ml)        c' = mc + 1;           // message's l leads
else                      c' = 0;                // own WALL won outright

Walk it with scene 3's real values (java src/Scenes.java 3) — Asha's clock +5, Ben's −5:

  1. tick 10, Asha edits: wall reads 15, l adopts it → 15 (c=0).
  2. tick 10, two more edits: wall still 15, l stuck, c borrows → 15.1, 15.2.
  3. tick 11: wall reads 16 > l → 16, c back to 0. Then a send → 16.1.
  4. tick 13, Ben receives it: his wall reads only 8, but the message says 16.1 → l' = max(0, 16, 8) = 16, tie with the message → c = mc+1 → 16.2. Cause 16.1 < effect 16.2. ✓
  5. tick 13, Ben works on: wall still 8, l still 16 → 16.3.

🔮 Predict (the case that catches everyone): Ben's clock is at (50, 2) and a message arrives stamped (50, 7) — the l's tie. What must the new c be? Is c+1 = 3 enough?

Reveal No — `(50, 3)` would stamp the receive *below* its own send `(50, 7)`: an inversion. When l ties, the new c must beat **both** counters: `max(2, 7) + 1 = 8`. This is planted as the bug `recvIgnoresMsgC` — run `java src/Tests.java broken` and watch it produce 8 genuine happens-before inversions. `src/YourClock.java` makes you implement all four cases yourself; this is case 4.

Why the bound holds — the argument is one sentence: l is the maximum over a set of actual wall readings (yours, and everyone's on your causal path), so it can exceed your own reading by at most (fastest clock − slowest clock) = the clock spread. Experiment 4 measures it as an equality, not an inequality:

skew clock spread measured drift maxC
2 4 3 17
8 16 15 31
20 40 39 49
50 100 99 80

And the division of labor is visible in the last column: crank the bursts to 30 events/tick and drift stays 15 while maxC climbs to 244 — load spills into c, never into l. That split is the entire invention.

Full scorecard at seed 42 (2,629 events, 28,720 happens-before pairs checked against the vector-clock truth): WALL 65 inversions + 92 ties, 152/200 torn cuts. LAMPORT 0 + 0 but drift +460/−13. NAIVE 0 + 0 but drift 470 and growing. HLC: 0 inversions, 0 ties, 0 torn cuts, drift ≤ 15, in 64 bits.

What HLC does not do — two honest boundaries. First, it is as one-way as the Lamport clock it contains (causal-consistency lab, remembered): stamp(e) < stamp(f) never proves e caused f; detecting concurrency still requires a vector clock. Second, order between events with no causal path and no token rests on nothing but the clocks being roughly right — a client writing via a fast node and a different client reading via a slow node has no arrow between them, and HLC promises that stranger nothing (that is external consistency, and it is exactly what TrueTime's commit wait bought in the distributed-transactions lab). Stage 11 and the token experiment put numbers on this.

Checkpoint — you hold the intermediate model. You can execute both update rules by hand, say why the naive merge fails structurally, and derive the drift bound in one sentence. Next: everything that bends or breaks it.

11. FAILURE SCENARIOS

A clock beyond its promised bound. Create: one node offset +60 in a cluster operated as ±8; watch: java src/Experiments.java 7. Measured: the believed drift bound was 16; actual 67 — because l is the max reading on the causal path, one liar infects every node downstream of its messages, and their stamps run an hour into the future together. What survives: ordering, completely (max doesn't care if the number is wrong). What dies: the bound — and with it every promise phrased in real time: staleness limits, "as of 5 p.m.", GC-by-age, and (in CockroachDB) the correctness of the uncertainty window itself. Production: this is why the bound is enforced, not assumed — CockroachDB crashes a node whose offset exceeds max-offset (distributed-transactions lab: crashing is the correct response to a lying clock), and why MongoDB signs its clusterTime — an unsigned token would let any client push every server's clock to the year 3000 (this experiment, weaponized).

The stranger's stale read. Create: write via the fast-clock node, read via the slow one, carry nothing; watch: experiment 6 / scene 5. 86 of 86 reads predate the write — the reader's "now" is simply smaller than the stamp the writer issued. Nothing is broken: there is no happens-before edge, so HLC promised nothing. Fixes, in production form: carry a causality token (the write's stamp; reader absorbs it via the ordinary receive rule) → 0/86 — this is MongoDB's clusterTime/afterClusterTime and the implementation of the causal-consistency lab's session token; or CockroachDB's route — treat every value stamped within [readTs, readTs + maxOffset] as ambiguous and restart the read above it (an uncertainty restart): pay ε in occasional read retries instead of TrueTime's 2ε on every write ack. Same ε, three different bills: writer waits (Spanner), reader retries (CockroachDB), client carries a token (MongoDB).

Counter overflow. Create: Clocks.pack(new Stamp(5, 70000)); watch: test pack_counter_overflow_fails_loudly, and the planted bug neverResetC — forget the reset-on-advance and maxC goes 31 → 718 in one small run; at production rates the 16-bit budget dies in milliseconds. Real overflow needs 65,536 causally chained events inside one wall tick — rare, but CockroachDB still handles it loudly rather than silently. The reset rule is what makes 16 bits a fortune.

The wall clock steps backwards. NTP corrections really do this. Create: lower a node's offset mid-run. HLC glides: l = max(l, pt) just holds l level while pt catches up, c absorbing the interval — no inversion possible, monotonicity by construction. A raw wall-clock stamper, by contrast, starts filing new events into the past. (Asserted from the rule; the visualization's skew sliders let you do it live.)

Messages reordered or lost. No effect on correctness: HLC stamps events, it does not deliver messages — there is no FIFO assumption anywhere in the rules (contrast the causal-consistency lab, where delivery needed the machinery). If you want causal delivery, HLC stamps are an input to it, not a replacement for it.

12. CORRECTNESS — the full scorecard

orders hb? detects concurrency? wall-meaningful? drift bound size waits?
wall clock ✗ (65–341 inv.) ✗ ✓ 0 8 B no
Lamport ✓ ✗ (one-way) ✗ (+460/−13) none 8 B no
vector clock ✓ ✓ ✗ none 8 B × n no
naive hybrid ✓ ✗ pretends (→898) none 8 B no
HLC ✓ ✗ (one-way) ✓ clock spread 8 B no
TrueTime ✓ (via ts) ✗ ✓ ε (hardware) 8 B 2ε per write

What is guaranteed (honest clocks assumed): happens-before ⟹ strictly smaller stamp; l ≥ pt; l − pt ≤ spread; every cut at (T, ∞) is consistent; the clock never blocks anything — stamping is a local computation, which is the entire liveness story and the deepest contrast with commit wait.

What is explicitly NOT guaranteed: concurrency detection (one-way, forever); external consistency for causally-unconnected clients (buy it with tokens, restarts, or commit wait); the drift bound under a clock that lies beyond its declared offset — the bound is conditional, and production systems spend real machinery (evictions, signatures) policing the condition.

Traded away, relative to each neighbor: vs vector clocks — concurrency detection, for O(1) size; vs TrueTime — external consistency for strangers, for zero waiting and zero special hardware.

15. PRODUCTION — where this clock actually lives

CockroachDB is the flagship. Every transaction timestamp and every MVCC version (isolation-levels lab: the version chains) is an HLC stamp — int64 wall nanoseconds + int32 logical, the packing of experiment 8 with wider fields. The distributed-transactions lab left you a question: how does CockroachDB skip TrueTime's commit wait? Now you own the answer: HLC orders everything causally connected for free, and for strangers it declares an uncertainty interval [ts, ts + max_offset] — a value in that window might have committed first by real time, so the read restarts above it. ε is paid in occasional read retries instead of on every write ack. The conditional bound is policed, not assumed: a node whose measured offset exceeds max_offset (default 500ms) self-terminates — experiment 7 is the incident it prevents. On restart, a node also waits out the offset before serving, so its l can't time-travel past its own crash.

MongoDB: $clusterTime is an HLC — 32-bit seconds + 32-bit counter — piggybacked on every message between every node and client, absorbed via the receive rule, which is how the whole deployment's clocks chase each other. afterClusterTime (the causal-consistency lab's session token, now with an implementation) is the token experiment shipped as a feature. And it is HMAC-signed, because experiment 7 shows exactly what an attacker could do with an unsigned one: one forged stamp poisons every server it touches, forever (l never comes back down).

YugabyteDB uses HLC the CockroachDB way plus leader leases; Kudu offers an HLC mode; the pattern generalizes: anywhere you meet "timestamp = physical + logical" in a storage engine, you are looking at this lab.

What the toy omits. Real resolution (nanoseconds, not ticks) and real NTP behavior (drift rates, not fixed offsets — the bound math is identical); persisting the clock across restarts (or waiting out max-offset instead, as Cockroach does); signing tokens; delivering the stamp inside RPC frameworks (piggybacking on everything, MongoDB-style); and the uncertainty-restart machinery itself — this lab's HLC orders; building a serializable database on top of it is the distributed-transactions lab's percolator plus these stamps.

What on-call sees. Clock-offset gauges with alerts long before max-offset (the bound is an SLO on NTP); node suicides tagged clock-offset (the correct crash); uncertainty-restart rates (the observable price of skew — rising restarts mean drifting clocks, not application bugs); and drift l − pt itself as a metric — a node whose HLC runs persistently ahead of its wall clock is downstream of a liar (experiment 7's infection, seen from the dashboard).

Commonly confused, one sentence each:

  • HLC vs vector clock: an HLC orders (one-way, O(1) size); a vector clock detects concurrency (O(n) size) — the causal-consistency lab's distinction, unchanged by the hybrid.
  • HLC's l vs a Lamport counter: l is only ever a reading a physical clock actually produced (hence the bound); a Lamport counter counts events (hence +460).
  • HLC vs TrueTime: the same ε, billed differently — TrueTime makes every writer wait 2ε for external consistency for everyone; HLC waits never, and covers strangers with reader restarts or client tokens.
  • Causality token vs commit wait: the token protects one client's session; commit wait protects all observers, including ones who never met the writer.

18. GLOSSARY

In order of appearance:

  • inversion — a happens-before pair the clock stamped in the wrong order (cause > effect).
  • tie — a happens-before pair the clock cannot order (equal stamps).
  • torn cut — an "as of T" snapshot containing a receive without its send.
  • hybrid logical clock (HLC) — the (l, c) clock: max wall reading on the causal path + a counter for what it can't separate (Kulkarni/ Demirbas 2014).
  • l (wall part) — always an actual physical-clock reading someone took; never incremented, only max'ed.
  • c (logical part) — orders events sharing one l; resets to 0 when l advances.
  • drift — l − pt: how far stamps run ahead of the local wall clock.
  • clock spread — fastest minus slowest physical clock; the bound on HLC drift, and a condition, not a fact.
  • naive hybrid — one merged value with +1s; ordered, and drifts without bound (118→898).
  • causality token — an HLC stamp carried by a client and absorbed via the receive rule; MongoDB's afterClusterTime.
  • one-way property — smaller stamp never proves causation; shared with Lamport clocks.
  • uncertainty restart — CockroachDB's answer to strangers: retry a read above a value stamped within [ts, ts+max_offset].
  • max-offset — the declared clock-spread bound a node must honor or die (Cockroach default 500ms).
  • clusterTime — MongoDB's signed HLC, piggybacked on every message.
  • 48+16 packing — one 64-bit long: 8,900 years of milliseconds + 65,536 counter values, overflowing loudly.

Solutions — hybrid logical clocks

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Way one (needs skew): machine B's clock is behind machine A's, so an event on B caused by an event on A gets a smaller timestamp — sort the logs and the effect precedes its cause (measured: 65 such inversions per run at skew 8, 341 at 20). The support engineer traces a request "backwards through time" and concludes the impossible. Way two (needs no skew at all): a clock has finite resolution, so two related events inside one tick get the same stamp — 79 unorderable pairs per run at skew zero. Fifty-millisecond NTP accuracy fixes neither: the first needs zero error, the second needs infinite resolution.

1.2 l may contain only a value some physical clock actually displayed — yours, or one carried in on a message. It may never contain an arithmetic invention (no +1). Since l is the max over a set of genuine readings taken "now-ish" on your causal path, it can exceed your own reading by at most the gap between the fastest and slowest clock — the spread. The moment you allow one +1 (the naive hybrid), l stops being a reading and the bound is gone (118→898 in experiment 3).

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 The message's l won: l' = max(own 0, msg 16, wall 8) = 16. The value 16 was physically produced by Asha's wall clock (running +5 at true tick 11) — Ben is now keeping time with a reading someone else's clock took, which is exactly what "freshest reading on the causal path" means.

2.2 tick 11: wall reads 16 > l. l advances, and c owes nothing: 16. Asha's own wall clock overtook her l (15) — when the wall part genuinely moves, the counter resets, which is the rule that keeps c tiny (maxC 31 over 2,629 events) and the 16-bit budget effectively infinite.

2.3 Fuzz.build pins node 0 to +skew and node 1 to -skew, so the spread is exactly 2*skew. The drift maximum is achieved when the slow node absorbs the fast node's freshest reading — but a message takes ≥1 tick to arrive, during which the slow node's own pt advanced by 1. Hence spread − 1 in every row: the measurement is tight to the mechanism, not noisy.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 Three-way tie at l = 50: c' = max(own 2, msg 7) + 1 = 8 → stamp (50, 8). Anything that only beats your own counter — (50, 3) — stamps the receive below its own send (50, 7): causality inverted.

3.2 NAIVE roughly doubles (470 → 898): its drift is accumulated +1s, so it scales with total events, i.e. with run length. HLC stays exactly 15: its drift is the clock spread, which time does not change. The syntactic difference: max(l + 1, pt) versus max(l, pt) — the +1 is on the wall-valued number in one design and banished to a separate field in the other.

3.3 The harness pins the writer to the +skew node and the reader to the -skew node, and the read follows the write within a tick — so the reader's "now" is always about 2*skew behind the stamp the writer just issued. Staleness isn't a race you sometimes lose; it is arithmetic. The real-world shape: a client whose writes go to one region (or primary) and whose reads go to a follower in another — precisely the MongoDB topology that made them ship clusterTime/afterClusterTime on by default.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Measured: 3 tests fail — the two rule tests (local_wall_advanced_resets_c, local_wall_stuck_increments_c) and hlc_maxC_small_relative_to_events (maxC 31 → 81 in this small run; the receive branches still reset, which is why it isn't worse). Crucially, order, drift, and cut tests all still pass — the bug is invisible to every correctness metric. The consumed resource is the 16-bit counter budget in the packed form: c now grows monotonically with events instead of resetting thousands of times a second, and at 1M causally-chained events/sec, 65,536 values last ~65 milliseconds. A bug with zero anomalies and a hard crash on a timer — the reset-on-advance rule is what makes 16 bits "a fortune".

4.2 Random offsets in [-8, +8] usually span less than the full ±8, so WALL's inversions shrink and HLC's measured drift lands below spread − 1 — every exhibit gets softer while remaining true. The echo: the linearizability lab's calm-network row and causal-consistency's no-injected-asymmetry lesson, third appearance — a test that doesn't force the adversarial configuration understates the failure; pinning the extremes is the harness making the adversary real.

4.3 Nothing gained, nothing broken — and you can prove it without running: after every event, the sender's own rule enforced l ≥ pt, so the stamp's m.l already dominates any m.pt you could ship alongside it. Absorbing m.pt adds a value that is ≤ one already in the max. (Shipping pt instead of l would be the recvIgnoresPt bug with extra steps.)

Rung 5 — Implement

long lNew = Math.max(l, Math.max(ml, pt));
if (lNew == l && lNew == ml) c = Math.max(c, mc) + 1;
else if (lNew == l)          c = c + 1;
else if (lNew == ml)         c = mc + 1;
else                         c = 0;          // own wall won outright
l = lNew;

Verified 6/6 against the harness. The hints decoded: reset only when l genuinely moved past everything it must dominate (the final branch — your wall reading alone won); when l ties with the message you must beat the message's counter, when it ties with both you must beat both — cases 3 vs 4 are exactly those two ties.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 (a) Correctness of stamps: intact — the rules never depended on the clock being right, and its first received message hands it a fresh l. (b) Drift: its own view is fine (l − pt on this node is large but that is the metric working: 40s slow clock → l runs ~40s "ahead" of its wall — a dashboard's downstream-of-a-liar signature in reverse). The cluster-wide bound is violated in the declared sense: this node is outside max-offset. (c) Local "as of now" queries: its pt-based notion of now is 40s in the past — it will serve snapshots that silently exclude 40 seconds of perfectly-visible history: stale reads with no error. At rejoin, before serving: re-sync NTP and verify offset within bound (or refuse — the CockroachDB suicide, applied at startup), and wait out max-offset so no stamp it issues can precede one it issued before the partition — CockroachDB literally does this sleep on restart.

7.2 The token enters the receive rule: l' = max(l, HUGE, pt) = HUGE — the first server's clock jumps ~146,000 years ahead; every message it sends carries HUGE onward; max means no node ever recovers (l never decreases). All ordering still "works", but every wall-time promise — snapshots, TTLs, GC-by-age, uncertainty windows — is destroyed cluster-wide, permanently, from one packet. That is experiment 7 (the +60 liar) with the offset chosen by an attacker. Defenses: (1) authenticate the token — MongoDB HMAC-signs clusterTime so only servers can advance it; (2) bound the jump — refuse/clamp any incoming stamp more than max-offset ahead of local pt (a liar can then only push you to the edge of the already- tolerated spread). Belt and suspenders; production uses both.

Rung 8 — Design (worked discussion, not a single right answer)

Clock: HLC per region-node, 48+16 packed, stamps on every audit entry; tokens (the entry's stamp) returned to writers and piggybacked on inter-region replication messages, MongoDB-style, so the regions' clocks chase each other continuously.

Requirement mapping. Causally-consistent replay: HLC order is exactly this — sort a tenant's entries by (l, c, regionId); the one-way property is fine because replay needs an order consistent with causality, not concurrency detection. "As of ": the consistent-cut property — cut at (T, ∞); correct within the clock spread, so publish the caveat "± clock offset" with the answer, and alarm when NTP degrades. Dashboard read-your-writes: the causality token — client carries its last write's stamp; any region absorbing it serves reads at ≥ that stamp (or waits the few ms until its clock passes it). This is the requirement that cannot be met by clock quality alone: no bound short of zero makes a slow-clock region's "now" cover a fast-clock region's fresh write; you need the token (per-session, cheap) — or global coordination you explicitly don't want for an audit log.

Concurrent entries (same tenant, two regions, no causal path): both stamps are valid and unequal; (l, c, regionId) totally orders them arbitrarily-but-consistently everywhere — acceptable for an append-only log (no conflict to resolve, only an order to agree on). Never present that order as "who acted first" to regulators when the gap is inside the clock spread — within ±offset, wall order between unrelated events is fiction; say so in the product.

At ±25ms vs ±2s. Ordering, replay, tokens: unaffected at any skew — that is the whole point of the design. What degrades at ±2s: "as of " gains a ±2s ambiguity band (disclose it), and cross-region "first" claims inside 2s become meaningless. What breaks if unpoliced: any component that trusted the bound (TTL cleanup, retention). So monitor: per-node NTP offset with a hard eviction threshold (crash beats lie), l − pt drift per node (the downstream-of-a-liar detector), token-wait durations at read time (rising = spread rising), and c-value distribution (bursts and chained load made visible). During the ±2s incident you widen the disclosed band and keep running — the availability-shaped answer an audit log wants, and the exact opposite of commit wait, which would have every writer stall 4s. If the product ever needs strangers'-order guarantees (billing cutoffs, auctions), that is external consistency: buy it with commit wait or a single sequencer for that stream alone — not by pretending the clocks are better than they are.
