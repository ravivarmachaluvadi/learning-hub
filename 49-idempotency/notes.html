Lab 49 · Work That Spans Services

Idempotency — one effect, no matter how many tries

Lab contents: this document · visualization.html (interactive: "The Second Tap") · EXERCISES.md · SOLUTIONS.md. No runnable code — the mechanism is demonstrated by hand-worked traces here and by the visualization's simulator.

You have met the slogan of this lab many times: eight prior labs end some chapter with "…and therefore consumers must be idempotent." The transactional-outbox lab said it about events, the networking lab measured why (TCP delivers bytes to kernels, never outcomes), raft said it about client retries of uncommitted entries, and distributed-locking left "idempotency keys" as the explicitly-unfinished half of its honest corner (box G). This lab opens the slogan up. Being idempotent is not a checkbox — it is a small protocol with three parts that each fail in a specific, measurable way when done naively, and one part that no amount of cleverness removes.

Depth map

BEGINNER MODEL (§1–§9): why duplicates are mandatory, why guessing doesn't work, and the key idea of naming intentions. INTERMEDIATE MODEL (§10): the mechanism, built four times, each version broken by a trace you can watch. ENGINEERING MODEL (§11–§12): the full failure catalogue and the exact statement of what is and is not guaranteed. PRODUCTION MODEL (§13): Stripe, Kafka, SQS, HTTP semantics, Spring shapes, and what to put on a dashboard.

1. WHY — the duplicate is not a bug, it is the price of reliability

When your code sends a request and no answer comes back, there are two worlds you cannot tell apart: the request never arrived (safe to resend) and the request was processed and only the answer was lost (resending repeats it). The networking lab measured this directly: a timeout elapsing at ≈ your configured limit is the fingerprint of silence, and silence carries no information about what the other side did.

So any client that wants reliability must retry — the communication-protocols lab called timeout + retry + idempotency "one atom" for exactly this reason. And every retry is, from the server's point of view, possibly a brand-new request. If nobody solves this, the failure mode is not exotic: it is a customer charged twice, an email sent three times, a shipment dispatched again. The retry that makes the system reliable is the same act that makes it wrong.

2. PROBLEM — Asha taps Pay twice

Asha is buying a ₹2,000 concert ticket in the PayEasy app. She taps Pay. The app sends POST /payments {amount: 2000, concert: "indie-night"} to the PayEasy server. The server charges her card at the bank, saves an order, and sends back "success" — but that reply is lost in a tunnel between two cell towers. Asha's app shows a spinner for ten seconds, then: "Something went wrong. Try again." She taps Pay again.

The server now receives a second POST /payments {amount: 2000, concert: "indie-night"}. Look at it from the server's chair: this request is byte-for-byte a legitimate purchase. There is no field in it that says "I am attempt two of an earlier intention." The server does the only thing it can: charges her again. Asha is out ₹4,000, and no component misbehaved — the network lost one packet, and everything else worked exactly as designed.

Two observations that shape everything downstream:

  • The duplicate is indistinguishable from a new order by content alone. Maybe Asha genuinely wants two tickets — friends exist. The bytes are identical either way.
  • Only Asha's phone knows that the two sends are one intention. The knowledge lives at the client, at the moment of the first send.

3. NAIVE SOLUTION — check before charging

The reasonable first fix: before charging, look for evidence you already did.

-- illustrative, to read, not to run
SELECT id FROM orders
 WHERE customer = 'asha' AND concert = 'indie-night'
   AND created_at > now() - interval '2 minutes';
-- none found? → charge the card, INSERT the order

If a recent matching order exists, skip the charge and return it. This is deduplication by guessing: infer "same intention" from similar content close together in time.

4. FAILURE — three ways, one deeper problem

🔮 Predict: Asha's app auto-retries after 3 seconds, and her first request was merely slow, not lost. Both requests are now inside the server, 3 ms apart, both at the SELECT. What happens?

Reveal

Both SELECTs run before either INSERT — both see "no recent order" — both charge. This is the check-then-act gap, and you have already watched it kill a design once: the distributed-locking lab measured the defensive pre-write check dying in its own gap. Checking harder, sooner, or twice does not help; between any check and the act it guards, the world can move.

Walked through concretely, the naive check fails three independent ways:

  1. Concurrent duplicates (the trace above): two requests interleave check-before-act. Frequency in real systems: double-taps and aggressive client retry policies make this the common case, not the rare one.
  2. The guess is wrong in both directions. False positive: Asha really does want two tickets, bought 40 seconds apart — the check silently swallows a real order. False negative: Asha's retry went through a different code path that changed a field (the app re-priced the cart to ₹1,900) — the content no longer matches and the duplicate sails through.
  3. Crash between charge and record. The server charges the bank, then dies before the INSERT. The retry's SELECT finds nothing — the evidence the check depends on is exactly what the crash destroyed.

The deeper problem all three point at: the server is trying to reconstruct intent from content, but intent is not in the content. The fix cannot be a better guess. The intent has to be named, by the party that has it, at the moment it exists — and acting on that name has to be atomic with checking it.

5. THE REAL REQUIREMENT

For one intention, any number of physical requests must result in:

  1. At most one effect. The card is charged once, no matter how many copies of the request arrive, in whatever order, however interleaved.
  2. Every attempt learns the one outcome. Asha's second tap must get back the receipt from the first charge — not an error, not a fresh charge. (A dedup layer that swallows duplicates but answers "duplicate!" leaves the client no better off; it still doesn't know what happened.)

The google-docs lab met requirement 2 in miniature: the server there kept a per-client edit number and re-sent the original confirmation for a duplicate edit — it called that "an idempotency key in its smallest form." This lab is that mechanism grown to full size.

6. KEY IDEA

The client mints a unique name for the intention — before the first send — and attaches the same name to every retry. The server makes claiming the name atomic with doing the work, stores the outcome under the name, and answers every later arrival of the name from the stored outcome.

That is the whole invention. Naming moves the "is this a duplicate?" question from guessing about content to equality of an identifier — a question with a crisp answer. Atomic claiming closes the check-then-act gap by making the check and the act one indivisible event. Storing the outcome satisfies requirement 2.

7. TERMINOLOGY — the professional vocabulary

Now that you have felt the problem, the standard words for its parts:

Idempotent — plain: doing it again changes nothing. Precise: in mathematics, f(f(x)) = f(x) — applying the function to its own output is a no-op (abs(abs(-3)) = abs(-3)). Why the term exists: it isolates the one property that makes retries safe, separate from other niceties like commutativity. In distributed systems the usage shifts subtly: we rarely feed an output back in; we mean processing the same request twice leaves the system in the state one processing leaves it in. SET x = 5 is idempotent; INCREMENT x is not. You met the mathematical version as one of the three CRDT laws (crdts: merge must be commutative, associative, idempotent — where sum-merge instead of max-merge read 11 where the truth was 2, purely from re-delivery).

Natural idempotency — plain: some operations are idempotent for free, by their own algebra. SET x=5, DELETE row 7, PUT /users/42 {...} (full replacement), a CRDT max-merge. Why it matters: if you can shape an operation to be naturally idempotent, you need none of this lab's machinery for the duplicate problem. The limit: "charge ₹2,000" has no natural idempotent shape — the effect is a new thing in the world each time. Those operations need the key.

Idempotency key — plain: the name the client gives the intention. Precise: a client-generated unique identifier (in practice a UUID, e.g. ck_7f3a9d10), minted once per intention, attached verbatim to every retry of it, and used by the server as the identity of the operation. Why it exists: it carries the client's knowledge ("these sends are one intention") across the network to the party that must act on it. Connection: the offer id in uber-system-design and the per-client edit number in google-docs were both this.

Deduplication (dedup) — plain: discarding copies. Precise: detecting that an arriving item's identity has been seen and suppressing re-processing. Distinction the term is coined for: dedup is the detect-and-suppress half only; idempotent processing also includes answering with the original outcome and surviving crashes mid-work. Every idempotency-key design contains a dedup step; a dedup step alone is not the design.

Request fingerprint — plain: a checksum of what the request asked for. Precise: a hash of the significant request content (e.g. sha256(method + path + body) = 9d1f…) stored beside the key. Why it exists: to catch the client bug of reusing a key for a different request — same key + different fingerprint is a contract violation and must be refused, never executed (you'll see it as a 422 in §10).

Response replay — plain: answering a retry with the saved original answer. Precise: the stored serialized response (status + body) returned for any arrival of an already-settled key, without re-executing anything.

Delivery guarantees — the three phrases you have used for many labs, now pinned down. At-most-once: send, never retry — duplicates impossible, loss possible. At-least-once: retry until acknowledged — loss impossible (eventually), duplicates guaranteed possible. Exactly-once delivery: impossible over a lossy network — this is the two-generals result wearing delivery clothes; no acknowledgement protocol can make the sender and receiver certain simultaneously. The industry's honest phrasing: exactly-once processing (or "effectively once") = at-least-once delivery composed with idempotent processing, so the effect count is one even though the arrival count isn't. When a vendor says "exactly-once", this is what the truthful ones mean.

Minor terms defined at first use later: claim, settled, dedup window, recovery point, re-drive, reaper.

8. MENTAL MODELS

The simple model: the numbered form and the filing teller. A bank branch handles transfer requests on paper forms. Each pad prints a unique serial number on every form. The teller keeps a filing cabinet ordered by serial. Procedure: when a form arrives, look up its serial. Never seen → file the form, do the transfer, staple the receipt to the filed form. Seen → photocopy the stapled receipt and hand it over; do not touch the money. A customer who wants a second transfer fills a fresh form — fresh serial. A customer who scribbles new amounts on a photocopied old form is turned away at the window: forms are immutable once numbered.

The engineering model: a idempotency_keys table in the same database as the business data, one row per key, carrying a tiny state machine:

stateDiagram-v2
    direction LR
    [*] --> RUNNING : atomic claim\n(INSERT, unique key)
    RUNNING --> SUCCEEDED : effect done,\nresponse stored
    RUNNING --> FAILED : effect refused,\nerror stored
    SUCCEEDED --> SUCCEEDED : duplicate → replay
    FAILED --> FAILED : duplicate → replay
    RUNNING --> RUNNING : duplicate → 409 retry-later

with columns key, fingerprint, state, response, created_at and one hard rule: the transition into the table (the claim) and the business effect must be atomic — or provably recoverable when they cannot be (§10).

Simple model Engineering model
the printed serial number the idempotency key (client-minted UUID)
the form's written content the request body
comparing the form to the filed one fingerprint check
the filing cabinet the idempotency_keys table
filing the form the atomic claim (INSERT, unique constraint)
doing the transfer the business effect
stapling the receipt storing the response
photocopying the receipt response replay
"come back later, teller busy with that serial" 409 on a RUNNING key

Where the analogy breaks — four places, each one a real design problem the teller never has:

  1. One teller, no races. A single human serializes everything. Real servers process duplicates concurrently; the atomic claim exists precisely because two threads can hold the "same form" at once.
  2. The teller doesn't die mid-transfer. If she did, a filed form with no stapled receipt would sit in the cabinet forever — and that is exactly the stuck-RUNNING state §11 deals with via reapers and re-drive.
  3. Paper serials can't collide; client UUIDs can be misused. The teller trusts the pad. The server must verify the client kept its side of the contract — hence the fingerprint.
  4. The cabinet is infinite. Real key tables are purged (Stripe: 24 hours), and the purge creates a reopened hole: a retry arriving after its key was purged is a new intention as far as the server can tell (§11.7).

10. THE MECHANISM — built four times

We now build the server's payment handler the way a good engineer actually would: simplest thing first, then fix what a trace breaks. Versions are named v1–v4; the visualization's designs B/C/D are v1/v2/v4.

v1 — dedup check (the naive solution, formalized)

Keep a seen_keys set. On arrival: if key present → return "duplicate"; else do the work, then add the key.

You already watched this die in §4, but now with the key the trace is sharper, because the guessing failures (2a/2b) are gone — content no longer matters — and what remains is pure atomicity:

t=0ms   request A (key ck_7f3a) arrives, thread T1
t=3ms   request B (key ck_7f3a) arrives, thread T2   ← auto-retry
t=4ms   T1: ck_7f3a in seen_keys?  no
t=5ms   T2: ck_7f3a in seen_keys?  no                ← the gap
t=6ms   T1: charge bank ₹2,000                        (charge #1)
t=9ms   T2: charge bank ₹2,000                        (charge #2)
t=10ms  T1: add ck_7f3a
t=11ms  T2: add ck_7f3a                               (no-op, too late)

Also unfixed: crash after the charge, before the add — the retry finds a clean set. Run visualization scenario 2 to watch both.

v2 — atomic claim: INSERT first, unique constraint arbitrates

Flip the order and let the database be the referee:

-- illustrative shape; one local transaction
BEGIN;
INSERT INTO idempotency_keys (key, fingerprint, state)
     VALUES ('ck_7f3a', '9d1f…', 'RUNNING');   -- UNIQUE(key) ← the claim
-- do the business work (all local writes)
UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - 2000 WHERE id = 'asha';
INSERT INTO orders VALUES (...);
UPDATE idempotency_keys SET state = 'SUCCEEDED', response = '{"order":184}'
     WHERE key = 'ck_7f3a';
COMMIT;

The duplicate's INSERT of the same key hits the unique constraint. The database's uniqueness arbitration is precisely an atomic check-and-act — there is no instant between "is it there?" and "now it is" in which a second inserter can slip. This is the isolation-levels lab's "a constraint beats an isolation level" doing its finest work, and structurally it is the seventh costume of the one primitive the optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking lab kept meeting: a conditional write, here INSERT … WHERE nothing wears this name yet. (Costumes one through six: expected-version, WATCH, If-Match, @Version, incarnation number, fencing check.)

Concurrency plays out cleanly (this is Postgres/H2 behavior for a unique index; flagged as vendor-verified behavior, not run in this lab): the duplicate's INSERT blocks until the first transaction resolves, then raises a unique violation; the handler catches it, reads the settled row, and replays the stored response. And a crash anywhere before COMMIT rolls back everything including the claim — the retry finds a clean table and re-executes from scratch, correctly, because none of the effect survived either. Claim, effect, and outcome share one commit point.

Checkpoint — intermediate model reached. You can now build a fully correct idempotent consumer for any effect that lives entirely in one database: this exact shape — a processed_messages table written in the same transaction as the effect — is the honest implementation of "consumers must be idempotent" that the transactional-outbox and cqrs labs required, and for many real systems it is the whole answer. Run visualization scenario 3 (design C, local effect, crash mid-work) and watch the rollback save it. What the next layer adds: the effect that leaves the database.

🔮 Predict: PayEasy's charge is not a local UPDATE — it is an HTTPS call to the bank. Can the call go inside the v2 transaction? What exactly goes wrong if you try?

Reveal

Two separate disasters. (1) Holding a transaction across a network call — the "lunch problem" the optimistic-locking lab measured: your locks and your connection are held hostage to the bank's tail latency. Bad, but survivable. (2) The fatal one: ROLLBACK cannot unsend a network request. If the charge succeeds at the bank and the server then crashes before COMMIT, the rollback erases the claim, the order, the stored response — every trace that anything happened — but the money moved. The retry finds a virgin key table and charges again. The atomicity that saved v2 was only ever atomicity of local writes.

v3 — v2 plus an external call: the dual-write problem returns

Put the bank call between the claim and the commit and trace the crash windows with real values:

step 1  BEGIN; INSERT ck_7f3a RUNNING          (uncommitted)
step 2  call bank: charge ₹2,000  →  bank says OK, charge exists   ← REAL
step 3  INSERT order #184                       (uncommitted)
step 4  UPDATE ck_7f3a → SUCCEEDED + response   (uncommitted)
step 5  COMMIT

Crash after step 2, before step 5: the bank charged (step 2 is in the world), the rollback erased steps 1/3/4 (they were only in the database's uncommitted buffers). Retry with ck_7f3a → no row → full re-execution → second charge. Run visualization scenario 4.

Recognize the shape: a durable local write and an external side effect that must happen together, with a crash possible between them — this is the dual-write problem from the transactional-outbox lab, returned one hop down the stack. The outbox solved it for "save data + publish event." We need its cousin for "record intention + call bank."

v4 — claim first, in its own transaction; recurse the key downstream

The outbox's move was: make the durable write happen first, alone, then do the unreliable thing, then record the outcome. Apply it here:

txn 1   INSERT ck_7f3a (state RUNNING, fingerprint 9d1f…); COMMIT
        ── the claim is now durable, crash-proof evidence of intent ──
step 2  call bank: charge ₹2,000, passing bank_key = ck_7f3a-charge
step 3  txn 2: INSERT order #184;
               UPDATE ck_7f3a → SUCCEEDED + response; COMMIT
step 4  send response to Asha

Now walk every arrival and every crash:

  • Duplicate after step 3 → key is SUCCEEDED → replay the response. Requirement 2 satisfied; zero re-execution.
  • Duplicate between txn 1 and step 3 (first request still working) → key is RUNNING → answer 409 Conflict, retry later. Why not make the duplicate wait for the outcome? Because holding the duplicate holds a connection and a thread for an unbounded time — the lunch problem again, now on the request path. A quick refusal with "in progress" is cheaper for everyone; the client's next retry will hit SUCCEEDED and get the replay.
  • Same key, different fingerprint (the app re-priced the cart to ₹1,900 but reused ck_7f3a) → 422 Unprocessable, and nothing executes. This is a client bug by definition — one name, two intentions — and the server must refuse rather than guess which intention is real. Scenario 6.
  • Crash after txn 1, before step 2 → key stuck RUNNING, bank never called, Asha's retries bounce off 409 forever. Safety intact (zero charges), liveness lost. The fix is a reaper (the same job-shape as the optimistic-locking lab's crashed-worker reaper): a background process that finds RUNNING rows older than a threshold and re-drives them — re-executes from where the evidence says they stopped.
  • Crash after step 2, before txn 2 — the crux. Key stuck RUNNING, and the bank charged. What may the reaper do? It cannot know whether step 2 happened (that's the two-worlds problem from §1, now the server's own problem — the doubt has moved, not died). If the reaper re-drives by blindly re-calling the bank: second charge.

The move that resolves the crux — and the lab's central insight: look at step 2 again. The re-driven bank call passes the same bank_key as the original attempt. If the bank itself implements idempotency keys, the re-drive's charge call is a duplicate by name at the bank, the bank replays its stored "OK, charge c_991" response, and the reaper learns the truth of step 2 retroactively — then completes txn 2 as if the crash never happened. Run scenario 5 and watch the re-drive land on one charge.

Idempotency is not a middleware you bolt onto your edge; it is a per-hop contract that must recurse until it reaches a hop that can answer duplicates by name. Your server is the bank's client, exactly as Asha's phone is yours. Each hop's retry-doubt is absorbed by the next hop's key table. The doubt only dies at a hop that can atomically claim-and-act (v2's single database, at the bottom of the chain).

And when the bottom hop offers no key — sending an email, calling a dumb legacy API? Then the doubt has nowhere to die, and you are in the distributed-locking lab's box G, whose honest answer this lab can now complete: at-most-once or at-least-once (pick which failure you prefer per operation — a duplicate marketing email is fine, dedup-best-effort; a duplicate payout is not, so hold it), plus reconciliation — a periodic job comparing your ledger with theirs — and a measured residual duplicate rate instead of a pretended zero.

For effects with multiple external steps (charge card → reserve seat → issue ticket), the same claim row grows a recovery_point column — after each completed step, a small transaction advances it (charged → seat_reserved → …) so a re-drive resumes instead of restarting, and each step's own external call carries its own derived key. That is the full Stripe-described architecture (§13), and it is v4 applied per-step; no new idea is needed.

11. FAILURE SCENARIOS — the catalogue

Each entry: how to create it → where to watch → what/why → the response → guarantees kept/lost → production handling. Design v4 assumed except where said.

  1. Lost response, later retry (the founding case). Create: drop the response in the viz; tap again. Watch: scenario 1 vs the same actions on design D. The retry hits SUCCEEDED → replay. Kept: both requirements. Lost: nothing. Production: this is the routine path — Stripe-style APIs serve replays constantly; it should be a counter on a dashboard, not an alert.
  2. Concurrent duplicate (double-tap / aggressive retry timer). Create: viz "double-tap". The claim's unique constraint arbitrates; loser gets 409 (v4) or blocks-then-replays (v2). Kept: at-most-one effect. Lost: the duplicate waits one extra client-retry round for its answer. Production: 409 + Retry-After; clients treat 409-on-idempotent-POST as "poll again," never as failure.
  3. Crash after claim, before effect. Stuck RUNNING; all retries 409. Kept: safety (0 charges). Lost: liveness for this key until the reaper re-drives. Production: oldest-RUNNING-age is the alarm to have (the twin of causal-consistency's oldest-blocked-dependency alarm); re-drive is safe because downstream is keyed.
  4. Crash after external effect, before recording (the crux). Bank charged; server has only RUNNING. Without a keyed bank: irreducible doubt — re-drive risks double-charge, not re-driving strands Asha. With a keyed bank: re-drive replays at the bank, truth recovered, exactly-one restored. Kept (keyed): everything, eventually. Lost: latency until re-drive. Production: this window is why serious payment APIs require their callers to send keys — the provider is protecting you from your own crashes.
  5. Same key, different body. 422, nothing executes, fingerprint mismatch counter increments. Kept: the key's meaning. Lost: nothing — but the client team has a bug to fix; a rising mismatch rate is a client-regression detector.
  6. Key store separate from the data store (keys in Redis "for speed", orders in Postgres). Create: viz design C/D with the key table set to "separate, volatile" — or just reason it: claim in Redis and effect in Postgres are two durable systems again — the dual-write problem rebuilt at the exact spot it was solved, plus Redis restart-from-empty silently forgets every claim (the quorums lab's "rebuilt-empty replica satisfies the quorum" trap, wearing a cache costume). Response: the key row lives in the same database, same transaction, as the effect it guards — or, across services, in the store at the bottom of the recursion. Production: this is the single most common real-world implementation error; Redis-backed dedup is acceptable only as a best-effort accelerator in front of a durable claim, never as the claim.
  7. Retry after key expiry (the dedup window). Stripe purges at 24 h; SQS FIFO deduplicates within 5 minutes only. A retry arriving after the purge is, to the server, a new intention → executed. Kept: nothing, by design — the window IS the guarantee's boundary, an explicit SLA. Production: pick retention ≫ any legitimate retry horizon (client timeout × max attempts, plus queue redelivery delays, plus a margin); emit a metric for keys arriving "shortly after purge" if you can; and make clients stop retrying long before the window closes.
  8. The client mints a fresh key per retry (the client-side bug that defeats everything). The server is helpless — two names, two intentions, two charges, all correct by contract. Response: none available server-side; the contract's client half is: mint at intention time (button render, not button press), persist the key with the pending intention (so an app restart retries with the same key), reuse verbatim until a terminal outcome. Production: SDKs generate and persist the key for you; UI double-submit guards are the shallow cousin of this rule.

Cross-cutting: notice which failures are safety (2, 4, 6, 8 threaten a second charge) and which are liveness (3, and the 409 waits). The design consistently spends liveness to buy safety — the same trade Raft made — and every liveness hole is patched by a background process (reaper) rather than by weakening the claim.

12. CORRECTNESS — what is guaranteed, what is not, what it cost

Guaranteed (v4, with every external hop keyed, within retention):

  • Per key, at most one effect-lineage. However many arrivals, whatever interleaving: at most one charge reaches the world. The mechanism is the claim's atomicity (unique constraint) plus the recursion absorbing crash doubt.
  • Every attempt eventually learns the one outcome (replay), once the key settles; RUNNING gaps are bounded by the reaper period.
  • Together: effectively-exactly-once — at-least-once arrivals, exactly one effect, observed by all attempts.

Explicitly NOT guaranteed:

  • Exactly-once delivery. Impossible; the design never tries. Arrivals are many; effects are one. (Two-generals, as in §7.)
  • Anything across different keys. Two keys are two intentions by definition. If Asha's phone mints fresh keys per tap (failure 8), or two services independently decide to charge her, the key table sees two legitimate operations. Business-level duplicate detection (velocity rules, "you already bought this — continue?") is a separate, heuristic layer — it is §3's guessing, redeemed by being advisory instead of load-bearing.
  • Freshness of a replayed response. The replay is a snapshot of the outcome, not current state: if the order was refunded an hour ago, a late retry of the original key still replays "order #184 confirmed." That is correct — the retry asks "what became of intention ck_7f3a?", not "what is true now?" — but it surprises people; state queries belong on GET, not on replays. (Kinship: the stale-read-as-non-event from linearizability-vs-serializability.)
  • Protection against reordering or interleaving of different operations. Idempotency answers "is this the same intention again?" and nothing else. A stale SET x=5 replayed after a newer SET x=7 is perfectly idempotent and still wrong — that is a versioning problem (expected-version, hlc stamps), and a concurrency problem (optimistic locking) — three orthogonal defenses that production systems stack. Compare fencing (distributed-locking): a fencing token refuses stale writers (an ordering identity), an idempotency key refuses duplicate writers (a sameness identity). Both are conditional writes at the resource; neither implies the other.

What was traded away: a durable write before the work and another after (latency + storage on every request, duplicate or not); a 409 path every client must implement; a reaper and its re-drive logic; retention management; and — the expensive one — the requirement that every participant cooperates: the client's key discipline, every hop's key table, down to a bottom that can claim atomically. A single non-cooperating hop reopens the doubt (and gets the box-G treatment: reconcile + measure).

The one-sentence relationships (commonly-confused corner):

  • Idempotency vs dedup: dedup suppresses copies; idempotency also answers them and survives crashing mid-copy.
  • Idempotency vs fencing: duplicate-refusal vs stale-refusal.
  • Idempotency vs optimistic locking: same-intention-twice vs two-intentions-colliding; the claim INSERT is the same conditional-write primitive both use.
  • Idempotency vs natural idempotency: the protocol manufactures for "charge ₹2,000" what SET x=5 has by algebra.
  • Exactly-once delivery vs exactly-once processing: impossible vs purchasable (this lab is the purchase).

13. PRODUCTION

The chain from toy to real: §10's v4 → Stripe's published architecture → your Spring service → the dashboard.

Stripe (the canonical public design; from their API docs and engineering blog — read them, you now have every concept they use): Idempotency-Key request header; keys stored with a fingerprint of the request; concurrent duplicate → 409 idempotency_key_in_use; settled duplicate → replayed response flagged Idempotent-Replayed: true; keys purged after 24 h; different-body reuse → 422. Their engineering write-up (Brandur Leach, "Implementing Stripe-like Idempotency Keys") describes exactly §10 v4 grown up: the key row as a state machine advanced by atomic phases (local transactions), recovery points between foreign-state mutations, each outbound call keyed, and a reaper ("completer") that re-drives stuck keys. That article is this lab's reading-list centerpiece.

The IETF draft draft-ietf-httpapi-idempotency-key-header standardizes the header name and the 409/422 semantics — worth knowing it exists (status: draft; check before citing it in an API spec).

HTTP method semantics — the spec-level layer above all of this: RFC 9110 declares GET/HEAD/PUT/DELETE idempotent by contract and POST not. Infrastructure acts on this: proxies, retry middlewares, and SDKs auto-retry idempotent methods and refuse to auto-retry POST. Two duties follow: never implement a non-idempotent handler behind an idempotent-by-contract method (a DELETE that decrements a counter invites infrastructure to corrupt you), and give POSTs that need retry safety an idempotency key, which is precisely the header's job. (PUT's idempotency is natural — full replacement — which is why "just use PUT with a client-chosen id" is a legitimate lightweight alternative for creation: PUT /orders/ck_7f3a makes the key the resource name.)

Kafka — two distinct features, often blurred: the idempotent producer (enable.idempotence=true, default since 3.0) gives each producer a PID and a per-partition sequence number, and brokers drop already-seen sequences — dedup for the producer→broker hop only, within a producer session; structurally it is google-docs' per-client edit number at scale. Transactions/EOS extend this with commit markers and read_committed consumers (the Percolator shape you met in distributed-transactions). Neither does anything for your consumer's side effects — "exactly-once" ends at the topic. Your consumer's effect is exactly-once only if you apply this lab: commit the effect and the consumed offset in the same local transaction (offsets stored in your database, not Kafka's), which is v2 verbatim with the offset as the key.

SQS FIFO: MessageDeduplicationId, hard 5-minute dedup window — failure 7 as a product spec. DynamoDB: conditional PutItem (attribute_not_exists(pk)) is the claim primitive without a relational database. Idempotent consumers in Spring — the shape you'd actually write (illustrative, to read):

@Transactional  // one local transaction = v2's single commit point
public void handle(OrderEvent evt) {
    try {
        jdbc.update("INSERT INTO processed_messages(id) VALUES (?)",
                    evt.messageId());          // the claim; UNIQUE(id)
    } catch (DuplicateKeyException dup) {
        return;                                 // settled → nothing to do
    }
    applyBusinessEffect(evt);                   // local writes only
}

and the edge-API version is v4: a filter/interceptor owning the key table, claim-first, replay, 409, fingerprint — or an off-the-shelf one; verify any library actually does atomic claim + replay + fingerprint, not just a Redis SETNX (failure 6 in a box).

What the on-call engineer watches: replay rate (routine; a spike means a client retry storm or a broken network path), 409 rate (concurrency pressure), oldest-RUNNING age (the page-worthy alarm: reaper down or re-drive failing), fingerprint-mismatch rate (a client released a bug), key-table size and purge lag, reconciliation deltas for box-G hops (the measured residual). Incident smell worth keeping: "duplicate charges" with a healthy key table almost always means failure 8 (client minting fresh keys) — check the keys on the two charges first; if they differ, the bug is in the app, not your layer.

What this lab intentionally omits (and where the real world handles it): multi-region key storage (a key table must be authoritative where the traffic lands; active-active regions turn the claim into a linearizability problem — Stripe pins an idempotent request family to a region); key-table sharding (by key hash — consistent-hashing's job); response-size limits and streaming responses (replay stores a bounded summary); security (keys are per-API-credential — tenant A must not replay tenant B's key; keys must be unguessable if the response is sensitive); stream-processor exactly-once (Flink's checkpoint-based two-phase sinks — a different mechanism for the same contract); and the business-heuristic advisory layer (§12) that catches cross-key duplicates.

16. GLOSSARY (in order of appearance)

  • idempotent — processing the same request again leaves the state the first processing left it in; math: f(f(x)) = f(x).
  • at-least-once — retry until acknowledged; duplicates possible, loss not (eventually).
  • at-most-once — never retry; loss possible, duplicates not.
  • exactly-once delivery — impossible over a lossy network (two-generals).
  • exactly-once processing / effectively once — at-least-once arrivals + idempotent processing = one effect, observed by all attempts.
  • check-then-act gap — the instant between a guard's check and its action, in which the world can move; kills v1.
  • deduplication — detecting and suppressing already-seen items; the detect half of idempotent processing.
  • idempotency key — client-minted unique name for one intention, reused verbatim on every retry.
  • natural idempotency — idempotent by the operation's own algebra (SET, DELETE, full PUT, CRDT merge); needs no key machinery.
  • request fingerprint — hash of request content stored with the key; catches key-reuse-with-different-body (→ 422).
  • response replay — answering a settled key's duplicate with the stored original response.
  • claim — the atomic INSERT of the key row (unique constraint) that makes this arrival the one executor.
  • settled — a key in a terminal state (SUCCEEDED/FAILED), safe to replay.
  • RUNNING — claimed, outcome not yet recorded; duplicates get 409.
  • 409 / 422 — in-progress duplicate / same-key-different-fingerprint refusals.
  • dual-write problem — two durable systems that must change together with a crash possible between (outbox lab); returns in v3 and failure 6.
  • recovery point — per-key marker of the last completed step of a multi-step effect, so re-drive resumes instead of restarting.
  • re-drive — re-executing a stuck RUNNING key from its evidence, safe only because downstream hops are keyed.
  • reaper — background process that finds and re-drives stale RUNNING keys.
  • dedup window / retention — how long claims are kept; beyond it a retry is a new intention by design (Stripe 24 h, SQS 5 min).
  • fencing token vs idempotency key — refuses stale writers vs refuses duplicate writers; orthogonal conditional writes.
  • reconciliation — periodic ledger comparison against an unkeyable hop; turns pretended-zero duplicates into a measured residual.
  • idempotent producer (Kafka) — PID + per-partition sequence numbers; dedup on the producer→broker hop only.
  • Idempotency-Key header — the HTTP carrier of the key (Stripe; IETF httpapi draft).

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 In design D, suppose duplicates arriving on a RUNNING key wait for the outcome instead of getting 409. Which property improves, which resource is newly at risk, and which prior lab's measured failure does the waiting version reenact?

4.2 Change the client so it mints the idempotency key when the Pay button is pressed instead of when the payment screen is rendered. Construct the user story where this one-line change causes a double charge.

4.3 Shorten key retention from 24 hours to 60 seconds. Name a concrete, realistic client behavior (numbers: timeouts, attempt counts, redelivery delays) that now produces a duplicate charge that 24 h would have absorbed.

Rung 5 — Trace

Design D semantics, by hand. Starting state: empty key table, empty orders, empty bank ledger. Events, in order:

e1  request (key ck_9, fp aa11, amount 2000) arrives
e2  server commits claim txn
e3  server calls bank (bank_key ck_9-charge); bank charges, replies OK c_501
e4  SERVER CRASHES
e5  request (key ck_9, fp aa11) arrives again           ← Asha's retry
e6  server restarts
e7  request (key ck_9, fp bb22, amount 1900) arrives    ← app re-priced, reused key!
e8  reaper runs (threshold exceeded), re-drives ck_9
e9  request (key ck_9, fp aa11) arrives again

For each event write: key-table row(s) after it, bank ledger, and the HTTP response (if any) sent to the client. Then state the final charge count and which event was answered "incorrectly" from the user's point of view even though the server followed the contract.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

Each design below contains one planted mistake. For each: construct a concrete run (named requests, interleaved steps) where it produces a wrong outcome, and name the violated guarantee.

6.1 "We INSERT the key row with a unique constraint — atomic claim, done right — then do the local business writes. For speed, the claim INSERT uses its own small transaction that commits immediately, and the business writes follow in a second transaction." (Effect is 100% local database writes.)

6.2 "Keys live in Redis: SET key outcome NX EX 86400. On duplicate we return the cached outcome. The order writes go to Postgres. Redis is configured with appendonly no — it's just dedup data."

6.3 "To keep the table small we store the key row only on success. Failed attempts store nothing, so the client can simply retry a failure with the same key." (Effect includes the external bank call; "failed" is judged by the server's own handler catching any exception.)

Rung 7 — Handle failure

The bottom hop strikes back: your payout service must call a partner bank whose API has no idempotency support — a bare POST /transfer that moves money every time it is called. You cannot change the partner.

Design, on paper, the safest achievable payout pipeline: what your claim row looks like, when you call the partner relative to your transactions, what the reaper is and is not allowed to do with a stuck RUNNING payout, what the reconciliation job compares, and what number you put on a dashboard as the honest residual. State explicitly which requirement from §5 you are giving up and which failure (over-pay or under-pay/late-pay) you chose to prefer, and why that is the right preference for payouts.

Rung 8 — Design

Ticket checkout at production scale: POST /checkout must (a) charge the card via Stripe, (b) reserve a seat in your seats database, (c) email the ticket via a legacy SMTP relay (no dedup of any kind), at ~500 checkouts/s, clients are flaky mobile apps, and finance requires provable at-most-one-charge per checkout.

Produce: the key flow end to end (who mints what, which hops carry which derived keys); the key-table schema including recovery points and the step order (justify which of charge/reserve comes first); the concurrent- duplicate and crash-at-every-step behavior; retention choices per store; the box-G treatment for the email hop; and the five dashboard metrics with the one you page on. Close with the two failure modes your design still permits, stated honestly.

Idempotency — solutions

Rung 1

1.1 A client that never retries loses work every time the network eats a message — and silence tells it nothing, so it cannot even know which work was lost. A client that retries turns every lost answer into a possible duplicate request, because the server cannot tell "attempt two of one intention" from "a second intention" by looking at the bytes. The key moves exactly one piece of knowledge: "these sends are the same intention" — knowledge that exists only at the client, at the moment of the first send, and nowhere else in the system.

1.2 (a) The answer: a dedup layer that says "duplicate, discarded" leaves the caller still ignorant of the outcome — the caller retried precisely because it doesn't know what happened; only replaying the stored original response answers the question actually being asked. (b) Crashes: dedup marks "seen" either before the work (crash after marking → the retry is suppressed and the work never happens) or after it (crash between work and marking → the retry re-executes). Only a claim that is atomic with the work — or recoverable evidence plus keyed downstream hops — survives both.

Rung 2

2.1 The ck_1 row; state SUCCEEDED (green) with the stored response beside it. 2.2 The key table — the log says the retry was answered by replay without contacting the bank or touching orders; no new bank/order activity appears at that step. 2.3 The request body (amount + item) — the fingerprint shown is a hash over the request content; the two requests with identical bodies show identical fingerprints.

Rung 3

3.1 2 charges. Inevitable at the moment the second request's check completes while the first has not yet recorded — from then on both threads are committed to charging; everything after is bookkeeping. The gap is between check and act, so the fatal step is the second check, not either charge.

3.2 The key table is empty — the claim was uncommitted, so the crash rolled it back; the bank ledger has 1 charge. The retry finds no row, claims fresh, and charges again → 2 charges. The rollback destroyed the evidence but not the effect.

3.3 The claim row survived (committed first), state RUNNING. The re-drive re-calls the bank with the same bank-side key; the bank recognizes it and replays its earlier "OK c_501" instead of charging; the server completes the outcome transaction. Final: 1 charge.

Rung 4

4.1 Improves: the duplicate's client gets its answer in one round trip (better latency for that caller, no 409 handling needed). Newly at risk: server threads/connections — every waiting duplicate holds one for the full duration of the original work, unbounded if the bank is slow. This reenacts the optimistic-locking lab's lunch problem: one slow actor converting its delay into everyone's resource starvation. (With bounded pools, a burst of duplicates on a slow key can occupy the whole pool — the networking lab's green-dashboard death.)

4.2 Asha taps Pay → request sent with key ck_a → response lost → app shows "Try again" → she taps Pay again → the press mints a fresh key ck_b → server sees two intentions → two charges, all correct by contract. Minting at render time makes both taps of the same screen carry the same key; a fresh key is minted only when a new payment screen (new intention) is shown.

4.3 Example: client timeout 30 s, 5 attempts with exponential backoff (30 s, 60 s, 120 s, 240 s…) — attempt 4 arrives ~3.5 minutes after attempt

  1. Or: the request rode a queue that redelivers after a 2-minute visibility timeout. With 60 s retention the key was purged before the late attempt arrives → treated as new → executed again. 24 h absorbs any sane retry horizon. Retention must exceed (max attempts × max backoff + redelivery delays) with a wide margin.

Rung 5

after key table (ck_9) bank response sent
e1 — (arriving) — —
e2 RUNNING, fp aa11 — —
e3 RUNNING, fp aa11 c_501 ₹2,000 — (crash imminent)
e4 RUNNING, fp aa11 (durable) c_501 none — connection died
e5 RUNNING, fp aa11 c_501 409 in progress, retry later
e6 unchanged c_501 —
e7 RUNNING, fp aa11 c_501 422 fingerprint mismatch (fp bb22 ≠ aa11; nothing executes)
e8 SUCCEEDED, response stored (re-drive: bank replays c_501; outcome txn commits) c_501 (still 1) — (reaper has no client to answer)
e9 SUCCEEDED c_501 200 replay of the stored response

Final charge count: 1. The "incorrect-feeling" answer is e7: the user changed the cart to ₹1,900 and got an error — but the server is right; ck_9 names the ₹2,000 intention, which was in fact executed. The app's bug is reusing a key across intentions; the 422 is the contract catching it before money moves twice.

Rung 6

6.1 Claim committed separately from the local effect. Run: request A claims ck_1 (txn 1 commits), starts business writes, crashes before txn 2. Retry arrives: claim exists → treated as duplicate → 409 forever / replay of nothing; the effect never happened and never will. The guarantee violated is §5's requirement 2 — an attempt never learns a real outcome — and re-driving is now mandatory even for a purely local effect, which the single-transaction v2 got for free. Splitting the claim from a local effect buys nothing and reintroduces the stuck state; claim-first is only for effects that cannot share the transaction.

6.2 Redis claim, Postgres effect — failure 6 embodied, two runs: (a) claim in Redis succeeds, server crashes before Postgres commit → retry is suppressed by Redis and replays a stored "outcome" for work that never happened (requirement 2 violated). (b) Redis restarts (appendonly no → empty): every settled key is forgotten; any late retry re-executes → double charge (requirement 1 violated). The claim was in a different durable domain than the effect: the dual-write problem rebuilt at the point of its own solution.

6.3 Success-only key rows. The handler judges "failed" by catching an exception — but the networking lab's lesson applies to the bank call inside the handler: a timeout is not "did not happen." Run: bank call times out at 30 s (bank actually charged, reply lost) → handler throws → stores nothing → client retries with the same key → no row → full re-execution → second charge. Violated: at-most-one-effect, in exactly the case the mechanism exists for. Failures must settle as FAILED rows (replayable, like Stripe) — and an ambiguous failure (timeout) must not settle at all until resolved via the keyed downstream or reconciliation; it stays RUNNING for the reaper.

Rung 7 (worked design discussion)

  • Claim row: payout_id (key), fingerprint, state (RUNNING/SENT_UNCONFIRMED/CONFIRMED/HELD), partner_ref?, created_at — in the payouts database, same store as the ledger.
  • Ordering: commit the claim (RUNNING) first; then the partner call; then commit outcome. Immediately before the call, advance state to SENT_UNCONFIRMED in its own small committed transaction — so the evidence distinguishes "never called" from "called, outcome unknown."
  • Reaper rules: a stuck RUNNING (crash before the call) is provably unsent → safe to re-drive the call. A stuck SENT_UNCONFIRMED is the irreducible doubt → the reaper must not re-call (that risks double-pay); it parks the payout as HELD for reconciliation. This is the at-most-once choice.
  • Reconciliation: compare partner statements/reports against SENT_UNCONFIRMED/HELD and the ledger, keyed by amount + account + your reference in the transfer narration (the closest thing to a key you can smuggle in). Matches promote to CONFIRMED; misses release the HELD payout for a fresh, human-approved send.
  • Dashboard residual: count and age of HELD payouts, plus reconciliation mismatch count — the honest number that replaces pretended exactly-once.
  • What's given up: §5 requirement 1 is kept by sacrificing liveness (payouts can stall in HELD awaiting reconciliation) — for payouts, under-pay-late is a support ticket and an apology; over-pay is money gone to clawback. At-most-once per hop + reconciliation is the correct preference. (A marketing-email hop would choose the opposite: at-least-once, duplicates shrugged off.)

Rung 8 (worked design discussion)

  • Keys: app mints checkout_key at checkout-screen render, persists it locally with the pending intention, reuses across retries and app restarts. Server derives per-step keys: <key>-charge → Stripe's Idempotency-Key; <key>-seat → the seat reservation (internal, but keyed so the step is re-drivable); email gets no key (none exists).
  • Schema: idempotency_keys(key, fingerprint, state, recovery_point ∈ {started, seat_reserved, charged, emailed}, response, created_at) in the orders database.
  • Step order — reserve seat before charging. Both orders can be made correct (both steps are keyed and re-drivable), so choose by cost of the interrupted state: seat-then-charge leaves a seat held for a customer whose card then fails — release it (cheap, internal, a compensation in the saga-lab sense). Charge-then-seat can take money for a sold-out seat → refund (slow, visible, finance-touching). Reserve with a TTL hold (uber-lab offer/lease shape), charge, then confirm the hold.
  • Concurrency/crash: duplicate on RUNNING → 409 + Retry-After; duplicate on settled → replay with Idempotent-Replayed: true; wrong fingerprint → 422. Crash anywhere → reaper re-drives from recovery_point: seat hold re-asserted by key (idempotent re-reserve), Stripe re-called with the same derived key (Stripe replays), email step — see below. Every recovery-point advance is its own small committed transaction.
  • Retention: key table 24–48 h (≫ any retry horizon; matches Stripe's window so your replay horizon isn't longer than your downstream's); Stripe's own 24 h noted as a dependency. Seat holds: minutes (TTL).
  • Email (box G): choose at-least-once — a duplicate ticket email is harmless, a missing one is a support call. Record emailed after the SMTP accept; a crash between send and record re-sends on re-drive. Best-effort dedup accelerator (Redis SETNX on <key>-email, short TTL) cuts routine duplicates without being trusted. Residual duplicate-email rate is measured, not denied.
  • Metrics: replay rate; 409 rate; oldest-RUNNING age (the page); fingerprint-mismatch rate; Stripe-replay-on-re-drive count (how often crashes actually bit); plus reconciliation delta of Stripe charges vs local orders.
  • Honest residual failure modes: (1) duplicate emails (chosen); (2) cross-key duplicates — a user who abandons checkout, re-opens a fresh checkout screen, and buys again holds two legitimate keys; only the advisory heuristic layer ("you already have a ticket — continue?") can catch that, and it is a guess by design.
