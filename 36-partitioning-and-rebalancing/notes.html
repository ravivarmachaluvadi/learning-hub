Partitioning & Rebalancing

One dataset, many machines — and the day the set of machines changes.

Lab format (no-code): this document + visualization.html (interactive simulator, also published as an artifact) + EXERCISES.md / SOLUTIONS.md. Everything here is demonstrated by hand-worked traces, by the simulator, or by a measured result cited from a prior lab — never by code you have to run.

Where you are: you already know how to hash a key onto a machine from the consistent-hashing lab — the ring, virtual nodes, fixed slots, and the measured disaster of hash(key) % n. That lab ended with an honest confession: "the ring is a pure placement function; it says where things belong and moves nothing — joining a node is a separate throttled streaming operation with its own state machine." This lab is that state machine, plus everything the placement function alone can't answer: how to draw the boundaries in the first place (by key order, not just by hash), what happens when the data or the traffic is lopsided, how queries find their partition, and how a piece of a live database physically moves from one machine to another without dropping a single write.

1. WHY — the problem nobody can dodge

Every system you've studied so far in these labs quietly assumed the data fits on the machines you have. Eventually it doesn't: the dataset grows past one machine's disk, or — usually first — the traffic grows past one machine's CPU and network. Replication (the quorums-and-replication lab) doesn't rescue you: replication makes more copies of everything, which helps reads and survives crashes but does nothing when everything is too big, and nothing at all for write traffic — every replica still receives every write.

So you must split the data: each machine holds only a piece. The moment you split, three questions appear that never existed before, and each one has a famous way of going wrong:

  1. Where does each record live? Draw the boundaries badly and one machine does all the work while the others idle.
  2. How does a query find its record? Route badly and every query interrogates every machine.
  3. What happens when the set of machines changes? Handle growth badly and adding capacity — the thing you did to make the system healthier — triggers a self-inflicted outage while terabytes shuffle between nodes.

Question 3 is the one that separates toy designs from real ones. In the consistent-hashing lab the cost of getting it wrong was a cache miss storm that healed in a minute. Here the data is the source of truth: what moves is not re-fetchable cache entries but the only authoritative copy, the move takes hours at disk-and-network speed, and writes keep arriving while it moves.

2. THE PROBLEM — Meera's library outgrows its building

Meera runs the public library in the town of Deodar (the same town whose noticeboards taught you eventual consistency). The collection has grown to 60,000 books and the building holds 20,000. The town council gives her two more buildings across town.

Now every book needs a home in exactly one building, and two kinds of people need to find it fast:

  • A reader who wants one specific book ("The Cedar Gate by R. Sharma") should walk to one building — not wander all three.
  • A browser who wants everything by one author should ideally find those books shelved together, not scattered.

And Meera knows the town keeps growing. A fourth building is a matter of time. Whatever scheme she picks today, she will one day have to re-home books while the library stays open — readers borrowing and returning the whole time.

No jargon yet. Just: 60,000 books, 3 buildings, and a future fourth.

3. THE NAIVE SOLUTION — split by the alphabet

Meera does the obvious thing: split by author surname.

  • Building 1: authors A–H
  • Building 2: authors I–Q
  • Building 3: authors R–Z

It has real virtues. Any clerk can compute a book's home in their head — no lookup table needed. The specific-book reader walks to one building. The browser is delighted: all of R. Sharma's novels stand on one shelf, in order. Splitting by key order keeps neighbouring keys together, and "everything by one author" is exactly a neighbourhood.

4. THE FAILURE — even thirds of the alphabet are not even thirds of anything

Three months in, the scheme is failing in two different ways — and telling them apart is the first real lesson of this lab.

Failure A — the counts are uneven. Surnames are not spread evenly across the alphabet. Building 3 (R–Z) holds 31,000 books — S alone is enormous (Sharma, Singh, Shah, Sen...) — while Building 2 (I–Q) holds 9,000. Building 3's shelves overflow while Building 2 echoes. Call this what it is: the amount of data per building is lopsided. This failure is annoying but fixable by redrawing the lines: nothing forced Meera to cut the alphabet into equal letter spans; she could cut it into equal book counts (A–G / H–R / S–Z, say). The boundaries should follow the data, not the alphabet.

Failure B — the queues are uneven, and no boundary fixes it. R. Sharma publishes a new novel and it becomes a phenomenon. Every day, hundreds of people come to borrow, return, or reserve that one book. They all queue at Building 3. Buildings 1 and 2 sit idle while Building 3's line goes around the block. Now watch carefully: redrawing boundaries cannot help, because all the demand is for a single title — however you cut the collection, that title lands somewhere, and wherever it lands drowns. Uneven demand is a different disease from uneven data, and it needs different medicine.

🔮 Predict: Meera gives up on the alphabet and instead assigns each book to a building by rolling a die (uniformly at random, recorded in the catalog). Which of the two failures does this fix — A, B, or both?

Reveal

It fixes A completely and B not at all — and it creates a third problem. Random assignment spreads the 60,000 books almost perfectly evenly (that's what randomness is good at). But the Sharma novel still lives in exactly one building, and the whole town still queues there: one popular record beats any placement scheme, because every scheme agrees each record has one home — you met this as the hot-key result in the consistent-hashing lab, and the fix (splitting the record itself) comes in stage 11.

The new problem: the browser is ruined. Sharma's twelve novels are now scattered across all three buildings at random. "Everything by one author" went from a one-shelf visit to a three-building tour. Random placement (and hashing, its deterministic cousin) destroys neighbourhoods.

And looming behind both failures is the fourth building. Recall the measured result from the consistent-hashing lab rather than re-deriving it: assign books by hash(book) % number_of_buildings and going from 10 buildings to 11 moved 99.0% of all keys when only 1/11 ≈ 9% had anything to gain by moving — 163,679 of 181,668 moved keys went sideways between old buildings, achieving nothing. For a cache that was a miss storm; for a library it is re-shelving essentially the entire collection to add one building.

5. THE REAL REQUIREMENT

Strip away the story and four requirements remain. Every real partitioned system is a set of compromises between exactly these:

  1. One computable home. Every record has exactly one authoritative location, and finding it must be cheap (a formula, or one lookup in a small table).
  2. Balanced load, not just balanced data. Even byte-counts per machine is necessary but not sufficient; the requests per second must spread too.
  3. Queries touch few pieces. The common query shapes — point lookup, "everything in this key range" — should hit one piece, or few.
  4. Cheap, safe change. Adding or removing a machine must move roughly 1/n-th of the data (the minimum that accomplishes anything), only to or from the changed machine, throttled, and without ever losing a write or serving from a half-copied piece while the library stays open.

Requirement 4 is where this lab earns its second noun. Requirements 1–3 are about partitioning; requirement 4 is rebalancing.

6. THE KEY IDEA

Two sentences generate everything that follows:

Cut the data into many more pieces than you have machines — choosing the cut to match your queries (by key order, or by hash) — and make the piece, never the record, the unit of ownership and movement.

Then "rebalancing" stops being a re-shuffle of records and becomes two smaller, tractable problems: reassigning whole pieces to machines, and a careful handover protocol that moves one piece while it keeps serving.

Meera's version: put every book on a wheeled shelf of manageable size, keep a card catalog saying which building each shelf currently lives in, and when Building 4 opens, roll a few entire shelves across town — never re-sort individual books, and update the catalog card as each shelf lands.

7. TERMINOLOGY — naming what you've already felt

You have now personally felt every problem these words exist to name. The load-bearing terms, full treatment:

Partition (also called a shard; Kafka says partition, MongoDB says shard+chunk, HBase says region, Cassandra says token range). Plain: one of the pieces the data is cut into — a wheeled shelf. Precise: a contiguous subset of the keyspace that is owned, served, and moved as a unit. Why the term exists: to give the unit of ownership a name that is deliberately NOT "machine" — the whole trick of stage 6 is that partitions and machines are different things, with a many-to-one mapping between them. Example: "partition 7 of 16 holds all keys whose hash lands in slot 7; today node B serves it." Connects to: the consistent-hashing lab's vnodes and hash slots were both partitions in this sense — you've met the mechanism, and now the concept gets its proper name.

Partition key. Plain: the part of the record that decides which shelf it goes on. Precise: the attribute (or attribute tuple) fed to the placement rule; records sharing a partition key are guaranteed co-located. Why: because you choose it, and the choice decides which queries are cheap (one partition) and which are scatter-everything — the single highest-leverage schema decision in a partitioned store. Example: keying orders by order_id makes "fetch order" cheap and "all orders for customer 42" expensive; keying by customer_id flips both. Connects to: the database-landscape lab's "partition key / hot partition" bullets, now with the mechanism attached.

Range partitioning. Plain: cut by key order — the alphabet scheme. Precise: each partition owns a contiguous key interval [lo, hi); boundaries are chosen (and re-chosen) to follow the data. Why: it is the only scheme where a range scan ("all Sharma", "all events between 09:00 and 10:00") touches one or few partitions. Example: HBase and Bigtable partition rows this way; so do CockroachDB and TiDB. Connects to: btree-and-lsm — inside each partition the keys are typically stored sorted anyway, so range partitioning extends the B-tree's sortedness across machines.

Hash partitioning. Plain: cut by dice-roll — but a deterministic dice-roll, so the home is computable. Precise: partition = hash(partition key) mod P (fixed P), or a position on a hash ring. Why: hashing destroys the correlation between "similar keys" and "same partition", which is exactly what kills skew from clustered keys — and exactly what kills range scans. Same coin, both faces. Connects to: the entire consistent-hashing lab lives inside this bullet.

Skew, and its two species you met as Failures A and B: data skew (uneven bytes per partition — fixable by moving boundaries) and load skew (uneven requests per partition — boundary moves don't help when the heat is a single key). A partition drowning in either is a hot partition (the consistent-hashing lab's hot key is the extreme case: one key hotter than everything else combined).

Rebalancing. Plain: moving shelves when buildings change — or when a shelf gets too fat. Precise: changing the partition→node assignment (and sometimes the partition boundaries themselves) and physically migrating the data, while serving. Why the term: the "re" matters — it names an ongoing operational activity, not a one-time setup. A partitioned system that cannot rebalance is a system you must size perfectly on day one, forever.

Partition map (routing table). Plain: the card catalog: shelf → building. Precise: the authoritative table partition → owning node (plus, per entry, an ownership generation number — the epoch, properly earned in stage 10.5), and cached copies of it in clients and nodes — cached copies that go stale whenever rebalancing moves something. Connects to: the consistent-hashing lab's "membership propagation window" — the ring made the map implicit; fixed-partition systems make it an explicit table someone must store and serve.

Scatter/gather. Plain: asking every building and merging the answers. Precise: a query that cannot be routed by partition key is broadcast to all partitions and the results merged. Legal, sometimes necessary, and quietly expensive in a way stage 10.7 will make exact.

Minor terms get one-liners at first use, and everything lands in the glossary (stage 16).

8. TWO MENTAL MODELS

The simple model — the library on wheels. Books on wheeled shelves; shelves assigned to buildings; a card catalog in every clerk's pocket saying which building each shelf is in today. Growth = roll whole shelves to the new building, one at a time, catalog card updated as each lands. A fat shelf gets split into two shelves. A scorching-hot book gets special handling.

The engineering model. Three pieces of state and one protocol:

State Owned by
1 Placement rule — key → partition (a hash formula, or a sorted boundary list for ranges) agreed by everyone, changes rarely (range splits)
2 Partition map — partition → node (+ the epoch, stage 10.5) one authoritative copy (a coordinator or consensus group), many stale caches
3 The data — each partition's records, living on its owner the owning node

…plus the migration protocol (stage 10.5): the choreography that moves one partition's data to a new owner and flips its map entry, under live writes, without violating "exactly one authoritative owner".

The mapping between the models:

Where the analogy breaks (read this — an unbroken analogy is a lie):

  1. A book exists once; a partition exists N times. Real systems replicate every partition (the quorums lab's N copies — often each partition is a whole Raft group). This lab's toy tracks one copy per partition to keep the movement visible; stage 13 says what replication adds back.
  2. Rolling a shelf is atomic; copying 10 GB is not. The shelf is unavailable for an hour, then wholly present. A partition must keep serving during its own move, which is why the handover protocol exists at all — the hard part has no library equivalent.
  3. One catalog; many stale caches. Meera has one catalog and updates it with a pen. A real partition map is cached by every client and node, and the caches learn of a move after it happened. The redirect machinery of stage 10.4 exists purely because of this gap.
  4. Books don't arrive mid-roll. Writes do. The entire drama of stage 10.5 is the writes that arrive while the shelf is on the truck.

10. THE ACTUAL MECHANISM

The key idea said there are two decisions. Take them in order, letting each version break before refining it.

flowchart LR
    K[key] -->|"decision 1: placement rule<br/>(range or hash)"| P[partition]
    P -->|"decision 2: partition map<br/>(assignment + rebalancing)"| N[node]

Keep this two-hop picture: key → partition → node. Decision 1 (key → partition) is about your queries and skew. Decision 2 (partition → node) is about balance and movement. Almost every confusion in this subject comes from mashing the two hops into one.

10.1 Drawing boundaries by key order — range partitioning done right

Fix Failure A properly. The alphabet cut failed because the boundaries were chosen by letter arithmetic; choose them by data instead. Concretely, with 60,000 books and a target of ~7,500 books per partition, walk the sorted author list and cut every 7,500 books:

p1: [A          … Chatterjee)   7,500 books
p2: [Chatterjee … Gupta)        7,500
p3: [Gupta      … Khan)         7,500
p4: [Khan       … Mehta)        7,500
p5: [Mehta      … Rao)          7,500
p6: [Rao        … Sharma)       7,500
p7: [Sharma     … Singh)        7,500   ← two boundary names this close is normal:
p8: [Singh      … Z]            7,500     the data is dense there, so the cuts are too

The boundary list IS the placement rule: to place or find a key, binary-search the sorted boundaries — 3–4 comparisons for thousands of partitions. Range scans still work: "everything by Sharma" is one partition (p7); "authors Rao through Singh" is three adjacent ones.

But data changes. p7 fattens as Sharma-adjacent authors keep publishing. The fix that makes range partitioning viable long-term is dynamic splitting: when a partition exceeds a size threshold, split it at its median key into two partitions; when two adjacent partitions shrink, merge them. The partition count now tracks the data automatically. This is exactly how HBase regions and CockroachDB/TiDB ranges work (typical thresholds: HBase ~10 GB regions, CockroachDB ~512 MB ranges).

One caveat that bites new deployments: a brand-new table is one partition until enough data arrives to trigger splits — so a bulk import hammers a single node while the system slowly splits behind it. The production fix is pre-splitting: declare expected boundaries at creation time (HBase lets you; so does CockroachDB's SPLIT AT).

🔮 Predict: Meera also archives the daily newspaper, keyed by date (2026-08-29, 2026-08-30, ...), range-partitioned. Where do today's writes land? And tomorrow's?

Reveal

Always the last partition. Date keys are ever-increasing, so every new key is greater than every existing key and lands in the final range — today, and tomorrow, and forever. Dynamic splitting does not save you: the moment the last partition splits, all new writes fall into the new last partition. One node takes 100% of the write load while the rest of the cluster stores cold history. This is the sequential-key hotspot, and it is not a rare trap — timestamps, auto-increment IDs, and Kafka-style offsets are the most natural keys in the world, and all three are sequential.

The cure is not a different rebalancer — no assignment of partitions to nodes helps when one partition gets all the writes. The cure is key design:

  • Compound key. Key sensor readings by (sensor_id, timestamp) instead of timestamp. The first component spreads writes across partitions (thousands of sensors → thousands of insertion points); the second keeps each sensor's readings sorted within its partition, so "sensor 17, 09:00–10:00" is still one cheap range scan. Cassandra formalizes this: the partition key (first part) is hashed for placement, the clustering key (second part) is range-ordered within the partition — hash's balance and range's scans, by putting each where it helps.
  • Bucketing/salting. No natural first component? Manufacture one: prefix the key with hash(key) mod 20 (a salt), turning one insertion point into 20. The tax: a range scan must now query all 20 buckets and merge — the scatter cost comes right back, scaled by the salt count. (You met the same remedy as "sub-key splitting" in the distributed-rate-limiting lab.)

10.2 Drawing boundaries by hash — and the mod that is safe

For workloads that never range-scan (session stores, user profiles, caches), hash partitioning is simpler: no boundary list to maintain, balance for free. But do not hash onto nodes — that's the mod n corpse from the consistent-hashing lab. Hash onto partitions:

partition = hash(key) mod P        where P = fixed partition count, P ≫ nodes

The insight that makes this modulo safe where the other one was fatal: P never changes. The thing that made mod n catastrophic was n changing; here the node count can change all it likes — it appears nowhere in the formula. key → partition is frozen forever; only the second hop, partition → node (a small table with P rows), ever changes. This is precisely the Redis Cluster design you met as a contrast in the consistent-hashing lab — CRC16(key) mod 16384 slots — now recognized as one of the two standard answers rather than an oddity.

The price is a judgement call you must make on day one: choose P. Too low, and partitions grow monstrous (a 10-node cluster with P=16 can never spread load more finely than 16 ways, and each partition's eventual size makes every future move slow). Too high, and per-partition overhead (files, metadata, connections, recovery bookkeeping) multiplies. Elasticsearch is the cautionary tale: an index's primary shard count is fixed at creation. That is the standard telling, and it is not quite the whole truth — there is one shape of P-change that costs almost nothing, and one partitioning rule that removes the decision altogether. Stage 10.8 does that arithmetic; for now, treat P as a number to think hard about on day one. Kafka allows increasing a topic's partition count but never decreasing — and increasing it silently breaks the promise that all messages for one key sit in one partition's history (old messages for key k stay in hash(k) mod P_old; new ones go to hash(k) mod P_new).

10.3 Assigning partitions to nodes — the rebalancing strategies

Decision 2. You hold P partitions (16, say) and n nodes (3). Assign each partition a home; when n changes, reassign as little as possible.

v1 — no partitions, hash keys straight onto nodes (mod n). Dead on arrival; the consistent-hashing lab measured the corpse (99.0% of keys moved for a 1-machine addition, 163,679 of 181,668 moves pure waste). Cited, not re-run. Its failure is what makes v2's guarantee visible.

v2 — fixed partitions, moved whole. With P=16 over nodes {A, B, C}:

A: p1 p2 p3 p4 p5 p6         (6 partitions)
B: p7 p8 p9 p10 p11          (5)
C: p12 p13 p14 p15 p16       (5)

Node D joins. Fair share is 16/4 = 4 partitions. D steals whole partitions from the most-loaded nodes until even:

A: p1 p2 p3 p4               (gave p5, p6)
B: p7 p8 p9 p10              (gave p11)
C: p12 p13 p14 p15           (gave p16)
D: p5 p6 p11 p16             (4 stolen partitions)

Count what moved: 4 of 16 partitions = exactly 1/4 of the data, all of it onto the newcomer, none of it sideways. That is the same monotonicity guarantee the hash ring bought with geometry — achieved here with a lookup table and grade-school arithmetic. Node removal is the mirror: the dead node's partitions are dealt out to the survivors, ~1/n each, nobody else disturbed. (Scenario 1 in the visualization runs this exact comparison against mod n; predict the two moved-fractions before pressing run.)

v3 — dynamic partitions (the range-splitting scheme of 10.1): partition count proportional to data, so the unit of movement stays a sane size forever, at the cost of running split/merge machinery. v4 — many small partitions per node (the ring + virtual nodes from the consistent-hashing lab): partition count proportional to nodes. All four in one table:

Strategy Partition count Move on node change Range scans Used by
mod n on nodes — (none) ~everything ☠️ no nobody, on purpose
Fixed P, whole-partition moves fixed at creation 1/n, minimal only if ranges, not hash Riak, Elasticsearch, Couchbase, Redis Cluster, Kafka
Dynamic split/merge ∝ data size 1/n, minimal yes HBase, Bigtable, CockroachDB, TiDB, MongoDB
Ring + vnodes ∝ node count 1/n, statistical no Cassandra, ScyllaDB, Riak

The consistent-hashing lab's closing judgement holds and extends: fixed slots trade coordination for control (someone must store and serve the map — but placement is exact and hand-movable); the ring needs no coordinator but gives only statistical balance. Dynamic splitting sits with the coordinator camp and adds the split machinery in exchange for range scans and right-sized partitions forever.

10.4 Routing — who knows where partition 7 lives today?

A client holds GET user:4711. It can compute the partition (hash or boundary search — the placement rule is public and stable). But which node serves partition 7 today is exactly the thing rebalancing keeps changing. Three standard answers, in increasing client sophistication:

  1. Ask any node. Every node keeps a (possibly stale) copy of the map, learned via gossip; if the wrong node is asked, it forwards or redirects. No extra infrastructure; one extra hop sometimes. (Cassandra: any node acts as coordinator — the quorums lab's coordinator-with-no-authority.)
  2. A routing tier. A dedicated proxy layer holds the map and forwards all requests; clients stay dumb. (MongoDB's mongos; HBase clients consult ZooKeeper + a META table.)
  3. A partition-aware client. The client library caches the map and talks straight to the owner — zero extra hops, and now client caches are the stale copies to manage. (Kafka clients, Redis Cluster clients, most Cassandra drivers do a hybrid.)

Whatever the answer, two hard sub-problems remain:

Stale maps are normal, so wrongness must be cheap. During any rebalance there is a window where caches disagree with reality — the consistent-hashing lab measured that window at 9.5% of the keyspace for a gossiped ring, 90.8% for mod n. The standard machinery makes the stale request self-correcting: the old owner answers with a redirect. Redis Cluster's two redirects make the distinction precise: MOVED = "permanently elsewhere — go there AND update your cached map", ASK = "for this one request go there, but do NOT update your map — the partition is mid-migration and I still own it". A stale client pays one extra round trip, never a wrong answer. (Scenario 6 in the visualization lets you route with a deliberately stale client.)

The authoritative map is itself data that must survive and be agreed on. Cached copies may be sloppy, but somebody must hold the truth, or two nodes can both believe they own p7. Real systems put the map in a consensus store — ZooKeeper (HBase, Kafka's controller before KRaft), etcd (Kubernetes-hosted systems), or a built-in Raft group (CockroachDB's meta ranges, TiDB's Placement Driver). This is where the raft lab plugs in: consensus is not needed for the data path, but the map is exactly the small, rarely-changing, must-agree state consensus was built for. Cassandra is the one big exception — no consensus, the map is gossiped and eventually consistent, which it gets away with because vnode ownership changes are operator-paced and the quorum overlap absorbs small disagreements.

10.5 The move itself — a partition migrates while it serves

Everything so far decided what should move where. Now the part the consistent-hashing lab refused to cover: partition p11 (2 GB, ~9,000 writes/hour arriving) must move from node B to new node D. The library stays open.

Migration v1 — copy, then flip. Obvious plan: D copies p11's data from B (takes, say, 10 minutes at throttled speed); when the copy finishes, the map flips p11 → D; B deletes its copy.

🔮 Predict: During the 10-minute copy, a user updates their address — a write to a key in p11, sent (per the current map) to B. The copy had already passed that key's position when the write landed. After the flip, the user reads their address from D. What do they see?

Reveal

The old address. The write landed on B behind the copy cursor, so it exists only in B's copy — which B deletes after the flip. The write is acknowledged, durable, and gone. Not lost in a crash — lost by protocol design, on a healthy cluster. Any write landing in the already-copied region during those 10 minutes suffers the same fate; at 9,000 writes/hour that's up to ~1,500 candidates. A 10-minute migration silently shredding a fistful of acknowledged writes is how this bug actually presents in the wild: rare, unreproducible, discovered weeks later. (Scenario 4 in the visualization plays this exact trace.)

Migration v2 — copy + catch-up + cutover. The fix mirrors how replicas bootstrap everywhere (you saw the shape in quorums' anti-entropy): the copy alone can never be current, so pair it with a live feed.

stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> SNAPSHOT: D asks B for p11
    SNAPSHOT: SNAPSHOT COPY — D streams a point-in-time copy;<br/>B serves normally AND logs every new p11 write to a delta log
    SNAPSHOT --> CATCHUP: snapshot done
    CATCHUP: CATCH-UP — D drains the delta log;<br/>new writes still land on B, still appended to the log
    CATCHUP --> CUTOVER: delta log nearly empty
    CUTOVER: CUTOVER — B briefly pauses p11 writes,<br/>D drains the last entries, map flips p11 → D (epoch++)
    CUTOVER --> SERVING: flip done
    SERVING: D serves; B forwards stragglers (ASK/MOVED), then deletes
    SERVING --> [*]

Walk it with concrete values. Snapshot: 10 minutes, during which 240 writes arrive at B — all served normally, all appended to the delta log. Catch-up: D drains those 240 while 12 more arrive; drains the 12 while 1 arrives. The log is now nearly empty and shrinking — the drain rate outruns the write rate, so the gap converges (if it doesn't — writes faster than the throttled drain — the migration can never finish; the throttle, not the protocol, is what you tune). Cutover: B pauses p11 writes for the final handful (milliseconds of write-pause for one partition, not an outage), D drains to zero, the map flips. Nothing was lost: every write either made the snapshot, or the delta log, or arrived after the flip at D. That sentence is the invariant the whole choreography exists to guarantee.

Migration v3 — v2 + an epoch on the ownership. One failure survives v2.

🔮 Predict: Right at cutover, B stalls — a long GC pause (the distributed-locking lab's "a process cannot observe its own delays"). The migration machinery times B out, completes the flip, D starts serving. B wakes, unaware, still believing it owns p11 — and a client with a stale map sends it a write. What happens?

Reveal

B accepts it. B's local state says "I own p11" — the wake-up looks like any other moment of normal service. Now B and D are both accepting writes for p11: split-brain on a partition, and the write B accepted will be silently lost when B's copy is deleted (or worse, resurrected if B's copy is ever used for recovery). This is the distributed-locking lab's stale-holder disaster with "lock" replaced by "partition ownership" — and the fix is the same fix: fencing. Give every map entry an epoch (a monotonically increasing ownership generation: p11@e5 means "5th owner"). The flip writes p11 → D, epoch 6 to the authoritative map. Every write request carries the epoch its sender believes; every owner checks it against the authoritative epoch it holds a lease on. B, at epoch 5, either refuses the write outright ("I can no longer prove I'm the owner — my lease expired during the pause") or accepts and is refused downstream. Either way the stale write dies with an error the client retries against the true owner — loud and safe instead of silent and lost. Scenario 5 in the visualization runs both variants.

That's the complete mechanism: epoch-fenced ownership, snapshot + delta handover, redirect-corrected routing. Real systems dress it differently (Cassandra "streaming", HBase region transitions, CockroachDB replica rebalancing via Raft snapshots + config change) but every one of them is recognizably this state machine.

10.6 Two things the migration protocol still gets wrong

Stage 10.5 built the handover up to v3 and stopped at a strong claim: every acknowledged write is at the new owner or still legitimately at the old one. That claim is true and it is narrower than it sounds, because a store does two things. Here are the two holes it leaves, both of which produce plausible wrong answers rather than errors.

Hole 1 — nobody said anything about reads.

While p11 is copying from B to D, D holds a growing but incomplete pile of records. Ask D for a key it has not reached yet and it truthfully answers "I don't have that" — which a client cannot distinguish from "that key does not exist". So during the copy, reads must follow ownership, not copy progress: B answers everything until the map flips, even for keys D already has. Copy progress is D's private business.

Now the flip happens, and the hazard reverses. B still has a complete copy of p11 on disk — it is deleted afterwards, not at the instant of cutover, and usually not for a while (you keep it as insurance until the new owner is verified). A client with a stale map sends a read to B. If B answers from that copy, the client gets a well-formed, plausible, entirely stale record, with no error, no timeout, and no way to tell. And it gets worse over time: B's copy froze at cutover, so the staleness grows without bound.

Note what the v3 epoch fence did and did not do. It checks the epoch on writes — that is why the woken owner's write bounced in stage 10.5. A system that fences writes but serves reads unconditionally has closed the loud failure and left the silent one open. So the rule is symmetric:

The epoch check belongs on every request that touches a partition, not just on the ones that change it. Fencing writes protects the future. Fencing reads protects the truth of the present.

The cheapest correct implementation is to make the demoted owner seal the partition at cutover — refuse everything for p11 and answer MOVED — which is the same machinery as F3's redirect, now understood as a safety mechanism rather than a convenience. Redis Cluster nodes reply MOVED for any slot they do not currently own; Kafka fetch requests carry a leader epoch and a replica rejects a fetch that names a stale one; HBase closes a region on the old RegionServer before the master reassigns it. In each case the deposed owner is made structurally incapable of answering, rather than trusted to be polite.

Watch it happen: viz Tab 2, scenario 7 — untick "old owner seals the shelf at cutover", finish a move, send one write (it routes to D), then press Stale client READS from the old owner. The verdict bar reports a wrong answer with zero writes lost. Tick the box and press the same button: the identical request comes back as a MOVED redirect — the seal doing its job.

Hole 2 — the delete that came back from the dead.

🔮 Predict: The snapshot copies key asha (version 3) to D at 10:00. At 10:04, still mid-copy, the user deletes asha; B removes it and records the change in the delta log. At 10:10 the delta drains and the map flips. Does asha exist on D?

Reveal

It depends on something the protocol never specified: what a delta log entry is. There are two obvious implementations and only one of them is correct.

Implementation A — "the delta log is a list of keys that changed." At drain time, D asks B for the current value of each listed key and stores it. Trace it:

10:00  snapshot: D receives asha = v3
10:04  delete asha   → B removes asha; delta log records the KEY "asha"
10:10  drain: D asks B "what is asha now?" → B: (nothing)
       D reads "nothing" as "no update to apply" → keeps asha = v3
10:11  cutover. D now serves asha = v3.

asha is back. She deleted her account, the deletion was acknowledged, and a routine rebalancing operation resurrected it. No node crashed, no message was lost, nothing timed out.

Implementation B — "the delta log is a list of operations." The entry is DELETE asha @ 10:04, D applies a deletion, and asha stays gone.

The general shape of the bug is one you have already met twice: absence cannot be shipped. The eventual-consistency and CRDT labs both hit it — a replica cannot distinguish "this key was deleted" from "I never heard about this key", so deletion must travel as an explicit record (a tombstone), never as a gap. Migration is replication with a shorter lifespan, so it inherits the same requirement. The same reasoning forces a second one: the delta must preserve per-key order (delete-then-recreate must not land as recreate-then-delete), which is why real delta streams are ordered logs with per-key versions rather than sets of dirty keys.

And this is exactly why the "next lab" suggestion at the end of this doc is change-data-capture: a stream of ordered operations including explicit deletes is not a migration detail, it is a product.

Both holes share a diagnosis worth stating on its own, because it generalizes past this lab: a protocol is only correct with respect to the operations it was checked against. v3 was verified against writes and was silent about reads; the delta was specified as "changes" and was silent about the shape of a change. Neither silence produced an error message. When you review a handover design — anyone's — the useful question is not "does it lose writes" but "what is the complete list of things a client can ask, and which of them did we check?"

10.7 Secondary indexes — the query that has no partition key

One mechanism left, because real queries aren't all by primary key: "all books tagged 'poetry'" — and tag is not the partition key, so no placement rule routes it. Two designs, trading read cost against write cost:

  • Local index (document-partitioned): each partition indexes its own records. Writes stay single-partition (record + its index entries co-live — cheap, atomic). Reads by tag must scatter/gather all P partitions, because matching records could be anywhere.
  • Global index (term-partitioned): the index is itself a partitioned structure, partitioned by the indexed term — all of tag=poetry lives in one index partition. Reads route to one partition. But now a single record write touches its data partition plus one index partition per indexed term — a multi-partition write, which (per the distributed-transactions lab's price list) is either slow or asynchronous. DynamoDB chose async: its Global Secondary Indexes lag the base table by an eventually-consistent window (the eventual-consistency lab's exact subject matter).

The scatter cost deserves its number, because it compounds in a way averages hide. Suppose each partition answers fast except for its slowest 1% of requests (a p99 tail — GC, compaction, a queue blip; the quorums lab measured this shape). A scatter query across 100 partitions must wait for all 100 answers, so it hits the tail if any partition does:

P(all 100 fast) = 0.99^100 ≈ 36.6%  →  ~63% of scatter queries eat tail latency

One machine's 1% problem becomes the fleet's 63% problem. This tail latency amplification is why partition-count decisions and scatter-query budgets are made together, and why global indexes exist despite their write cost.

10.8 The number you got wrong three years ago — changing P

Stage 10.2 sold fixed partitions on one promise: P never changes, so the mod is safe. Then it charged you for that promise — "P is a forever decision". Sooner or later everyone tests whether "forever" is literally true. It usually happens like this: you chose P = 16 when the table was 40 GB across three machines. Three years later it is 2 TB across twenty machines, each partition is 125 GB, and every rebalancing move now takes hours because the unit of movement is enormous. You do not have a balance problem. You have a granularity problem, and the only cure is more pieces.

So: can you change P after all? The answer is the most useful piece of arithmetic in this lab, and it is not "no". It is "only if you double".

🔮 Predict: P goes from 16 to 32. Every key's shelf is computed as hash(key) mod P, so changing P re-addresses a large share of the data. Of 420 keys, how many bytes actually have to travel across the network to another machine?

Reveal

None. Measured in the visualization's Tab 3 on its 420-key sample: 48.8% of keys land on a different partition — and 0% change machine.

The reason is one line of modular arithmetic. If hash(k) mod 16 = 3, then hash(k) mod 32 can only be 3 or 19 — because h mod 32 is either h mod 16 or h mod 16 + 16, and nothing else is possible. So partition 3 does not scatter when P doubles; it cleanly cuts in two, into new partitions 3 and 19, and no key from any other partition can arrive in either. Every byte of the new partition 19 is already sitting on the machine that held partition 3. The split is a local file operation plus a one-row map edit — the newborn partition is created where its data already lives.

Elasticsearch's _split API is exactly this, which is why it refuses any target shard count that is not a multiple of the current one, and why it can implement a split by hard-linking the existing segment files and then dropping the documents that no longer belong, rather than copying anything. (It still makes a new index and requires writes to be blocked while it runs — the mechanism is cheap, the operation is not free.)

One condition is hiding in that "0%", and it is not arithmetic. The split is local only if the placer creates the child on the machine that holds the parent. A placer that re-derives placement from a formula on the partition id instead — a perfectly reasonable-looking partition mod nodes — sends the children elsewhere and turns the free split back into a full redistribution. (Exercise 5.3 works this out on eight keys by hand: 0 crossings when children stay home, 4 of 8 when they are re-dealt.) This is why real systems ship splitting and rebalancing as two separate commands: split in place now, rebalance later if you want to. The two hops again.

Now the other direction — the number in between. You do not want 32 partitions, you want 24. Same 420 keys, P from 16 to 24: 68.8% of keys change partition, and 68.8% must cross the network (measured, Tab 3, scenario 9). Nothing cleanly cuts in half. h mod 16 tells you nothing useful about h mod 24: keys sharing an old partition are spread over several new ones — the pattern only repeats every lcm(16, 24) = 48 — so the new partitions cannot be carved out of the old ones locally, and the data has to be shipped.

That is the honest content of "P is a forever decision": P is doublable, and otherwise frozen. Halving is the mirror image (Elasticsearch's _shrink requires the target to be a factor of the source, for the same reason read backwards). Any other target is a full redistribution wearing the costume of a config change.

The escape hatch: partition by hash RANGE, not by hash remainder. Here is the move that dissolves the problem instead of managing it. Keep hashing the key — you still want hash's balance — but instead of taking a remainder, give each partition a slice of the hash space:

    hash space: 0 ────────────────────────────────────── 2³²
                [   h0   ][   h1   ][   h2   ][   h3   ]  ← 4 partitions
    key k  →  hash(k) = 0x6A2F…  →  falls inside h1 → partition h1

Look at what vanished from the rule: P. The lookup is "which slice contains this hash", and no partition's boundaries are computed from the total count. So partition h1 can be cut at its own midpoint any afternoon, into h1-left and h1-right, and no other partition is affected at all — measured in Tab 3 (scenario 10): splitting the fattest partition re-addressed 6% of keys (the ones inside the partition being cut) and moved 0% across the network, with P going from 16 to 17. Not a multiple. Not a redistribution. Just a boundary list getting one entry longer.

You have seen both halves of this before and can now see they were the same idea twice: this is stage 10.1's range partitioning with auto-split, applied to the hash of the key rather than the key itself. It buys splittability forever and hash's balance, and it forfeits exactly one thing — range scans on the original key, which the hash destroyed anyway.

That completes the table from 10.3 with the column nobody puts in it:

Rule key → partition Can P change later? Range scans
hash mod n on nodes changes whenever a node joins ☠️ n/a — it is the bug no
hash mod P on partitions frozen doubling only (cheap: local split); any other target = full redistribution no
hash range partitions frozen any partition, any time (cheap: local split) no
key range partitions frozen any partition, any time yes

So why does anyone still use mod P? Because a remainder needs no state. A hash-range scheme has a boundary list that must be stored, agreed on, and kept current at every client — the map is bigger and it changes shape, not just contents. mod P needs no boundary list at all: a client with only the number P can compute hop 1 offline forever. Kafka and Redis Cluster take that deal deliberately, and Redis takes it to its conclusion — 16,384 slots, frozen for all time. Read in the light of this section, that number stops being an oddity and becomes the actual solution to resharding: pick P so absurdly large that no cluster will ever outgrow the granularity, and you never need to change it. The cost is paid up front and forever (a 16,384-row map, always), instead of unpredictably later.

How to choose P, if you must choose one. Work backwards from the unit of movement, not from today's node count:

  1. Pick a target partition size — the size at which moving one is comfortable. Real systems land between 512 MB and 10 GB (CockroachDB ~512 MB, HBase ~10 GB); call it 5 GB.
  2. Estimate the data volume at the furthest horizon you would bet on — not today's, and not infinity. Say 2 TB.
  3. P ≈ 2 TB / 5 GB ≈ 400. Round to a power of two — 512 — because powers of two are the only numbers you can later double and halve cheaply.
  4. Sanity-check the floor: P must be at least a small multiple of the largest node count you will run (you cannot balance 20 nodes with 16 partitions), and the ceiling: P files, P memtables, P recovery jobs, P entries of fan-out on every scatter query.

If that sizing exercise makes you uncomfortable — you genuinely do not know the horizon — that discomfort is the argument for hash-range partitions or for Redis's absurd constant. Both remove the guess.

One correction to a promise made earlier. Stage 10.2 said Kafka lets you increase a topic's partition count but never decrease it, and that increasing breaks the "all messages for key k live in one partition" property. Both true — and note where Kafka sits in this section's arithmetic: increasing partitions in Kafka moves no bytes at all (existing messages stay exactly where they are; only future producer routing changes). Kafka's resharding is free in bandwidth and expensive in semantics. Elasticsearch's _split is the opposite trade — semantics preserved exactly, at the cost of a new index and a write freeze. There is no scheme where changing P is free in both.

10.9 Where a partition is allowed to live — placement constraints

Every rebalancing rule so far has had one goal: evenness. Give each node about the same number of partitions, about the same number of bytes, about the same heat. That is the only goal a toy needs. It is never the first goal of a real cluster, and the reason is a sentence you can feel without any distributed-systems vocabulary at all:

Three copies of your data in three machines on the same rack is not three copies. It is one copy, plus two spares that share a power supply, a top-of-rack switch, and a maintenance window.

Replication buys you survival of independent failures. Two machines in one rack do not fail independently — that is what the consistent-hashing lab named a failure domain, and it has been sitting in your vocabulary without a job since. Here is its job: the placer must not merely spread partitions, it must spread each partition's copies across failure domains. And once the placer takes orders about where copies may go, other people join the queue with orders of their own:

  • Rack / zone / region diversity — no two copies of a partition in one rack; ideally one copy per availability zone.
  • Data residency — an EU customer's rows must be stored on machines in the EU. Not a preference; a legal constraint, and it depends on the content of the partition, not on load at all.
  • Latency locality — put the partitions serving Tokyo users on the Tokyo nodes, so the common read is 5 ms and not 150 ms.
  • Heterogeneous hardware — the new machines have 4× the disk; they should hold 4× the partitions (the consistent-hashing lab's weight, which was a count of ring positions there and is a capacity number here).

Balance has quietly changed from the objective into the tie-breaker. The placer is now a constraint solver: first the set of legal homes, then the evenest choice within it.

🔮 Predict: Three machines, all in rack r1. Six partitions, three copies each. You switch on the rule "no two copies of a partition in the same rack". What does the rebalancer do?

Reveal

It places nothing, and says nothing loudly. All six partitions become unplaceable at the third copy — there is no second rack to put it in, let alone a third. (Tab 4, scenario 12: 6 of 6 unplaceable with one rack; still 6 of 6 with two racks, though now each partition gets 2 of its 3 copies — under-replicated, which looks half-fine on a dashboard; 0 of 6 unplaceable once a third rack exists.)

The thing worth carrying away is the shape of the failure, not the arithmetic. A constrained placer that cannot satisfy its rules does not compromise, does not pick the least-bad option, and does not page anyone. It stops moving. And a rebalancer that is not moving looks exactly like a rebalancer that has nothing to do — a calm cluster, flat graphs, no errors. The only difference is a status field somebody has to go and read.

This is precisely what an Elasticsearch YELLOW cluster with UNASSIGNED shards is: not a bug, not a slow copy, but a constraint solver reporting no legal solution — usually for cluster.routing.allocation.awareness (rack rules) or a disk watermark. The cluster is telling you it refuses to lie about your redundancy. _cluster/allocation/explain exists solely because "why is nothing happening?" is the standard question this failure produces.

The rule interacts with the content of a partition, too. Turn on data residency with no European machines running and exactly the European partitions freeze while the rest of the cluster rebalances happily around them (Tab 4: toggle "EU shelves may only live in EU buildings" with no EU node — half the library stalls). A partial outage on a legal boundary, invisible in any cluster-wide average.

Real names for all of this, so the docs are readable: Cassandra's NetworkTopologyStrategy (replicas per datacenter, rack-aware within one), Elasticsearch's shard allocation awareness plus forced awareness, CockroachDB's zone configurations and partition-level replica constraints (the mechanism behind its data-domiciling feature), MongoDB's zone sharding (tag a shard-key range to a zone), Kafka's rack-aware replica assignment, and Kubernetes' pod topology spread constraints — the same idea for stateless pods, which is a good hint that this is a general scheduling problem that partition placement happens to be an instance of.

The tradeoff, stated plainly. Every constraint you add removes degrees of freedom from the placer. With enough constraints there is no legal placement at all, and the system's correct response — refusing to place — is indistinguishable from idleness. So constraints are worth exactly what they protect: rack diversity earns its cost the first time a rack dies; a residency rule earns it the first time a regulator asks. A locality preference that is enforced as a hard rule and only worth 20 ms of latency does not — and that distinction, hard constraint versus soft preference, is one real systems make explicit (Elasticsearch's include/require/exclude, Cassandra's strategy-per-keyspace, Kubernetes' requiredDuringScheduling versus preferredDuringScheduling). Get it wrong in the "hard" direction and your cluster stops rebalancing at 3 a.m. for a reason that is entirely your own.

CHECKPOINT — intermediate model complete. You can now pick boundaries (range vs hash vs compound), pick a rebalancing strategy (fixed / dynamic / vnodes), route with stale maps, narrate the three-phase migration with its invariant — and, from the three sections just added, say what a handover owes reads and deletes, price a change to the partition count before anyone runs it, and state where a partition is allowed to live and what happens when nowhere is. Next: the ways all of this fails in production, and what is actually guaranteed.

11. FAILURE SCENARIOS

Each one: how it arises → where to watch it → what happens and why → what survives, what's lost → how production handles it. Read F7–F9 with one question in mind: how would you find out this was happening? Those three produce no error, no latency spike and no failed request.

F1 — The celebrity key (load skew that no scheme fixes). One key (viral post, flash-sale SKU, the Sharma novel) out-heats the rest of the keyspace combined. Watch: viz Tab 1, workload "celebrity", any scheme — the owning partition glows red; add nodes and press rebalance: the hot partition just moves house, still glowing (Scenario 3). Why: every scheme agrees one key has one home; that agreement is load-bearing (it's what makes reads findable), so no placement change helps. Response: split the key itself — append a salt to writes (sharma-novel#0..19), scatter the 20 sub-keys, merge on read; or absorb reads in a cache layer (the caching lab). Guarantee kept: every sub-key still findable. Lost: single-key atomicity across the sub-keys, read simplicity. Production: DynamoDB's throttling made "hot partition" a household word (its adaptive capacity now auto-shifts throughput toward hot partitions, and it can split a partition by load); Twitter/X famously special-cases celebrity accounts.

F2 — Sequential keys (the write hotspot you designed in). Timestamp or auto-increment partition key + range partitioning → all writes land on the last partition forever (stage 10.1's checkpoint). Watch: viz Tab 1, range scheme + "timestamps" workload (Scenario 2) — one partition's write heat pins while the cluster idles. Why: ever-increasing keys have a single insertion frontier. Response: compound key or bucketing (10.1); or accept it deliberately for append-mostly history where the frontier node is sized for the whole write load and old partitions serve reads (this is roughly Kafka's life: the head of each partition takes all appends — Kafka spreads load by having many partitions via key hashing, not by range). Production: HBase's docs warn about "monotonically increasing row keys" on page one; Bigtable's schema guide leads with "field promotion" (their name for the compound-key fix).

F3 — The stale map that never learns. A client caches p7 → B, p7 moves to D. Watch: viz Tab 2's stale client (Scenario 6). Why it's benign when done right: B answers MOVED → client retries at D and repairs its cache — one wasted round trip. How it goes wrong: B is dead (can't redirect — client must refetch the whole map from the authority: the map service just became a dependency of the data path), or the redirect chain itself is stale (bounded redirect-hops + full-map refresh is the standard escape). Guarantee kept: no wrong answers, ever — redirects or errors, never silently stale routing to a node that answers with old data it shouldn't own (that protection is F6's epoch). Production: Redis Cluster MOVED/ASK; Kafka clients refetch metadata on NotLeaderForPartition; MongoDB's mongos versioned chunk maps.

F4 — The naive handover (acknowledged writes shredded). Copy-then-flip migration loses every write that lands behind the copy cursor (10.5 v1). Watch: viz Tab 2, protocol "naive", press write mid-snapshot (Scenario 4) — the verdict bar turns red naming the lost key. Why: the snapshot is a past; only a delta feed makes the destination current. Response: v2's catch-up log. Guarantee lost in v1: durability of acknowledged writes — the worst guarantee to lose, because nothing errors. Production: every serious system ships the v2 shape (Cassandra streaming + hints, CockroachDB Raft snapshot + log catch-up, MongoDB chunk migration's "catch up on mods" phase — its docs narrate literally this state machine).

F5 — The rebalance storm (the cure at scale becomes the disease). Wire an automatic rebalancer to a failure detector and let both react fast. Node C pauses (GC, 90 seconds — the gossip lab's slow-vs-dead undecidability). The detector declares C dead; the rebalancer starts re-replicating/moving C's 40 partitions; the copy traffic loads B and D, whose heartbeats now lag; the detector suspects them... Watch: this one is a hand-trace (stage 11's box), not a viz scenario — the mechanism is the gossip lab's detector meltdown (100% false-DEAD at 90% packet loss) with a payload attached: every false verdict now triggers terabytes of movement, and the movement manufactures the next false verdict. Why: the feedback loop — movement causes load causes silence causes verdicts causes movement. Response: rate-limit the rebalancer (max concurrent moves, bandwidth caps), require sustained evidence before moving data (minutes, not seconds — moving data is expensive; a dead node's partitions served by replicas can wait), and keep a human approval gate for large-scale movement. This is why DDIA flatly recommends rebalancing not be fully automatic. Production: Cassandra separates "down" (route around, cheap, fast) from "removed" (stream data, operator-invoked removenode); Elasticsearch has cluster.routing.allocation throttles and a delayed_timeout precisely so a rebooting node doesn't trigger shard shuffling; Kafka's Cruise Control caps concurrent moves.

F6 — Two owners (split-brain on a partition). The cutover races a paused old owner (10.5 v3's checkpoint). Watch: viz Tab 2, fencing OFF, "pause old owner at cutover" (Scenario 5) — both nodes accept writes for p11; flip fencing ON and rerun: the stale write bounces with an epoch error. Why: a node's belief about its own ownership is a cached fact like any other, and it goes stale like any other; only an epoch check at write time makes staleness detectable. Guarantee: with epochs, at most one effect lineage per partition per epoch — the distributed-locking lab's exact formulation, because it is the exact mechanism (ownership = a lease; epoch = the fencing token; the partition = the resource). Production: HBase fences via ZooKeeper region ownership + WAL fencing; Kafka partitions have leader epochs checked on every fetch/produce; CockroachDB replica ownership rides Raft terms, which are epochs by construction.

F7 — The rule with no solution (the rebalancer that goes quiet). Placement constraints that cannot all be satisfied: three copies required, two racks available, rack-diversity enforced; or EU-residency enforced with every EU machine down for maintenance. Watch: viz Tab 4, scenario 12 — switch on the rack rule with all buildings in one rack and read the status column. Why: a constrained placer treats rules as a hard filter and balance only as a tie-break, so with an empty legal set it does the correct thing and places nothing. How it presents: as nothing happening. No error rate, no latency spike, no moves — a cluster that looks calm and is quietly running below its promised redundancy. Guarantee kept: the constraint itself (the system never lies about your rack diversity or your residency). Lost: durability margin, silently — you believe you have three copies and you have two. Production: Elasticsearch UNASSIGNED shards and a YELLOW cluster, with _cluster/allocation/explain existing purely to answer "why is nothing happening?"; Cassandra refusing to satisfy a NetworkTopologyStrategy that names a datacenter with too few racks; Kubernetes pods stuck Pending on unsatisfiable topology spread constraints. The monitoring lesson is specific: alarm on under-replicated partitions, not on rebalancing activity — the dangerous state is the one with no activity at all.

F8 — The stale read from the deposed owner. After cutover, the old owner still holds a full, frozen copy of the partition on disk; a client with a stale map reads from it. Watch: viz Tab 2, scenario 7 — with fencing ON and sealing OFF, the stale write bounces loudly while the stale read is answered with old data and no complaint. Why: v3's epoch check was specified for writes, and reads were never mentioned. What the client sees: a well-formed, plausible record with no error and no timeout, growing staler every minute, because the old copy stopped advancing at the flip. Guarantee kept: nothing is corrupted — the true state is intact at the new owner. Lost: the read's freshness guarantee entirely, and worse, its detectability. Response: seal the partition at cutover — the demoted owner refuses every request for it and answers MOVED, so staleness becomes a redirect rather than an answer. Production: Redis Cluster answers MOVED for any slot it does not own; Kafka fetches carry a leader epoch and a replica rejects a stale one; HBase closes the region before reassignment. Note the pattern: the deposed owner is made structurally unable to answer rather than trusted to behave.

F9 — The resurrected delete. A key deleted during the snapshot phase comes back alive at the new owner. Watch: stage 10.6's hole 2 — the hand trace with asha at 10:00/10:04/10:10. Why: the delta log recorded which keys changed rather than what happened to them, and reading a deleted key back yields nothing, which is indistinguishable from "no update". Guarantee lost: durability of an acknowledged delete — the one write whose loss is a compliance incident rather than an inconvenience (the user asked to be forgotten and was not). Response: deltas are ordered logs of operations with explicit tombstones and per-key versions, never sets of dirty keys. Why it is familiar: the eventual-consistency and CRDT labs established that absence cannot be shipped; migration is short-lived replication and inherits the rule. Production: every replication and CDC stream carries explicit delete events for exactly this reason — and tombstone lifetime becomes its own operational concern (Cassandra's gc_grace_seconds exists so a tombstone outlives the repair window that could otherwise resurrect the row).

12. CORRECTNESS — what is actually guaranteed

The invariants a correct partitioned store maintains:

  1. Complete coverage, no overlap. Every possible key maps to exactly one partition (the boundary list has no gaps; the hash's range is fully covered). Trivial-sounding; real bugs live here (the consistent-hashing lab's 32-bit-hash-on-64-bit-ring bug put 100% of keys on one node silently).
  2. One authoritative owner per partition per epoch. Enforced by the epoch/fencing machinery, not by hope (F6).
  3. Handover completeness. A new owner serves only after snapshot + delta drain — every acknowledged operation, deletes included, is at the new owner or still legitimately at the old one, never in neither (10.5's invariant, corrected by 10.6: a delete shipped as an absence is a delete lost, F9).
  4. Routing safety. A stale map costs extra hops, never wrong answers — provided the epoch check guards reads as well as writes. Fence only writes and this invariant fails silently at the deposed owner (10.6's hole 1, F8): the standard implementation is that a demoted owner seals the partition and answers MOVED to everything.
  5. Rules before balance. A copy is placed only where the placement constraints allow; balance decides among the legal options and never overrules them. When the legal set is empty the placer stops rather than compromising — correct, and indistinguishable from idle (10.9, F7).

What is explicitly NOT guaranteed:

  • Perfect balance. Fixed partitions balance to the granularity of one partition; vnodes balance statistically (the consistent-hashing lab measured 1.06× at 100 vnodes, never 1.00×); load balance is chased, not proven — a hot key defeats all of it (F1).
  • Cross-partition atomicity. Partitioning is precisely the thing that makes a multi-key write span machines; nothing in THIS lab restores all-or-nothing across partitions — that bill is the distributed-transactions lab's whole subject (2PC/Percolator), and its cost is why partition-key design tries so hard to keep transactional neighbours co-located.
  • Global index freshness when indexes are async (10.7) — an eventually-consistent window, with everything the eventual-consistency lab says about un-promised behavior.
  • Continuous perfect availability during rebalancing. The cutover pause is real (milliseconds per partition, done right); done wrong it's the outage.
  • That a legal placement exists at all. Constraints are hard filters, and a set of them can be jointly unsatisfiable (three copies, two racks). The system's response is silence, not an error (F7) — which is why the alarm belongs on under-replicated partitions, never on rebalancing activity.
  • That the partition count you chose is revisable. Under hash mod P, only doubling and halving are cheap; every other target is a full redistribution (10.8). Hash-range partitions give the revisability back, at the cost of carrying a boundary list.

13. PRODUCTION — the same machine in every costume

The toy → real chain. This lab's model (one copy per partition, one map, explicit migrations) maps onto real systems with one big addition: every partition is itself replicated — usually each partition is a replication group (leader + followers, or a Raft group). That composition resolves cleanly: rebalancing moves replicas, not the partition-in-the-abstract — adding a node means some partitions add a replica there and drop one elsewhere (the snapshot+catch-up protocol IS how a new replica bootstraps), and "moving" a leader is often just a leadership transfer with zero data motion, the cheapest rebalancing there is. The quorums lab's machinery lives inside each partition; this lab's machinery decides where each partition's replicas live. And once there are several replicas to place, stage 10.9's constraints stop being optional: "three copies" only means something if the three cannot die together, so every production placer is rack- and zone-aware before it is balance-aware.

How each of them lets you change P, since that is the decision you cannot undo: Kafka increases only, moving no bytes but breaking per-key history; Elasticsearch _splits to a multiple and _shrinks to a factor (cheap, via hard-linked segments, on a write-blocked index) and otherwise wants a reindex; Redis Cluster never changes its 16,384 and moves slots instead; HBase, Bigtable, CockroachDB, TiDB, MongoDB and DynamoDB split partitions individually and continuously, so "choosing P" is not a decision anyone makes there. Stage 10.8 is the arithmetic behind that table.

Two of these deserve a paragraph because they extend the concept:

  • Kafka's consumer groups rebalance consumption, not data. The partitions don't move; the assignment of partitions to consumers does, on every membership change — and the original ("eager") protocol was stop-the-world: all consumers drop all partitions, wait, get reassigned. A deploy of 50 consumers = 50 rebalances = minutes of paused consumption. Cooperative sticky rebalancing (KIP-429) made it incremental — only the partitions actually changing hands stop. Same lab, different resource: assignment churn is a cost even when zero bytes move.
  • DynamoDB's adaptive capacity is F1 institutionalized: it splits partitions by observed heat, not just size — load-based splitting as a managed service, with the hot-key ceiling still yours to design around (their docs still open with "distribute your partition keys").

What the educational version omitted, honestly listed: replication inside each partition (quorums/raft labs — the biggest omission, addressed above); cross-partition transactions (distributed-transactions lab); how the map's consensus store itself scales; multi-tenant fairness and per-partition quotas (one customer's partition starving its neighbours is F1 with a billing relationship attached); backpressure during migrations; and split hysteresis (real systems split at 10 GB but merge at 2 GB, not 5 — a gap that prevents flapping at the threshold). Geo-placement and rack awareness were on this list and are now stage 10.9; the toy still models them as a single from-scratch solve, where a real placer computes the minimal set of moves from the current placement to a legal one — the difference between knowing the answer and getting there without a storm (F5).

The operator's dashboard — what on-call actually watches, each metric traceable to a stage of this lab:

  • Per-partition size histogram (data skew, F2 creep, jumbo partitions — "jumbo" being MongoDB's official word for a chunk too fat to migrate).
  • Per-partition request rate / heat map (load skew, F1 — the ratio busiest:median is the number to alarm on, not any absolute).
  • Rebalance in flight: moves running, bytes queued, ETA, and the throttle utilization — a migration that can't outrun the write rate never converges (10.5's drain race).
  • Stale-routing rate (MOVED/redirect counts — a spike means the map churned faster than caches heal; F3).
  • Epoch-rejection count (should be ~0; a burst = something stale is writing — F6 caught in the act, which is the fence working).
  • Under-replicated / unplaceable partition count (F7) — the one metric whose healthy value is a hard zero, and the only warning you get that the placer has quietly given up. Alarm on this, never on "rebalancing is idle", because idle is exactly what the failure looks like.
  • Requests refused for un-owned partitions (F8) — the seal working. Like the epoch-rejection count, a burst is informative rather than alarming; a permanent zero on a cluster that rebalances is suspicious, because it may mean nothing is checking.
  • The F5 tripwire: movement triggered per hour + detector verdict rate on one graph — the storm announces itself as both climbing together.

16. GLOSSARY

In order of first appearance:

  • Partition (shard / region / chunk / token range) — a contiguous piece of the keyspace, owned and moved as a unit.
  • Data skew — uneven bytes per partition; fixed by moving boundaries.
  • Load skew — uneven requests per partition; boundary moves don't fix the single-hot-key case.
  • Hot partition — the partition currently drowning (in either skew).
  • Partition key — the record attribute that determines placement; the highest-leverage schema choice.
  • Range partitioning — boundaries by key order; buys range scans, risks sequential hotspots.
  • Hash partitioning — boundaries by hash; buys balance, forfeits range scans.
  • Rebalancing — changing partition→node assignment (and sometimes boundaries) with data migration, live.
  • Partition map / routing table — authoritative partition → node (+epoch) table, plus its many stale caches.
  • Scatter/gather — broadcasting a query to all partitions and merging results.
  • Dynamic splitting — auto-split a partition at a size/load threshold at its median key; auto-merge shrunken neighbours.
  • Pre-splitting — declaring partition boundaries at table creation so bulk loads don't hammer one node.
  • Sequential-key hotspot — ever-increasing keys (timestamps, auto-increment) pinning all writes to the last range partition.
  • Compound key — (partition component, ordering component); hash-spread across partitions, range-ordered within. Cassandra: partition key + clustering key.
  • Salting / bucketing — manufacturing a spreading prefix (hash mod B) for a hot or sequential key; costs B-way scatter on reads.
  • Fixed partitions — hash(key) mod P with P frozen forever; only the small partition→node table ever changes.
  • Monotonicity (recalled from consistent-hashing) — node changes move keys only to/from the changed node, never sideways.
  • Routing tier — a proxy layer that owns map lookups so clients stay dumb (mongos).
  • Partition-aware client — a client library caching the map and talking straight to owners.
  • MOVED / ASK — Redis Cluster's redirects: permanently-moved-update-your-map vs mid-migration-one-request-only.
  • Migration — the snapshot → catch-up → cutover protocol moving one partition between nodes under live writes.
  • Delta log (catch-up log) — writes accepted by the old owner during the snapshot, replayed to the new owner; the thing whose absence loses acknowledged writes.
  • Cutover — the brief write-pause + final drain + map flip ending a migration.
  • Ownership epoch — monotonically increasing generation number per partition; the fencing token (distributed-locking lab) worn by rebalancing.
  • Split-brain (partition ownership) — two nodes concurrently believing they own one partition; prevented by epoch checks, not by timeouts.
  • Tombstone — an explicit "this was deleted" record; deletion must travel as a record because absence cannot be shipped (a gap is indistinguishable from never-heard-of-it).
  • Sealing a partition — the demoted owner refuses every request for a partition at cutover and answers MOVED, making stale reads structurally impossible rather than merely impolite.
  • Read fencing — applying the ownership-epoch check to reads, not only writes; without it a stale map yields a plausible stale answer instead of a redirect.
  • Rebalance storm — the movement→load→false-verdict→more-movement feedback loop; why rebalancers are throttled and gated.
  • Local (document-partitioned) secondary index — each partition indexes its own records; cheap writes, scatter reads.
  • Global (term-partitioned) secondary index — index partitioned by indexed value; one-hop reads, multi-partition (often async) writes.
  • Tail latency amplification — a scatter query is as slow as its slowest partition: per-partition 1% tails become fleet-level 63% at 100-way fan-out.
  • Hash-range partitioning — each partition owns a slice of the hash space rather than a remainder class; keeps hash's balance and restores the ability to split one partition at a time.
  • Partition doubling — the one cheap change to a fixed P: h mod 2P is either h mod P or h mod P + P, so every partition cuts locally in two and no bytes cross the network.
  • _split / _shrink — Elasticsearch's names for doubling-style and halving-style resharding; the multiple/factor requirement in their APIs is this arithmetic showing through.
  • Failure domain (recalled from consistent-hashing) — a set of machines that can fail together (rack, zone, power feed); replicas of a partition must be spread across them or "three copies" is a fiction.
  • Placement constraint — a hard rule about where a partition's copies may live (rack diversity, zone, residency); filters the legal set before balance chooses within it.
  • Hard constraint vs soft preference — whether an unsatisfiable rule blocks placement or merely loses a tie; the distinction real systems make explicit (Elasticsearch require/prefer, Kubernetes required/preferred).
  • Data residency — a legal requirement that certain records be stored in a certain jurisdiction; placement driven by the content of a partition rather than by load.
  • Shard allocation awareness — Elasticsearch's name for rack/zone-aware placement; Cassandra's equivalent is NetworkTopologyStrategy, CockroachDB's is zone configurations, MongoDB's is zone sharding.
  • Under-replicated / UNASSIGNED — a partition with fewer legal copies than required, or none; the state a constrained placer enters instead of breaking a rule, and the only thing worth alarming on when a rebalancer goes quiet.
  • Jumbo partition — one too fat to migrate (MongoDB's term); the failure mode of unsplittable growth.
  • Leader epoch (Kafka) / Raft term — production names for the ownership epoch.
  • Consumer group rebalance — Kafka reassigning partition consumption (not data) across consumers; eager (stop-the-world) vs cooperative (incremental).
  • Adaptive capacity — DynamoDB's load-based splitting and throughput shifting; F1 management as a service.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 One machine eventually can't hold all the data (disk) or serve all the traffic (CPU/network) — usually traffic breaks first. Replication makes more copies of everything: every replica still stores the full dataset and still receives every write, so it multiplies read capacity and survivability but does nothing for total size or write throughput. Splitting gives each machine a different piece, so both storage and write load divide across the fleet. They're orthogonal and composed: real systems replicate each piece.

1.2 At pieces == machines, "rebalance" and "re-cut the data" are the same operation — to change the machine count you must redraw boundaries through the records themselves, which is mod n's disease (the consistent-hashing lab measured 99% of keys moving). With many pieces per machine, the piece-to-key mapping is frozen; only the small piece-to-machine table changes, so growth means relocating whole pieces — a table edit plus bulk copies — and the moved fraction drops to the theoretical minimum of ~1/n. The surplus of pieces is also the balance granularity: you can't spread 3 pieces evenly over 4 machines.

1.3 Data skew: uneven bytes — the S-surname shelf holds 3× the books of the X shelf. Load skew: uneven requests — one bestseller gets more borrows than the rest of the library combined. Boundary moves (split the S shelf, merge quiet neighbours) fix data skew and the multi-key kind of load skew; they cannot fix a single hot key, because every scheme gives one key one home. That needs key-level surgery (salting) or a cache in front.

1.4 Both halves are true of different systems, and the question tells you which. They are right that under hash mod P the formula freezes P: every client computes hash(key) mod P offline, so changing P re-addresses data everywhere at once. They are wrong that this makes P unchangeable — doubling (and halving) is cheap, because h mod 2P is either h mod P or h mod P + P, so each partition cuts locally in two with nothing crossing the network. And they are wrong in a second, bigger way if their system partitions by hash range rather than by remainder: then P is not in the rule at all and any partition can be split at any time. The question to ask: "is our rule a remainder or a range?" Everything else follows from the answer.

1.5 Three copies protect you against independent accidents — one disk dies, one machine reboots, one process crashes. Put all three copies in the same rack and they stop being independent: one power supply, one network switch, one clumsy afternoon of maintenance takes all three at once. So you have three copies of the data and one copy of the risk. A rack is the unit of "things that fail together", which is why serious systems refuse to place copies without knowing which rack each machine is in. (In cloud terms the same argument climbs a level: availability zone, then region.)

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 P=8 over 3 nodes: best split is 3/3/2, so best balance = (8/3)/(8/3)… in shelf counts the busiest holds 3 shelves against a fair share of 8/3 ≈ 2.67 → 1.125×. P=24 over 3: 8/8/8 → 1.00×, and over 4 nodes 6/6/6/6 → still 1.00×. Raising P buys balance granularity and smaller (cheaper, faster) moves; it costs per-partition overhead ×P (files, metadata, recovery bookkeeping) and wider scatter fan-out (a local-index query now asks 24 partitions, not 8 — and the tail-amplification math worsens with fan-out).

4.2 The delta log grows at 30 writes/second forever — the drain never catches up, so the migration literally cannot reach cutover; it is a queue with arrival rate > service rate (the replication-lag lesson from the quorums lab). The operator must raise the migration throttle (or move the partition during a quieter window, or split the partition first so each half's write rate fits under the throttle). Dashboard: rebalance in flight — bytes queued/ETA and throttle utilization; the tell is an ETA that grows instead of shrinking.

4.3 Threshold 5: partitions stay tiny — near-perfect balance and cheap moves, but per-partition overhead explodes and every query fans wider; the split machinery runs constantly (and each split churns the map, so stale-map redirects rise). Threshold 10,000: splits almost never happen — minimal overhead, but partitions become jumbo (slow, risky moves; coarse balance; the unit of movement stops being "manageable"). Rows implicated: "more partitions" (both directions) and "dynamic split/merge" in stage 12's table.

4.4 Costs: (1) per-partition overhead ×1,024 — files, memtables, open handles, recovery bookkeeping, heartbeat and metadata rows, for partitions averaging 30 MB; (2) every scatter query fans out 1,024 ways, so tail-latency amplification is at its worst (the 0.99^n curve of stage 10.7 is essentially zero at n = 1,024); (3) the map is 1,024 rows to store, agree on, propagate and keep fresh in every client cache, for a cluster of four machines. The fix is halving — Elasticsearch's _shrink, or its equivalent — and it can only target a factor of the current count: 512, 256, 128, … 1. Cost in availability: shrink-style operations typically require the source shards to be collected onto one node first and writes blocked for the duration, so it is a planned-maintenance operation, not a background one.

4.5 The broken invariant is routing safety ("a stale map costs extra hops, never wrong answers"). Shortest run: migrate p11 from B to D; cut over; one write lands at D (the true value advances); a client whose cached map still says B issues a read; B answers from the copy still on its disk. Three events and a wrong answer, with no node failure, no pause and no partition. Detection is the nasty part: nothing errors, so there is no rate to alarm on — the only signals are indirect (clients reporting impossible sequences, a read-repair or checksum job noticing divergence, or the age of the deposed owner's copy). With the read check present the client gets a MOVED redirect instead: it pays one extra round trip and repairs its map. That is the cost — an extra hop on some reads, plus a check on the hottest path — and it buys the difference between "slow sometimes" and "wrong invisibly".

4.6 Soft preference. Hard is wrong here because a rule worth 100 ms should never be able to stop the placer entirely: lose the Tokyo machines to maintenance and a hard rule leaves those partitions unplaced and unavailable — you traded 100 ms for an outage. Backwards in the other direction (soft when it should be hard) is worse in a quieter way: a residency rule expressed as a preference will be silently violated the first time the EU machines are full, and nothing will report it, because a satisfied-preference system considers itself healthy. Rule of thumb: constraints that protect correctness or legality are hard; constraints that protect performance are soft.

Rung 5 — Trace

5.1 Fair share = ⌊6/3⌋ = 2. Steal 1: A and B tie at 3; node-name order picks A; A's highest is p4 → C. (A:{p0,p2}, B:{p1,p3,p5}, C:{p4}.) Steal 2: B is most-loaded at 3; B's highest is p5 → C. Final: A={p0,p2}, B={p1,p3}, C={p4,p5}. Moved: p4, p5 → 2/6 = 33.3% of keys, which is exactly the newcomer's fair share — minimal, zero sideways. Invariant checks: coverage — p0…p5 each appear exactly once in the final assignment (count them); single owner per epoch — each stolen partition's map entry flips once, epoch bumped; handover completeness — each of the two moves runs the v2/v3 protocol; routing safety — old owners redirect until caches heal.

A write is lost under v1 exactly when it lands at the old owner in a region the snapshot has already passed — and by the end of the copy, that's every region.

5.3 P = 8, partition = h mod 8, node = partition mod 3:

P=8 partition 3 3 4 4 5 5 6 6
node A A B B C C A A
P=16 partition 3 11 4 12 5 13 6 14

(a) 4 of 8 keys changed partition — exactly the half whose hash had the 16-bit set, which is the arithmetic of stage 10.8 in miniature. (b) With children created where their parents live (p11 with p3, p12 with p4, p13 with p5, p14 with p6), the node column is unchanged for all eight keys: 0 crossings. (c) Re-dealt by partition mod 3, the new nodes are A, C, B, A, C, B, A, C — 4 crossings, i.e. every key that changed partition also changed machine.

So "doubling is free" depends on two things, and only one of them is arithmetic. The arithmetic guarantees the split is local; the placer has to then choose to keep the child where the parent was. A placer that re-derives placement from a formula on the partition id throws the gift away and turns a free split into a full redistribution. (This is why real split APIs are explicitly "split in place, rebalance afterwards if you want to" — two operations, deliberately separate, exactly the two hops of stage 6.)

For P = 12 the partitions become 3, 11, 8, 4, 1, 9, 6, 2: 5 of 8 changed, and all 5 cross. No stays-home rule can help, because there is no parent-child relationship to exploit — a key from old partition 5 can land in new partition 1 or 9, and old partition 5's other keys land elsewhere again. Nothing was split; everything was re-shuffled.

5.4 Delta log as keys that changed — the resurrection version:

after B holds D holds delta log
copy asha asha,ben,chirag asha —
delete ben asha,chirag asha {ben}
copy ben asha,chirag asha {ben} (nothing to copy; D's ben stays absent)
delete asha chirag asha {ben, asha}
copy chirag chirag asha,chirag {ben, asha}
catch-up chirag asha,chirag drains: "what is ben now?" → nothing → no change; "what is asha now?" → nothing → no change
cutover — asha, chirag —

D serves asha, which was deleted. The ben deletion survived only by luck — the copy cursor reached ben after the delete, so it copied nothing. That luck is the tell: correctness that depends on the race between the cursor and the workload is not correctness.

Delta log as operations: entries are DELETE ben @t2, DELETE asha @t4; catch-up applies both; D holds chirag only, which is right. Both deletions survive regardless of cursor position.

Which is worse? The asha case, and not because one row differs. A lost write means a user's change is undone and they will usually notice and redo it. A lost delete means data the user asked you to destroy is quietly alive again on a new machine — that is a compliance incident, it is invisible to the user who requested it, and it is discovered by an auditor rather than by a customer.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 "Empty when we checked" is a check-then-act gap (third lab running): between D observing the empty log and the map flipping, B accepts write W and appends it — W is on B, invisible to D, and B's copy is deleted. Concrete run: log empties at t=1; W arrives at t=2; flip at t=3; W gone. Violated: handover completeness. The brief write-pause exists precisely to make "empty" still true at the instant of the flip — drain-then-flip must be atomic with respect to new writes.

6.2 An in-memory flag read by the node about itself is exactly the stale holder's self-belief. Run: rebalancer calls B to set still_owner=false → the call races B's GC pause (or B is partitioned from the rebalancer, or B restarts and re-initializes the flag to true) → B wakes/reconnects, flag still true, accepts writes → two owners. The flag is a push of a fact to the least reliable witness; the epoch is a check against an authoritative fact carried by every request. Violated: single owner per epoch. (Also note the restart case: the distributed-locking lab's token-counter-in-memory bug, same shape.)

6.3 Salting must be write-random, read-ALL. Writes scattering to key#0..19 while reads sample one random salt means each read sees 1/20th of the writes — reads silently miss ~95% of the data (e.g. a counter reads ~5% of its true value). Correct: reads fan to all 20 sub-keys and merge (that's the tax salting pays), or maintain a merged rollup. The flaw isn't load balance — load is even — it's correctness: the salt made one logical key into 20 physical keys and the read pretended otherwise.

6.4 Three seconds of silence triggers terabytes of movement: this is F5 armed. Run: normal GC pause of 5 s on node C → C declared dead → C's partitions stream to survivors → streaming saturates B and D's network → their heartbeats miss → more drains → the movement manufactures the next verdict (gossip lab: silence is the only symptom, and load supplies it). "We tested by killing nodes" tested only the true-positive path — the detector's false positives under load were never exercised (the falsifying test: pause, don't kill). Fixes: sustained-evidence thresholds (minutes), concurrent-move and bandwidth caps, route-around-first (replicas serve while the node is merely suspected), human gate for bulk movement.

6.5 Two planted mistakes, one arithmetic and one categorical. The arithmetic: 100 is not a multiple of 64, so there is no local split — h mod 64 tells you nothing about h mod 100, and essentially the whole dataset is re-addressed and must be shipped. (64 → 128 would have been nearly free.) The categorical error is the last sentence: this is not the same operation as adding a node, only bigger. Adding a node changes hop 2 — a table of partition→node rows — and moves ~1/n of the data with the key→partition mapping untouched, so clients' cached arithmetic stays correct throughout. Changing P changes hop 1, the mapping every client computes for itself, which means there is a window where clients disagree about which partition a key belongs to — not merely about where a partition lives. Redirects cannot repair that: a MOVED tells you where a partition is, and here the partition itself is the wrong one. Violated: nothing in the invariant list — which is the point. This design breaks an assumption underneath the list, that key→partition is stable. "Overnight" is also wrong on its own terms: at 4 TB with a throttled rebalancer this is days, and during them the cluster is neither in the old scheme nor the new one.

6.6 The mistake is in the evidence, not the design: "the dashboards stayed green all week". Consolidating three racks into two makes every three-copy partition unplaceable under the rack rule (F7) — and a constrained placer's response to "no legal placement" is to stop moving, which registers on a dashboard as zero rebalancing activity, zero errors and normal latency. Concrete run: rack C is decommissioned on Monday; each partition's third copy becomes homeless; the cluster serves reads and writes perfectly all week from two copies; on Friday a rack loses power and partitions that were supposed to survive it lose their only remaining copy. The violated guarantee is the durability margin the replication factor was purchased for, and it was lost on Monday, silently. Secondary flaw worth naming: "moves partitions continuously toward the emptiest machine" is a rebalancer with no hysteresis and no throttle, which is F5's other half — it will chase noise. The monitoring lesson is the one from F7: alarm on under-replicated partitions, never on rebalancing activity, because the dangerous state is the one with no activity.

Rung 7 — Handle failure (worked designs)

7.1 D crashes mid-catch-up. State at crash: B has all data + the delta log + is still map owner (epoch unchanged — the flip never happened); D has a partial snapshot + partially applied deltas, all of it non-authoritative. Who notices: the migration coordinator (whoever drives the state machine — the rebalancer) via D's death or lease expiry; B also notices the drain stopped. Safety consequence: none — clients were routed to B throughout, so no acknowledged write ever depended on D. The migration simply aborts: B discards the delta log, D's partial copy is garbage-collected on restart. Resume vs restart: restart-from-scratch is always correct; resume is an optimization needing (a) the snapshot copy to be durably checkpointed on D with its copy-cursor position, and (b) B to retain the delta log from the snapshot's start point (or a WAL position — which is why real systems anchor migrations to a log offset: "resume" = re-ship deltas since offset X). The invariant that must hold: D never serves until it holds snapshot + complete delta; the cheapest durable bookkeeping is a single migration record in the authoritative map store — p11: migrating B→D, started@offset X, state=COPYING — because whoever recovers (a new coordinator, a restarted D) can read it and know the flip never happened, so B is still the owner. The one thing that must NOT exist: any path where D serves based on local state alone.

7.2 Routing loop for a dead old owner. (1) Send to the cached owner with a short timeout. (2) On MOVED: update cache, retry at the named node — done. (3) On timeout/connection-refused: don't retry the same node; fetch the map entry from the authority (or ask any other cluster node, who forwards or answers from its cache — a second opinion is cheaper than the authority). (4) Cap redirect-chains (2–3 hops) then fall back to the authority. (5) Herd control, from the caching + distributed-locking labs: single-flight the map fetch per process (one in-flight refresh, everyone else waits on it) and exponential backoff with jitter on retries — otherwise the owner's death converts every client into a synchronized stampede on the map service (which is exactly when the map service can least afford it). (6) Negative-cache the death briefly so new requests don't re-probe the corpse.

7.3 The shape of the answer matters more than the specific minutes.

Minute 1: do nothing about data. Serve from the two surviving copies, mark the zone-C replicas unavailable, and route around them. Data movement is the most expensive action available and the evidence so far ("we cannot reach zone C") is the cheapest kind.

Minute 30: still no re-replication, but the state should now be visible — every partition is under-replicated by one copy, and that is the number on the dashboard. This is the window where an automatic rebalancer would already have started copying a third of the cluster, which is precisely F5: the movement would load the surviving two zones, whose heartbeats would then lag.

Hour 4: a judgement call, and the honest answer is that it should be a human's unless the outage is well past any plausible network event. Re-replicating into two zones means either violating the one-copy-per-zone rule (creating two copies in one zone — durability you do not actually have) or leaving the partitions under-replicated legally. Both are defensible; picking silently is not.

If zone C is gone permanently: re-replicate, deliberately, throttled, with the placement rule either relaxed on purpose or a third zone provisioned first.

The decision that separates the two cases when nobody knows which it is: is the loss of a machine treated as a change in membership, or as a temporary absence? Cassandra makes this the difference between a node being "down" (route around; free) and removenode (stream data; operator-invoked); Elasticsearch makes it index.unassigned.node_left.delayed_timeout. The rule is the same everywhere: routing around is automatic and instant; moving data is delayed and gated, because the first is cheap and reversible and the second is neither.

Page on: under-replicated partition count (F7 — it is the actual loss of durability margin). Deliberately do not page on: rebalancing activity, either its presence or its absence — high activity is normal during a planned move and zero activity is normal during a healthy day, so both alarm shapes generate noise while missing the real failures (F5 is caught by movement and detector verdicts climbing together, not by movement alone).

Rung 8 — Design: the payments ledger (worked discussion)

Partition key & scheme. Key everything by account_id, hash-partitioned; within a partition, entries clustered by (account_id, entry_time) (compound key: hash spreads accounts; the clustering component keeps one account's entries contiguous and time-ordered). Query (a) — post entry + update balance — is single-partition (balance row and entry stream share the account's partition), so it can be a local transaction: no 2PC on the hot path, which is the whole game (distributed-transactions lab's decision ladder: "co-locate keys on one shard" beats everything above it). Query (b) statement = one-partition range scan over the clustering key. Query (c) — all entries for a calendar day — is the sacrifice: it becomes scatter/gather across all partitions. Defend the sacrifice: it runs once daily, offline, latency-tolerant; better still, don't scan the ledger at all — stream entries via CDC/outbox into a date-partitioned analytical copy (database-landscape lab: derived, rebuildable, its own partitioning matched to its query shape). Never range-partition by time here: F2 would put every posting on one partition.

Fixed vs dynamic. Fixed P with a generous pre-split — e.g. P = 4,096 — because the fleet doubles yearly and fixed-P rebalancing is table-edit + whole-partition streams with zero boundary churn; 4,096 partitions over even a few hundred nodes keeps ~10+ partitions/node for balance granularity. (Dynamic splitting is defensible too — CockroachDB would be a fine substrate — but with a hash scheme and a known key population, fixed-P is simpler and P can be sized once, honestly: 500M accounts × ~100 KB/account lifetime ≈ 50 TB → ~12 GB/partition at P=4,096, moveable in minutes at a tame throttle.)

Whales (0.1% at 100×). 100× the median write rate on one account is a load-skew problem inside one partition. Data answer: the entry stream for a whale can be bucketed (account_id + month-style clustering, or salt the stream sub-key while keeping the balance row single) — but the balance row itself must not be salted: it's the thing query (a) atomically updates, and splitting it recreates the distributed-rate-limiting lab's invariant problem. So: keep the balance row single (it's one row — 100× a median row's write rate is hot but bounded), give whale partitions dedicated placement (a partition holding a whale gets a node with headroom — placement constraints, stage 13's omission list), and alarm on per-partition heat so new whales are noticed, not discovered.

Migration & epochs. Protocol v3 verbatim: snapshot at a WAL offset, delta = WAL tail, brief write-pause cutover, epoch bump in the map; the store checks the epoch on every write (reads too, if you serve reads from partition owners — the distributed-locking lab's read fence). Because it's money, add the idempotency lab's layer: entry posts carry idempotency keys, so a client retry across a MOVED/epoch-refused boundary can never double-post — the migration machinery will cause retries, and retries must be harmless before you rebalance anything.

The map lives in a Raft-backed store (etcd or equivalent): P=4,096 rows plus epochs is tiny, changes rarely, must never fork. Clients are partition-aware with the rung-7.2 routing loop.

Automatic vs manual. Automatic: leadership transfers (no data motion), single-partition migrations under caps (≤2 concurrent, bandwidth-throttled, sustained-evidence health checks), split of a partition that trips size/heat alarms — with hysteresis. Manual (human approves): anything moving >1% of partitions at once (fleet doubling is a planned, staged campaign), any response to a suspected node (route around automatically; never bulk-move on suspicion — F5), and the annual P-adequacy review.

Five dashboards first: (1) per-partition heat histogram + busiest:median ratio (whale detector); (2) per-partition size histogram (jumbo detector); (3) migrations in flight: bytes queued, ETA trend, throttle utilization; (4) epoch-rejection + MOVED rates (should be ~0 / low-and-spiky-only-during- moves); (5) movement-per-hour vs detector-verdicts-per-hour on one graph (the F5 tripwire). Plus the idempotency lab's oldest-RUNNING age on the posting path, because ledger.

The honest tension: (a) "post atomically" wants everything about an account on one partition forever; (d) "never lose an acknowledged entry while doubling the fleet yearly" wants aggressive movement. The compromise: movement happens at partition granularity with v3 + idempotent posts, and the posting path is designed to tolerate migration (retries harmless, epoch checks loud) rather than migrations being scheduled around the posting path. The second tension — query (c) vs the hash scheme — is resolved by refusing to serve it from the ledger at all: a derived, date-partitioned copy answers it, one day behind, and the regulator's SLA (daily) makes that honest.

8.2 — The resharding you inherited (worked discussion)

Target partition count. Work from the unit of movement, not the machine count. Eleven hours per 62 GB partition is the symptom; pick a target partition size that makes a move routine — say 4–8 GB, i.e. under an hour throttled. 4 TB / 6 GB ≈ 680, so 1,024 is the number to reach for: it is a power of two, so it is reachable by doubling from 64 (64 → 128 → 256 → 512 → 1024, four doublings) and it can be halved later if you overshoot.

Which route. Three exist and only one survives contact with this system. Full redistribution to an arbitrary P is rejected: 4 TB re-shuffled, days of it, with every client's mod 64 arithmetic wrong the whole time — and, worse, wrong in a way redirects cannot repair (exercise 6.5). Switching to hash-range partitions is the right destination and the wrong migration: it changes hop 1 for every one of nine client teams simultaneously, so it belongs in the next system, not this quarter. Repeated doubling is the answer: each step splits every partition locally into two, moves nothing across the network, and can be done one partition at a time.

Does the client library change? Yes, and this is the real project. Clients hardcode 64 in md5(user_id) mod 64, so the number must become data — fetched from ZooKeeper, versioned, and cached with the partition map rather than compiled in. That change must ship to all nine teams and be fully adopted before the first split, because a client computing mod 64 while the cluster is at 128 addresses a partition that no longer exists. Sequence: (1) ship a library that reads P from the map and understands redirects; (2) verify adoption — no client on an old version, measured, not assumed; (3) only then split. If any team cannot upgrade, a routing tier in front is the fallback: it costs a hop and buys independence from the slowest team.

Losing nothing in the process. Each split is: freeze writes for that one partition (milliseconds), record the split point and the new ownership epoch in ZooKeeper atomically, open the two children, unfreeze. Because no data moves, there is no snapshot/delta problem for the split itself — the delta machinery matters for the rebalancing afterwards, when children are spread across machines, and there it is the full v3 protocol including operations, not dirty keys, so deletes cannot resurrect (10.6 hole 2). Deletes deserve explicit attention here because this is user-document storage: a resurrected document is a deletion request quietly reversed.

Reads during the change. Two hazards, both from 10.6. During a child's rebalancing move, reads follow ownership, not copy progress — the old owner serves until the map flips. After the flip, the old owner must seal the partition and answer MOVED, or nine teams' cached maps will silently harvest stale documents. Since this store did not previously have per-partition epochs at all, adding them is part of the client-library work, not an afterthought.

Duration and write-blocking. The splits themselves are fast — the blocked window is per-partition and measured in milliseconds, so four doublings across 64 → 1,024 partitions is thousands of tiny freezes, not one long outage. The slow part is optional and afterwards: rebalancing 1,024 small partitions across 24 machines to even things out, throttled, over days, with no write blocking at all. That split (cheap-and-atomic vs slow-and-background) is the payoff of keeping the two hops separate.

Rollback at 40%. Splits are individually committed, so "rollback" means stop, not undo — a cluster with 600 of its partitions split and 424 not is a perfectly valid cluster, because the map is a table and nothing requires the partitions to be uniform. That is the property to verify before starting: the map must express per-partition boundaries rather than a single global P. If it cannot, fix that first, because a design where the intermediate state is invalid has no rollback at any percentage.

The one property that made this expensive: hop 1 was a formula compiled into nine independent clients, so changing the shape of the data required a synchronized change to code owned by other people. The sentence for the next design doc: the number of partitions is data, not a constant — it lives in the map, clients read it, and no client may assume it.
