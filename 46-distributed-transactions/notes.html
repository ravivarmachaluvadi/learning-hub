Where you are: BEGINNER MODEL — stages 1–9. No prior knowledge of this concept assumed; everything from your earlier labs gets a one-line refresher when it appears.

1. WHY does this exist?

When a business action must change data that lives on several machines — money leaving an account on one and arriving on another — two things must be true at once: the changes must all happen or none happen, and everyone else looking at the data must never see the in-between. Get the first wrong and money is destroyed. Get the second wrong and every report, balance check, and fraud rule in the company computes on money that briefly doesn't exist. Databases solved both on one machine decades ago. This lab is about what it costs to get them back when the data is split.

2. THE PROBLEM, as a story

A bank has grown too big for one computer, so its accounts are split across branch machines: names starting A–M live on branch machine S0, N–Z on S1. (Your consistent-hashing lab called this sharding; here it just means "alice's row and bob's row are on different computers".)

Priya the teller moves 50 from alice (on S0) to bob (on S1).

Omar the auditor has one job: at any moment he pleases, he sums every account across every branch, and the total must equal the money the bank actually holds — 200. Not "usually 200". Always 200. An auditor who can be fooled by walking between branches at the wrong second is not an auditor.

Priya's problem and Omar's problem are different, and the whole lab hangs on that difference. Priya needs her two changes to happen together. Omar needs them to appear together — to him, and to every other reader in the system, forever.

3. THE NAIVE SOLUTION

Commit at S0, then commit at S1:

shardS0.commit(debit(alice, 50));   // done!
shardS1.commit(credit(bob, 50));    // done! ...probably

You already know this beast: it is the dual write from your outbox lab, and the two-phase-commit lab spent its whole story killing it. Measured here anyway (experiment 1): crash a few clients mid-transfer and money is permanently wrong in 28 of 40 runs.

So we reach for the fix we already own: two-phase commit — lock and stage on both shards (prepare), durably log the verdict, then commit both. The 2PC lab proved this makes the pair all-or-nothing, and our experiment 1 confirms it: with 2PC plus its crash recovery, money wrong in 0 of 40 runs, crashes and all.

Job done?

🔮 Predict: With 2PC working perfectly — no crashes at all — can Omar's audit ever be wrong?

Reveal Yes, routinely. 2PC makes the two commits **both happen**, but it commits shard S0 at one moment and shard S1 at a slightly later moment. Omar can read S0 after its commit and S1 before its commit. In experiment 2, with **zero crashes**, auditors sweeping during commits got a wrong total **16 times** under 2PC — barely better than the naive design's 14. 2PC was never designed to say anything about readers.

4. THE FAILURE, watched closely

Run java src/Sim.java 1 and watch it happen:

Priya: COMMIT applied on S0. alice is now 50 — visible to everyone.
Omar reads alice on S0: sees 50   (the new world)
Omar reads bob on S1:   sees 100  (the old world)
Omar's total: 150. Fifty units of money do not exist.
Priya: COMMIT applied on S1. bob is now 150. Total is correct again: 200

Nothing crashed. Nothing was misconfigured. 2PC kept every promise it ever made — and the audit was still wrong, because "commit" across two shards is not an instant, it is a window, and Omar walked through the window.

This is the deeper problem the naive solution just uncovered, and it deserves its own names:

  • Atomic durability — the changes all end up on disk or none do. 2PC gives you this. It is a promise about the final state.
  • Atomic visibility — no observer, ever, sees some of the changes without the rest. 2PC does not give you this. It is a promise about every moment along the way.

Omar's wrong total is a fractured read: a read that caught a transaction half-applied across shards. You have seen its single-machine cousin: the isolation-levels lab called it read skew, and fixed it there with a snapshot. Hold that thought — it is exactly where this lab is going, and exactly where it gets hard: on one machine a snapshot was cheap because one machine has one clock. Two shards have two clocks and no shared "now".

5. THE REAL REQUIREMENT

A distributed transaction — the thing this lab is named after — needs all three of:

  1. All-or-nothing on disk (atomic durability). 2PC's job; solved.
  2. All-or-nothing to every observer (atomic visibility). Every reader sees a transaction entirely or not at all — Omar's job.
  3. A defensible order (isolation). Concurrent transactions must not corrupt each other — the isolation-levels lab's job, now spread over machines that share nothing.

And one more, which you won't miss until stage 10's ending: once the system tells a client "done", no later reader may see a world without that transaction — the real-time rule you met in the linearizability lab, wearing a new costume ("external consistency").

6. THE KEY IDEA

Two sentences, which the rest of the lab unfolds:

Give every transaction one position in a single, global order of time — one number — and make every commit and every read happen "as of" a number. Then a transaction is either entirely before your read or entirely after it, and the fractured in-between is not just avoided but unobservable.

Make the commit itself one local write in one durable place — so that the verdict "did it commit?" can be answered by anyone, even after every coordinator involved is dead.

The first sentence is a snapshot made global. The second is the 2PC lab's commit point, moved out of the coordinator's private log and into data everyone can reach.

7. TERMINOLOGY — the words, now that you've felt the problems

The load-bearing terms. Each exists to make a distinction you have now watched matter.

Shard. One independent database holding a slice of the keys, committing its own changes atomically with its own local machinery. alice's shard can promise things about alice; nobody is in charge of alice-and-bob. That missing "nobody" is the subject of the lab.

Atomic visibility. The guarantee that no reader anywhere sees a transaction partially applied. Distinct from atomic durability — stage 4's whole point — and coined atomic visibility in the research literature (the RAMP papers) precisely because "atomic" alone had come to mean only durability. Example: experiment 2's TPC row — durable in 40/40 runs, fractured 16 times.

Fractured read. A read that observes some of a transaction's writes but not the rest (Omar's 150). The cross-shard sibling of the isolation-levels lab's read skew.

Snapshot / consistent cut. A single timestamp s, with reads returning each key's newest version committed at or below s. It is a cut through history: everything ≤ s is in, everything above is out. For the cut to be consistent across shards, "committed at ts" must mean the same thing on every shard — which is only true if timestamps come from one order.

Timestamp oracle. A service handing out numbers from one counter, so that "one global order of time" is trivially true. Two calls per write transaction (start, commit), one per read. The simplest possible correct answer, and a single point of failure and scale — measured in experiment 5, removed in stage 10's final act.

Prewrite / primary / secondary. Percolator's names. Prewrite: stage a value and take a lock on every written key. The primary is the one key the transaction appoints as the place its verdict will live; every other written key (secondary) carries a lock that points at the primary. Plain-English version: every sticky note on every branch says "the decision is taped to alice's page".

Commit point (revisited). In the 2PC lab: the coordinator's durable decision record — one place, owned by one process, and the reason 2PC blocks. Here: replacing the primary's lock with a commit record, in one local shard write. Same concept, relocated into data. Scene 2 shows the exact line; experiment 9 proves it is the line.

Lock TTL / resolution. A reader that hits a lock consults the primary and then acts: roll forward (finish a committed-but-unfinished txn's cleanup), wait (fresh lock, txn alive), or roll back (stale lock, no commit record — bury the corpse). This is the mechanism that deletes the blocking problem.

External consistency. Spanner's name for the linearizability lab's real-time rule applied to transactions: if T1's client was told "done" before T2 started, T2 must see T1. A stale snapshot is the violation: a snapshot number smaller than an already-acknowledged commit's number.

Uncertainty interval (ε) / commit wait. A clock that might be wrong by at most ε doesn't know "now"; it knows "now is somewhere in [t−ε, t+ε]". Commit wait: after choosing a commit timestamp, hold the "done" message until the timestamp is provably in the past for every clock in the system (~2ε). The ack gets slower; the clocks stay wrong; correctness returns.

Minor terms defined on first use later: distributed deadlock, first-committer-wins (from isolation-levels), write skew (ditto), TrueTime, HLC.

8. TWO MENTAL MODELS

The simple model: the numbered-ticket bank. Head office has one roll of numbered tickets. Every action — starting a piece of work, sealing a deal — tears off the next ticket, and every page of every ledger records the ticket number under which it was written. An audit is not "walk around reading pages"; it is "tear off a ticket — say #40 — and read every page as it stood at #40", ignoring anything written under a later number. A deal touching two branches gets sticky notes on both pages saying "pending — the decision will be taped to alice's page", and sealing the deal is one act: taping the verdict, with its ticket number, onto alice's page.

The engineering model. Each shard: key → {commitTs → value} (MVCC version chains — same picture as isolation-levels), plus a lock table of claims (startTs, primaryKey), plus the primary's commitRecord: startTs → commitTs. A write transaction: startTs → prewrite all keys (primary first) → commitTs → one write at the primary → lazy cleanup. A read: one snapTs for all shards; per key, take the newest version ≤ snapTs, detouring through the resolution rule when a lock with startTs ≤ snapTs is present.

Simple model Engineering model
ticket roll at head office timestamp oracle (later: clocks + ε)
ticket number on a ledger page commitTs on a version
"read the world as of #40" snapshot read at snapTs=40
sticky note "decision on alice's page" secondary lock pointing at the primary
taping the verdict to alice's page the primary commit record — the commit point
a note left by a teller who went home an abandoned lock, past its TTL

Where the analogy breaks. (1) A ticket roll never runs out and never goes down; a real oracle is a process — TiDB runs one and engineers its availability hard. (2) The tempting upgrade "skip head office, use wristwatches" silently changes the model: tickets never go backwards, clocks do — that one difference is stages 10.5–10.6. (3) Tearing a ticket is instant; in the real system getting a timestamp is a network round trip that can reorder against other messages — the sim models it as one step, real systems batch it.

10. THE ACTUAL MECHANISM — four versions, three funerals

v1 — 2PC + "just read the current values"

This is stage 4. Engine TPC in the code. Durability perfect (0/40 lost), visibility broken (16 fractured audits with no crashes; scene 1 shows one). Buried. But its corpse defines the goal: the fix must close the window between the per-shard commits — or make the window unobservable.

v2 — close the window with locks: distributed 2PL

The isolation-levels lab's answer on one machine was strict two-phase locking: readers take read locks, writers take write locks, everyone holds them to the end. Distribute it (engine TPC_2PL): Omar read-locks alice, then bob; Priya's prepare write-locks collide with Omar's read locks; whoever is second waits. Result: fractured audits: 0 (experiment 2). Correct! Serializable, even — 2PL doesn't care how many machines the locks live on.

Three prices, all measured:

  • Readers wait, always. Omar queues behind every in-flight transfer (experiment 8: 159 reader wait ticks vs 0 for the no-lock designs).
  • Distributed deadlock — and no one can see it. Priya locks alice→bob, a reverse transfer locks bob→alice. Each shard sees exactly one waiting transaction — the cycle exists only across shards, so no shard's local deadlock detector can ever fire. A distributed deadlock is a waits-for cycle whose edges live on different machines. The lab breaks it with timeouts: one victim aborts, the survivor commits (test d2pl_cross_shard_deadlock_timeout_kills_one_victim_survivor_commits). Experiment 4: 632 timeout aborts on a hot pair — and the isolation-levels lock-ordering fix still works across shards (632 → 320).
  • The blocking problem, now with victims. In the 2PC lab, a dead coordinator froze participants. Here it freezes innocent readers: kill coordinators mid-commit and 281 auditors gave up waiting forever (experiment 3). 2PC's defining flaw, inherited by every read in the system.

🔮 Predict: Could we let Omar read old versions instead of waiting — each shard keeps its version history (MVCC, like isolation-levels), and Omar reads "as of when my audit started" per shard?

Reveal Per shard, yes. Across shards, "when my audit started" is not one moment — S0's version numbers (its local sequence) and S1's have no relation to each other. A cut at "S0's seq 17, S1's seq 12" can still land after the transfer's commit on one shard and before it on the other: the same fracture, wearing MVCC clothing. A snapshot only works if **one order of time spans all shards.** That is the next version.

v3 — one order of time: the oracle, and Percolator

Give the cluster a timestamp oracle — one counter. This is Google Percolator's design (2010, built on Bigtable; today the heart of TiDB), and it answers both halves of the key idea at once. Engine PERCO. The commit protocol, with the real values from scene 2 (java src/Sim.java 2):

sequenceDiagram

    participant O as Oracle
    participant S0 as S0 (alice = PRIMARY)
    participant S1 as S1 (bob = secondary)

    P->>S0: PREWRITE alice: lock(start=2, primary=alice), stage 50

    P->>S0: COMMIT PRIMARY: alice=50@3, drop lock, commitRecord{2→3}
    Note over S0: ← THE COMMIT POINT (one local write)
    end
    P->>S1: COMMIT bob: 150@3, drop lock (lazy cleanup)

Walk it with the story's eyes:

  1. start-ts = 2 — Priya's snapshot. All her reads are "as of ticket 2".
  2. Prewrite, primary first. Every written key gets a lock + staged value. bob's lock points at alice: "my verdict will live there". A prewrite fails if the key is locked (someone mid-flight) or has a committed version above start-ts — the first-committer-wins rule from isolation-levels, now enforced per shard with no coordination.
  3. commit-ts = 3, then the flip: in one local write on S0, alice's lock becomes the committed version alice=50@3 plus the record 2→3. This single write commits the whole transaction — experiment 9 kills the client at every one of the seven steps and the outcome flips exactly there: steps 1–5 → rolled back, money 200; steps 6–7 → rolled forward, money 200. Never anything else.
  4. Secondaries commit lazily. Pure cleanup — the verdict already exists.

And the read side, which is where the magic actually lives. Omar, snapshot s, hits a lock with startTs ≤ s:

stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> AskPrimary: lock with startTs ≤ s
    AskPrimary --> RollForward: commitRecord[startTs]=cts, cts ≤ s
    AskPrimary --> ReadBelow: cts > s (committed after me)
    AskPrimary --> Wait: no record, primary lock alive & fresh
    AskPrimary --> RollBack: no record, lock past TTL or primary lock gone
    RollForward --> [*]: apply staged@cts here, then read
    ReadBelow --> [*]: newest version ≤ s
    Wait --> AskPrimary: retry shortly
    RollBack --> [*]: clean lock+staged, then read

This rule (in code: Engines.resolveAndRead, ~30 lines — and src/YourResolver.java makes you write it yourself) is what deletes v2's funerals in one stroke, measured:

  • Fractured audits: 0, with readers never blocking behind healthy commits (experiment 2; scene 4 shows Omar finishing a dead client's commit himself, rollForwards=47 across experiment 3's crash storm).
  • The blocking problem: gone. Same crash storm that stranded 281 locked readers forever strands 0 percolator readers — the worst case is waiting out one TTL (30 ticks), after which any reader buries the corpse (scene 3). The verdict lives in a shard, and shards outlive coordinators.
  • Atomicity under every crash: 0/40 money-wrong with clients dying at random steps (experiment 1).

Why is it safe for random readers to roll transactions back and forward? Because the primary is a single arbitration cell: a committing client flips it only if its lock is still there; a cleaning reader removes the lock first. Both race to one row on one shard, and one of them loses cleanly — the loser's next step observes it lost (test: a rolled-back client's commit attempt finds its lock gone and aborts; counter rolledBackByReader).

What percolator does not fix — it is honest snapshot isolation, distributed: write skew survives (experiment 7: two doctors, one invariant "someone on call", each on-call flag on a different shard; both transactions read {1,1} under their snapshots, write different keys, both commit, on-call count 0). Same anomaly, same mechanism-shaped hole as the isolation-levels lab, and 2PL — which locks what was read — still prevents it. Remember the closing line of that lab: snapshot checks what you wrote; serializable also checks what you read. Distribution changed nothing about that sentence.

🔮 Predict: The oracle is one process handing out numbers. What exactly goes wrong when you try to run this design across two continents — or when the oracle dies?

Reveal Every transaction — reads included — makes a round trip to the oracle before its first byte of work (experiment 5: ~2 calls per write, 1 per read, 1,913 calls in 40 small runs). Cross-continent, that is +100ms on *everything*, and the counter itself becomes the throughput ceiling and the availability linchpin. TiDB accepts this trade and engineers around it (a Raft-replicated "PD" service, batched timestamp grants). Google, building Spanner planet-wide, could not. Their way out is the last act.

v4 — fire the oracle: clocks, the lie, and commit wait

Replace the oracle with each client's own clock (engine PERCO_CLOCK): start-ts = my clock now, commit-ts = my clock now. Everything else identical. Fractured reads: still 0 — the lock check never depended on where timestamps came from. But scene 5 (java src/Sim.java 5):

tick 16: Priya's transfer commits at ts=18001 and she is told: DONE.
tick 18 (AFTER the ack): Omar starts an audit. His slow clock hands him
snapshot 13002 — which is LESS than 18001. He reads alice=100.
A transfer that was confirmed done... is invisible.

Priya's clock runs 5 ticks fast, Omar's 5 slow. Omar begins after Priya was told "done" and reads a world without her transfer — the real-time rule from the linearizability lab, violated by a wristwatch. Experiment 6 measures it: at skew 8 ticks, 10 stale snapshots; at 20, 25 — and remember that lab's darker lesson, which repeats here: at skew 0–2 the count is 0. A design with no guarantee is indistinguishable from a correct one while the clocks happen to behave.

The fix is not better clocks — no clock is perfect. It is admitting imperfection into the protocol. TrueTime (Spanner's clock API) returns an interval [earliest, latest] guaranteed to contain true time, ε wide on each side. Then (engine PERCO_CLOCK_WAIT):

  • take commit-ts = now().latest — a timestamp that is, if anything, in the future;
  • commit wait: before telling the client "done", spin until now().earliest > commit-ts — until the commit timestamp is provably in the past for every honest clock on Earth.

Now anyone who starts after the ack gets snap-ts = now().latest ≥ real time > commit-ts. Measured: stale snapshots 0 at every skew; the bill is the ack — average commit latency 6.7 → 24.8 ticks at ε=8, 51.0 at ε=20 (~2ε, exactly as predicted by the two inequalities). And the second-order bill: locks are held ~2ε longer, so reader waiting rises too (experiment 8). Spanner's bet is engineering, not mathematics: GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every datacenter push ε to a few milliseconds, so commit wait costs less than a disk sync. Nobody's clock got better. The ack got slower.

11. FAILURE SCENARIOS

Each: how to create it → where to watch → what happens, why, what holds, what bends, and the production face of it.

Client/coordinator dies mid-commit (percolator). Create: killAtStep 1–7; watch: experiment 9 (the table), scenes 3–4, visualization Kill client. Before the primary flip: no commit record can ever appear, so the first reader past the TTL rolls everything back — the transaction never happened. After the flip: the record exists, so every reader rolls the leftovers forward — it fully happened. Holds: atomicity, visibility, non-blocking reads (worst wait = one TTL). Bends: the writer's latency promise — a transaction can be committed and its owner never learns it, so client retries must be idempotent (your outbox lab's law, resurfacing). Production: TiDB readers do exactly this resolution; its TTLs scale with transaction size, and monitoring watches "resolve lock" rates.

Coordinator dies holding locks (distributed 2PL). Create: kills(2) on TPC_2PL; watch: experiment 3. 281 readers strand forever (until recovery) because the verdict lives only in the dead coordinator's log — the 2PC lab's blocking problem, now taxing every reader in the cluster. Holds: safety — nobody reads a wrong value. Bends: liveness, catastrophically. Production: this is why lock-based XA across services is feared in operations — a wedged transaction manager turns into a cluster-wide brownout, and DBAs end up making heuristic decisions (manually forcing commit/abort per participant — breaking atomicity by hand, under pressure).

Distributed deadlock. Create: opposite-direction transfers on a hot pair; watch: experiment 4, test ..._survivor_commits. Each shard sees half a cycle; no local detector can fire; a timeout kills one victim and the survivor commits. Holds: atomicity, consistency. Bends: latency and throughput (632 timeout aborts), fairness (victims are arbitrary). Production: real systems use lock-wait timeouts (MySQL XA), global waits-for sampling, or avoid it structurally — deterministic databases (stage 15) order transactions before executing them.

Clock skew beyond ε. Create: give ClockTs an offset larger than the eps you pass; not measured above because it breaks the model's honesty — TrueTime's entire contract is that the interval contains true time. If a clock drifts outside its promised bound, commit wait's inequality is built on sand and external consistency silently fails — the linearizability lesson again: no violation until the day there is one. Production: Spanner evicts nodes whose clocks misbehave; CockroachDB crashes a node whose offset exceeds the configured maximum (default 500ms) — crashing is the correct response to a lying clock.

Straggler locks from slow (not dead) clients. Create: lower LOCK_TTL below a healthy commit's duration — a reader will roll back a live transaction; the client's own commit then finds its lock gone and aborts (counter rolledBackByReader). The TTL is a bet: too long → readers wait on corpses; too short → healthy writers get murdered. Holds: safety, always (the primary cell arbitrates). Bends: write availability under load spikes. Production: TiDB heartbeats long transactions to extend their TTL.

Oracle down / partitioned. Every new transaction in the cluster stalls at startTs — reads included. Not modeled (the oracle here is an object); the mitigation is the v4 story, or TiDB's: make the oracle a Raft group (your raft lab: elections in ~1.5 timeouts) and grant timestamps in batches.

12. CORRECTNESS — what each design actually promises

atomic durability atomic visibility isolation external consistency reads block? deadlock?
NAIVE ✗ (28/40) ✗ (14) none ✗ no no
TPC ✓ ✗ (16) write-locks only ✗ no writers
TPC_2PL ✓ ✓ (0) serializable ✓* yes writers+readers
PERCO ✓ ✓ (0) snapshot (write skew!) ✓ (oracle orders acks) no no (aborts instead)
PERCO_CLOCK ✓ ✓ (0) snapshot ✗ (10 stale @ skew 8) no no
PERCO_CLOCK_WAIT ✓ ✓ (0) snapshot ✓ (0 stale) no no

* 2PL's external consistency comes free from blocking: you cannot read around a lock you are waiting on.

The shape to internalize — nothing in the right columns is free:

  • Visibility via locks costs reader waiting and inherits 2PC blocking.
  • Visibility via snapshots costs a global time order — an oracle (SPOF, round trips) or clocks+ε (commit wait ≈ 2ε on every write ack).
  • Isolation above snapshot costs aborts (SSI-style read tracking) or waiting (2PL). Percolator alone leaves write skew alive — the one row of the table where distribution changed nothing at all.
  • And everything above assumes each shard itself survives — in production every shard and the oracle are themselves Raft/Paxos groups (stage 15), paying that lab's price underneath this one.

Safety vs liveness, one more time: every mechanism here keeps safety unconditionally (no run in 40×10 experiments showed wrong money or a fractured read on the ✓ designs — and the planted bugs each break exactly one cell: run java src/Tests.java broken). What failures attack is liveness: who waits, for how long, and whether a corpse can make the waiting permanent. The design progression of this whole lab is liveness repair: 2PC blocks participants → 2PL blocks readers too → percolator blocks nobody beyond a TTL → commit wait deliberately blocks only the final ack, because that is the cheapest place in the system to pay.

13. THE CODE — the lines that carry the guarantees

Read in this order (all in src/Engines.java):

  1. resolveAndRead — the percolator read rule; the diagram in stage 10 as ~30 lines. The load-bearing branches: l.startTs > snapTs (a lock from a later transaction is none of your business — miss this and readers stall on the future); cts <= snapTs roll-forward (a reader completing a stranger's commit); the TTL rollback (liveness); the final else (aborted leftovers). YourResolver.java is this method with the decisions blanked out.
  2. PercoTransfer.commitPrimary — the commit point. Note the guard: if our lock is gone, a reader outran us and we are already rolled back — the primary cell arbitrating the race. The line writing commitRecord is the single write experiment 9 pivots on.
  3. PercoTransfer.doStep prewrite arm — latestCommitTs(key) > startTs → abort: first-committer-wins with zero cross-shard coordination, because a snapshot plus that one local check is the conflict check.
  4. ClockTs.commitWaitDone — one inequality, now().earliest > commitTs; the entire content of "Spanner waits out the uncertainty".
  5. TpcTransfer + LockingReader — v1/v2 for contrast; the readersLock flag is the only difference between "fractured 16" and "fractured 0 but 281 stranded readers".

The scheduler (Core.Sched) steps one random live driver per tick, so every interleaving and crash the doc describes is just a sequence of step() calls — which is also why every number above is reproducible from its seed.

15. PRODUCTION — from this toy to real systems

The chain: this lab's percolator → real Percolator/TiDB → Spanner / CockroachDB → and, running the other direction, the patterns that avoid the whole game.

What the toy deliberately omits, and who supplies it:

  • Replication. Our shards are objects that never die. In every real system each shard is a Raft/Paxos group (your raft lab, verbatim: Spanner = 2PC over Paxos groups; TiDB = percolator over Raft-replicated regions; CockroachDB likewise). The 2PC-lab's closing line — "the two compose" — is these systems' literal architecture. The coordinator state itself lives in a group, so the participant-side blocking problem is solved by replication even before percolator's reader-resolution.
  • The oracle's availability. TiDB's PD is a Raft group granting timestamp batches; the toy's single object stands in for all of that.
  • Version GC. Version chains grow forever here; real systems garbage- collect below a safe timestamp — the same "one open snapshot pins cleanup" story as isolation-levels' VACUUM, now cluster-wide.
  • Latency engineering. Real Percolator/TiDB add async commit and 1PC fast paths; CockroachDB's parallel commits write an explicit transaction record and prewrites concurrently, cutting a round trip. Spanner holds locks at Paxos leaders, and read-only transactions run lock-free entirely on snapshots (this lab's SnapshotReader is that).
  • Serializable-and-above. CockroachDB layers SSI-style read tracking (isolation-levels' rw-antidependency machinery) on its snapshot core, so write skew aborts instead of committing; Spanner's read-write transactions use pessimistic locks. Our PERCO row's write-skew hole is a choice real vendors patch, each in one of the two ways stage 12 priced.
  • Clock honesty. TrueTime is hardware + an eviction discipline; CockroachDB substitutes HLC (hybrid logical clocks — a Lamport counter piggybacking on wall clocks) plus per-read uncertainty restarts instead of commit wait, trading Spanner's write-side wait for occasional read-side retries — same ε, charged to a different account.

The Java/Spring face of this topic — XA/JTA. XADataSource, JTA transaction managers (Narayana, Atomikos), @Transactional spanning two resources: that is v2 of this lab — lock-based 2PC — living in your app tier. It works, and operations teams dread it for exactly the reasons you measured: in-doubt transactions holding locks (experiment 3), heuristic outcomes when humans break ties, and a transaction manager whose log is a non-replicated commit point. Modern practice: one ACID database per service; the outbox for state-change + message (its lab called 2PC "the road not taken" — now you've priced the road); sagas for multi-service flows, accepting visible intermediate states (that lab's "trades atomicity and isolation for availability" is precisely "fractured reads by design, with compensations instead of rollback"). Kafka's "exactly-once" transactions are also this lab: a transaction coordinator, markers in partitions as the commit point, and readers (read_committed consumers) that skip uncommitted data — percolator-shaped, not 2PL-shaped.

The decision ladder an architect actually walks:

  1. Can the keys live in one database? Do that. BEGIN…COMMIT plus isolation-levels knowledge beats everything in this lab.
  2. Can they be co-located onto one shard (same account family, same region)? Single-shard transactions are the fast path of every system here — TiDB 1PC, Cockroach's single-range fast path.
  3. Is "state change + notify others" the real need? Outbox.
  4. Is it a multi-step business flow that can tolerate visible intermediate states with compensation? Saga.
  5. Only now — cross-shard invariants that must never be seen broken (ledgers, uniqueness, inventory holds) — pay for a distributed transaction, and pick which currency: reader waiting (2PL/XA), oracle round trips (TiDB), or commit wait ε (Spanner-style).

What on-call sees. Commit latency p99 tracking clock ε (Spanner publishes ε; a GPS outage widens commit wait cluster-wide); "resolve lock" / transaction-retry rates (TiDB) — spikes mean crashing clients or hot keys; lock-wait queues and in-doubt XA transactions (the page at 3 a.m. is "decide this heuristic outcome"); clock-offset alarms (CockroachDB nodes suicide past max offset — a crashed node beats a lying one); and the GC/oldest-snapshot age, this lab's version chains meeting isolation-levels' VACUUM lesson at production scale.

Commonly confused, one sentence each:

  • 2PC vs distributed transaction: 2PC is one component (atomic durability); a distributed transaction is durability plus visibility plus isolation — this lab measured a system with perfect 2PC and 16 broken audits.
  • Saga vs distributed transaction: a saga chooses fractured visibility and buys availability with compensations; it is the same trade-off table, opposite corner — not a cheaper implementation of the same guarantee.
  • Snapshot vs serializable, distributed: identical to one machine — percolator ships write skew; check what you read, not just what you wrote, to close it.
  • Linearizability vs external consistency: the same real-time rule — for single operations vs whole transactions (strict serializability).
  • Percolator's primary vs 2PC's coordinator log: the same commit point, but one is data any reader can reach; that single relocation deletes the blocking problem.

18. GLOSSARY

In order of appearance:

  • shard — one independent database owning a slice of the keys.
  • dual write — committing to two systems separately and hoping; no atomicity (28/40 runs lost money under crashes).
  • two-phase commit (2PC) — prepare everywhere, log a verdict, commit everywhere; atomic durability with a blocking coordinator (prior lab).
  • atomic durability — all of a transaction's writes survive, or none.
  • atomic visibility — no reader ever observes a transaction partially applied; NOT implied by atomic durability.
  • fractured read — a read catching a transaction half-applied across shards (Omar's 150); read skew's cross-shard sibling.
  • distributed transaction — atomic durability + atomic visibility + isolation, across shards.
  • external consistency — if T1 was acknowledged before T2 began, T2 sees T1; the real-time rule for transactions (= strict serializability).
  • snapshot / consistent cut — one timestamp; read each key's newest version at or below it.
  • timestamp oracle — a single counter service defining the global time order; ~2 calls per write txn, 1 per read.
  • distributed 2PL — strict two-phase locking with lock tables on many shards; serializable, blocking, deadlock-prone.
  • distributed deadlock — a waits-for cycle whose edges live on different shards; invisible to every local detector.
  • victim — the transaction a timeout (or detector) aborts to break a deadlock; its release lets the survivor commit.
  • Percolator — Google's snapshot-isolation transaction protocol over a sharded store (TiDB's core): prewrite → primary flip → lazy cleanup.
  • prewrite — stage a value + take a lock on every written key before any commit.
  • primary / secondary — the one key holding the transaction's verdict / every other written key, whose locks point at the primary.
  • commit point — the single durable write that decides the transaction; here, replacing the primary's lock with a commit record.
  • commit record — startTs → commitTs at the primary; the verdict any reader can consult.
  • first-committer-wins — a prewrite finding a committed version newer than its snapshot aborts (isolation-levels' rule, per-shard here).
  • lock TTL — how long a lock may sit before readers may presume its owner dead and roll it back.
  • resolution / roll forward / roll back — a reader finishing or undoing a dead client's transaction by consulting the primary.
  • write skew — two snapshot transactions reading a shared rule and writing disjoint keys, jointly breaking the rule; survives distribution (doctors: on-call 0).
  • stale snapshot — a snapshot below an already-acknowledged commit timestamp; the external-consistency violation.
  • uncertainty interval (ε) — the honest error bound around a clock reading: true time ∈ [t−ε, t+ε].
  • TrueTime — Spanner's clock API returning that interval, backed by GPS + atomic clocks.
  • commit wait — holding the client's ack until commit-ts is provably past for every clock (~2ε); buys external consistency from lying clocks.
  • hybrid logical clock (HLC) — wall clock + Lamport counter; CockroachDB's TrueTime substitute (with read-side uncertainty restarts).
  • parallel commits / async commit / 1PC — production fast paths shaving commit round trips (CockroachDB, TiDB).
  • XA / JTA — the Java standard for app-tier lock-based 2PC across resources; works, blocks, pages people.
  • heuristic outcome — a human manually forcing one participant's commit/abort in a wedged XA transaction; atomicity broken by hand.
  • in-doubt transaction — a participant prepared but verdict-less; the thing heuristics get applied to.

Solutions — distributed transactions

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 2PC promises that both shards' changes end up applied or neither does — a promise about the final state of the disks. It says nothing about what other people see while it happens: the two shards commit at two different moments, and any reader arriving between them sees money that has left one account and not reached the other. The auditor notices; so does every report, fraud check, and balance API. The missing promise is atomic visibility: nobody, ever, sees half a transaction. Measured: with perfect 2PC and zero crashes, 16 of the lab's audits were wrong.

1.2 Time: give every transaction one number in a single global order, commit "at" a number, read "as of" a number — then any transaction is entirely before your snapshot or entirely after it, which kills fractured reads. Place: make the commit one local durable write (the primary's commit record) — then the verdict can be learned by anyone even when the coordinator is dead, which kills the blocking problem.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 alice — the first key the transaction writes. Nothing deeper: some deterministic choice must appoint the verdict's home, and "first write" is Percolator's.

2.2 The lock (with startTs=2), the staged value 150, and the pointer primary=alice inside the lock. A reader follows the pointer — the lock's primaryKey — to alice's shard and looks up commitRecord[2].

2.3 COMMIT PRIMARY on S0: ... commitRecord{2->3}. At that moment S1 still holds only the lock and the staged 150 — it has not heard anything — and yet the transaction is committed: any reader from now on will roll bob forward using the record.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 Yes. A transaction's commit-ts is always greater than its start-ts, so a lock born at ts=12 can only ever commit above 12 — strictly above the reader's snapshot of 10. Whether it commits or aborts, it is invisible at ts=10 by definition; the newest version ≤10 is the correct answer either way. This branch is why readers don't stall on the future.

3.2 281 vs 0. In lock-based 2PC the verdict lives in the dead coordinator's memory/log, so a reader waiting on a lock has nobody to ask; in percolator it lives in the primary's commit record, so any reader resolves the lock itself (rolling forward 47 times and back 272 in that experiment).

3.3 0 — and that is the danger. The design has no external-consistency guarantee, yet at small skew it produces zero violations, making it indistinguishable from a correct system in every test that doesn't inject skew. Same lesson as the linearizability lab's calm-network row: a green result without injected asymmetry is close to worthless.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Measured: exactly one test fails — perco_dead_client_before_flip_gets_rolled_back_by_a_reader, and on a telling assertion: the reader was supposed to wait when it met a fresh lock (blocked == null), but with TTL=2 no lock is ever "fresh" — the WAIT branch becomes unreachable and readers instantly bury possibly-live transactions. In experiment 3's crash storm the reader wait ticks drop 254 → 0 and roll-backs rise 272 → 277: patience has been replaced by murder. Real-world face: an over-aggressive lock-resolution timeout killing slow (not dead) transactions under load spikes. Safety is untouched — money never goes wrong, because the primary cell arbitrates the race and the writer's commitPrimary discovers it lost — it is liveness for writers that dies. (TiDB's fix: long transactions heartbeat their TTL upward.)

4.2 Every reader now pays a primary lookup for locks that could not possibly concern it (transactions that started after its snapshot), and readers can be made to wait on transactions that began after they did — gratuitous latency and, under a steady write stream, reader starvation. Correctness survives: the resolution rule still answers correctly in every case; the early return is purely an efficiency-and-liveness branch.

4.3 First-committer-wins is the only write-write conflict check the design has — snapshots never see each other, so this per-shard comparison is what stops two transactions overwriting each other blindly. Delete it and concurrent writers to the same key both commit, the later commit-ts silently winning: the lost update, straight from isolation-levels. Measured blast radius: 5 tests fail, not just perco_first_committer_wins_conflict_abort — the fuzz money checks go red too, because a transfer stages value-at-snapshot + delta, so an overwritten concurrent debit doesn't just lose an update, it loses money (the two-transfers-from-alice case double-counts her old balance).

Rung 5 — Implement

The rule, in the shape of YourResolver.decide:

if (lockStartTs > snapTs) return Action.READ_BELOW_SNAPSHOT;
if (primaryCommitTs != null)
    return primaryCommitTs <= snapTs ? Action.ROLL_FORWARD_THEN_READ
                                     : Action.READ_BELOW_SNAPSHOT;
if (primaryLockHeld)
    return lockAgeTicks > TTL ? Action.ROLL_BACK_THEN_READ
                              : Action.WAIT_AND_RETRY;
return Action.ROLL_BACK_THEN_READ;   // no record, no primary lock: it aborted

Verified 6/6 against the harness. The three hints decoded: the verdict lives at the primary (never guess from the secondary); a lock from a transaction younger than your snapshot is none of your business; the only case for patience is a fresh lock whose primary is still held — a live commit in progress.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 Before prewriting, the client durably records its own transaction intent keyed by a client-chosen idempotency id, including the start-ts it obtained. On retry, it first asks the primary shard for commitRecord[startTs]: present → the transfer committed, report success (and optionally finish secondaries); absent with the primary lock gone → it aborted (or was rolled back), safe to run a fresh transaction; absent with the lock still held → the original attempt may still be in flight — resolve via the normal TTL rule before retrying. The re-derived law: a timeout is not a failure; retries must be idempotent — the transactional-outbox / raft-lab rule, now with the commit record as the deduplication truth.

7.2 Order of stoppage: new write transactions stall at start-ts; then new reads stall (they need snapshots too); in-flight transactions that already hold both timestamps still finish, and lock resolution still works (it needs no oracle) — so the cluster freezes politely rather than corrupting. Escapes: (TiDB) make the oracle a Raft-replicated service that grants timestamps in batches, so its failover is an election (~seconds) and its throughput is amortized; (Spanner) remove the oracle entirely — clocks

  • ε + commit wait, paying ~2ε on every write ack instead of a round trip on every transaction.

Rung 8 — Design (worked discussion, not a single right answer)

Traffic classes. In-region transfers (95%): keep both accounts on the same shard group — single-shard ACID transactions, no distributed protocol at all (decision-ladder rungs 1–2; this is also why account→region assignment is a product decision, not just an infra one). Cross-region transfers: percolator-style snapshot transactions with primary = the debited account's shard, so the "never negative" check and the verdict live together — the balance check is a read at start-ts plus first-committer-wins on the debit row, and its strictness matters: "never negative" is a constraint on a row you WRITE, so snapshot isolation suffices (no write-skew exposure for that specific invariant; document this reasoning — if the invariant ever spans rows you only read, you need SSI or locks).

Timestamps. 50ms inter-region rules out a naive central oracle for the read path (every read pays a cross-region hop). Either (a) TiDB-shape: oracle in one region, co-locate each account's transactions with it, accept the hop for cross-region commits only, and serve in-region reads from region-local "safe timestamp" watermarks; or (b) Spanner/Cockroach-shape: HLC or bounded clocks with commit wait / uncertainty restarts. With ~ms NTP bounds and no atomic clocks, (b) means hundreds of ms of ε — likely unacceptable — so (a) with engineering, or rented Spanner, is the honest answer.

Fraud pipeline. Snapshot reads at a slightly stale timestamp (follower/watermark reads): atomic visibility without touching locks or the oracle — it must never run at read-latest (that reintroduces Omar's 150), and staleness of a few hundred ms is fine for scoring. The regulator's requirement ("confirmed ⇒ visible to any subsequent read") is external consistency and binds only confirmed-then-read flows: enforce commit wait (or read-refresh) on the confirmation path specifically.

Refuse distributed transactions for: notification/analytics fan-out (outbox + CDC), multi-step flows with human time in them — refunds, KYC, disputes (sagas with compensations; intermediate states are visible and that's correct for these), and anything expressible as a UNIQUE/CHECK constraint on one shard (isolation-levels' law: a constraint beats a protocol).

Monitor: cross-region commit p99 vs ε / oracle RTT; transaction retry

  • resolve-lock rates (hot accounts show up here first); oldest-running snapshot & version-GC lag; in-doubt/stranded-lock age; clock-offset alarms with eviction, not tolerance; and per-region ledger-sum drift checks — the auditor Omar, productionized, because the whole lab began with him.
