Lab 24 · Why Distribution Is Hard

The CAP Theorem — A Learning Lab

The three-sentence version: When your data lives on more than one computer and the network between them breaks, you must choose: refuse to answer until the network heals (stay consistent), or keep answering with possibly-stale data (stay available). You cannot do both — that impossibility is the CAP theorem. Every distributed database you will ever use has made this choice, and knowing which one it made tells you how it will hurt you.

1. WHY — why does this idea exist?

Once your application's data lives on more than one computer — for speed, for surviving crashes, or for serving users on different continents — those computers have to keep their copies in sync by talking over a network. But networks fail. Cables get cut, routers die, a whole data center goes dark for ten minutes. The CAP theorem exists because engineers kept promising systems that would stay perfectly in sync AND always answer requests, even during network failures — and kept failing to deliver. In 2000, Eric Brewer conjectured this wasn't bad engineering, it was impossible, and in 2002 Gilbert and Lynch proved it. Knowing something is impossible is incredibly useful: it stops you searching for it and makes you design for the tradeoff instead.

2. PROBLEM — the story of the two bank tellers

Imagine a small bank with two branches: one downtown, one uptown. Your account balance is written in a ledger book, and each branch keeps its own copy so you can walk into either one. Whenever a teller changes a balance, she phones the other branch so both books stay identical.

You have $100. One stormy day, the phone line between the branches goes dead.

You walk into the downtown branch: "I'd like to withdraw $100." At the same moment, your roommate (who shares the account) walks into the uptown branch: "I'd like to withdraw $100."

Each teller looks at her book. Each book says $100. Neither teller can call the other branch to check or to announce the withdrawal.

What should the tellers do?

3. NAIVE SOLUTION — "just keep both books updated"

The bank's original plan was exactly that: every change is phoned to the other branch immediately, so both books always match, and every customer is served at either branch instantly. When the phone line works, this plan is fine.

4. FAILURE — the storm breaks the plan

With the line dead, each teller has exactly two options, and both hurt:

  • Option 1 — serve the customer anyway. Both you and your roommate walk out with $100 each. Total withdrawn: $200 from a $100 account. When the line is repaired, the books disagree ($0 downtown, $0 uptown, but the bank is missing $100). The bank stayed open but its books became wrong.
  • Option 2 — refuse until the line is fixed. The teller says "Sorry, I can't safely do this right now, come back later." The books stay correct but the bank was effectively closed to you.

There is no third option. The teller cannot both give a guaranteed-correct answer and serve you, because being correct requires information sitting on the other side of a dead phone line. That's the entire theorem, in a storm.

5. KEY IDEA

When replicas of your data cannot communicate, every request forces a choice: answer possibly-wrongly, or don't answer. Correctness and responsiveness cannot both survive a communication failure.

6. TERMINOLOGY — the same story, in professional words

Now let's name the pieces the way engineers do. Every term defined at first use:

Term Plain-English meaning (in our story)
Node / replica One computer holding a copy of the data (one bank branch with its ledger book).
Replication Keeping copies of the same data on several nodes (the phone-call protocol between branches).
Network partition A network failure that splits nodes into groups that cannot talk to each other, while each group is still running (the dead phone line — both branches are open, they just can't communicate).
Consistency (the C in CAP) Every read returns the most recent write — as if there were only ONE ledger book that everyone shares. Formally called linearizability. ⚠️ Not the same "C" as in ACID transactions (that one means "no rule of the database is violated").
Availability (the A in CAP) Every request sent to any working node gets a real (non-error) answer, without waiting for the partition to heal (no teller ever says "come back later").
Partition tolerance (the P in CAP) The system keeps operating in some form even when a partition happens (the bank as a whole doesn't burn down just because the phone line died).
CP system A system that, during a partition, chooses Consistency: some requests get refused or blocked (Option 2 — the teller says no).
AP system A system that, during a partition, chooses Availability: all requests get answers, some possibly stale or conflicting (Option 1 — everyone gets their $100).
Split-brain The dangerous situation where both sides of a partition keep accepting writes, each believing it's authoritative — the two ledger books diverging.
Quorum A majority of the nodes; enough that any two majorities always share at least one member. A common trick for building CP systems: only the side of the partition holding a majority may proceed.
Eventual consistency The AP world's consolation prize: replicas may disagree during the partition, but once it heals, they reconcile and converge to the same value — eventually.
Stale read A read that returns an old value because the node answering hasn't heard about a newer write.

7. MENTAL MODEL — two islands

Carry this picture: a partition turns your cluster into two islands with no boats between them. A write lands on Island 1. Now someone on Island 2 asks to read that data. There are only two honest behaviors:

  • Island 2 says "I can't answer safely" → you preserved consistency, sacrificed availability. CP.
  • Island 2 answers with what it has (the old value) → you preserved availability, sacrificed consistency. AP.

And the most important correction to the popular "pick 2 of 3" picture: partitions are not optional. You don't get to "not pick P" — networks will partition; that's physics and backhoes, not a design choice. A "CA system" would be one that never experiences partitions, which means a system that isn't distributed at all (one node — one ledger book). So the real decision is never "which 2 of 3?" It is:

When (not if) a partition happens, do we give up C or A?

9. ACTUAL MECHANISM — the proof, with real numbers

CAP isn't a rule of thumb; it's a two-paragraph proof (Gilbert & Lynch, 2002). Walk it with concrete values:

  1. Setup: nodes G1 and G2, both storing balance = 100. The network between them partitions. Assume the system claims to be both consistent and available during this partition.
  2. Client 1 sends write balance = 150 to G1. Because the system is available, G1 must complete it and answer OK. The replication message to G2 cannot arrive — the partition eats it.
  3. Client 2 sends read balance to G2. Because the system is available, G2 must answer with a real value, now, without hearing from G1. The only value G2 has is 100.
  4. G2 answers 100. But the most recent completed write was 150. That read is stale — consistency is violated. The only way G2 could have avoided it was to block or error — violating availability.

Contradiction either way. So no system can guarantee all three of C, A, P. Notice the proof needs only two nodes, one write, and one read — the impossibility is that fundamental. Everything real systems do (quorums, leaders, retries, clever clocks) only decides which guarantee bends and how gracefully — never escapes the choice.

How real designs pick a side, mechanically:

  • CP via quorum: with N replicas, require a majority (⌊N/2⌋+1) to acknowledge each write, and a majority to serve each read. Any two majorities overlap in at least one node, so every read intersects the latest write. During a partition, the minority side can't assemble a majority → it refuses. Correct, but partially unavailable.
  • AP via accept-locally: every node accepts reads and writes with what it has, records them (often with version metadata), and syncs when the partition heals. Always answers, but reads can be stale and concurrent writes conflict.

A tiny code sketch of the fork in the road (pseudo-Python, not a project — the interactive simulator is the real "runnable" here):

def handle_write(node, key, value):
    acks = replicate_to_peers(node, key, value, timeout=1.0)
    if mode == "CP":
        if acks + 1 < majority(N):          # couldn't reach a quorum
            raise Unavailable("partition: refusing rather than diverge")
        commit(node, key, value)             # safe: any future quorum read sees this
    else:  # AP
        commit(node, key, value)             # answer now, sync later
        queue_for_reconciliation(key, value) # divergence is now possible

The R + W > N rule generalizes this: with N replicas, W acks per write and R nodes per read, reads are guaranteed to see the latest write iff the read and write sets must overlap — R + W > N. Cassandra exposes exactly these knobs (ONE, QUORUM, ALL), letting you slide between CP-ish and AP-ish per query.

10. FAILURE SCENARIOS — walking through the ways it hurts

Scenario 1 — the stale read (AP). Partition splits {G1} from {G2}. User updates their shipping address via G1; the confirmation page (served from G2) shows the old address. Harmless-looking, until it's a password change that "didn't take" on half the site.

Scenario 2 — split-brain writes (AP). During the partition, you write balance = 0 (withdrew $100) on Island 1; your roommate writes balance = 0 on Island 2. The partition heals. Both islands now sync… two different histories for the same account. The system must reconcile:

  • Last-write-wins (LWW): keep the write with the newest timestamp, silently discard the other. Simple — and quietly loses data (one withdrawal vanishes from history; the bank eats the loss).
  • Keep both, ask the application: version metadata (vector clocks) detects the conflict and hands both values to the app (Amazon's Dynamo did this — the shopping cart merged by union, which is why deleted items sometimes reappeared).
  • Design values to merge themselves: CRDTs — data types (counters, sets) built so concurrent updates merge deterministically. /learn crdt later.

Scenario 3 — the minority island (CP). A 5-node quorum system partitions 3-vs-2. The 3-side keeps a majority and keeps working; every client routed to the 2-side gets errors until the partition heals. Users on the wrong side of the backhoe see an outage — by design. This is also why quorum clusters have odd node counts: 4 nodes can split 2-2 and then nobody has a majority — the whole system stalls.

Scenario 4 — retries turn one write into two. Client writes to G1, the ACK is lost (partitions eat answers too, not just requests), client retries against G2. The write happened twice. CAP-world systems need idempotency (safe-to-repeat operations) on top of everything else — a preview of /learn idempotency and /learn transactional-outbox.

Scenario 5 — the partition heals. CP systems: minority catches up from the majority, life resumes; nothing to reconcile because the minority never accepted anything. AP systems: anti-entropy kicks in — replicas exchange version metadata (hashes/Merkle trees), detect divergence, run the reconciliation policy from Scenario 2. The pain of an AP system is deferred, not avoided.

11. CORRECTNESS — what CAP does and does not say

The guarantee, precisely: in an asynchronous network (messages can be delayed or lost, no shared clock), no read-write system can guarantee both linearizable consistency and total availability under arbitrary partitions. It is a safety vs liveness tradeoff:

  • Consistency is a safety property — "nothing bad ever happens" (no read ever returns stale data).
  • Availability is a liveness property — "something good eventually happens, for every request" (every request gets an answer).
  • A partition forces you to break one. Pick which, per system — or even per operation.

What CAP does NOT say — the famous misreadings:

  1. "Pick 2 of 3." No — P isn't optional (stage 7). The choice is C-vs-A, and only during partitions.
  2. "An AP system is never consistent." No — the theorem constrains behavior during partitions. Most of the time there is no partition, and the tradeoff that dominates daily life is latency vs consistency: waiting for replicas before ACKing costs milliseconds on every write. PACELC captures both: if Partitioned, choose A or C; Else, choose Latency or Consistency. (/learn pacelc.)
  3. "C means ACID." No — CAP's C is linearizability (single-copy illusion); ACID's C is "constraints hold." Unrelated claims sharing a letter.
  4. "Availability = high availability / five nines." No — CAP availability is a strict formal property (every request to any live node answers). A CP system can still have superb uptime in practice; it just can't guarantee answers under partition.
  5. "It's binary." Real systems are dials, not switches: R/W/N quorum knobs, per-query consistency levels, bounded-staleness modes. CAP marks the ends of the dial; engineering picks the position.

12–13. CODE & EXPERIMENT → the simulator

For a theorem there is no protocol to implement — the "lab equipment" is the interactive simulator, visualization.html: three replicas holding one bank balance, two customers, a partition switch you flip yourself, CP/AP modes, and R/W quorum sliders. Every event is narrated in plain English ("Customer R was shown the wrong balance") with the protocol detail underneath, and a scoreboard counts the two casualties separately: times a customer saw wrong data (C violated) and times a customer was refused (A violated). Four guided experiments play themselves if you press Run this for me.

Two controls carry more weight than they look like:

  • Which machine each customer talks to. Nothing about a customer decides whether they experience the outage — only which replica their connection happens to land on. Move customer L from machine A to machine B before cutting the network and L's outage disappears entirely, while the storm is identical. This is why real clients are handed a list of nodes to try.
  • Where the network tears ({A} | {B,C} vs {A,B} | {C}). Same cluster, same mode, same requests — the tear location alone decides who is refused.

Both are exercises 2.2 and 2.2b in EXERCISES.md.

14. PRODUCTION — where the real systems sit

System Choice during partition How it does it
ZooKeeper, etcd, Consul CP Raft/ZAB consensus; the majority side elects a leader and serves; the minority refuses. Used exactly where wrong answers are worse than no answers: locks, leader election, config.
Cassandra, DynamoDB (Dynamo lineage), Riak AP (tunable) Accept-locally + anti-entropy; per-query consistency levels (ONE…QUORUM…ALL) move individual reads/writes along the C↔A dial.
MongoDB CP-leaning Single-primary replica set; a partitioned-away primary steps down, minority side stops accepting writes; read preferences can relax C for reads.
Kafka Configurable In-sync-replica (ISR) machinery: acks=all + min.insync.replicas=2 ≈ CP for produce; acks=1 trades toward availability/latency and can lose acknowledged writes on failover.
Google Spanner CP (marketed "effectively CA") Still refuses when it must — but Google's private network plus TrueTime (GPS/atomic-clock time bounds) makes partitions so rare that users almost never observe the C-vs-A moment. The theorem isn't beaten; the P is engineered toward zero.
Plain old single-node Postgres Not in the game One node — no partition between replicas is possible. This is the "CA" corner, and the price is that it's not distributed (its replication add-ons re-enter the game: synchronous ≈ CP, asynchronous ≈ AP).

The production question is never "is this database CP or AP?" as trivia — it's "when the network partitions at 3 a.m., which page do I get: 'error rate up' (CP) or 'customers seeing wrong data' (AP)?" Pick the page you'd rather receive, per use case: payments and inventory counters usually CP; shopping carts, likes, presence indicators usually AP.

16. RELATED CONCEPTS

  • PACELC — CAP's needed upgrade: adds the no-partition tradeoff (latency vs consistency) that dominates normal operation.
  • Consensus (Raft/Paxos) — the machinery that makes the CP choice safely: how a majority side agrees on a leader and a log. CAP says you must sometimes refuse; Raft is how you refuse correctly.
  • Eventual consistency & CRDTs — the machinery that makes the AP choice livable: how diverged replicas converge without losing your shopping cart.
  • ACID vs BASE — single-database transaction guarantees vs the "Basically Available, Soft-state, Eventually consistent" posture of AP stores; CAP is the why behind BASE's existence.
  • Linearizability vs serializability — CAP's C is linearizability (one object, real-time order); serializability is about multi-object transactions. Different guarantees, frequently confused.

CAP Theorem — Solutions

Part 1 — Predict

1.1 No — the read returns the new value. With a healthy network, W=1 still triggers asynchronous replication to the other replicas (in the simulator it lands everywhere immediately; in real systems within milliseconds). R+W ≤ N means staleness is possible (no overlap is guaranteed), not that it's certain. This is why AP systems look perfectly consistent in demos and dev environments: the tradeoff only becomes visible under partition or replication lag. CAP constrains the worst case, not the average day.

1.2

  • L write → REFUSED. Island 1 is {A} alone: 1 reachable < W=2.
  • L read → REFUSED. 1 reachable < R=2.
  • R write → OK. Island 2 is {B,C}: 2 ≥ W=2. The write commits on the majority side.
  • R read → OK, and consistent. B and C both have the latest write.

Island 1's customers experience the outage; island 2's customers never notice anything. A CP partition outage is partial — it hits whoever is stuck with the minority. This is why real CP systems put replicas in 3+ failure domains: to make the majority side as reachable as possible.

1.3 Nobody can write — W=3 needs all three nodes, and no island has three. Both sides can read (R=1). No stale read is possible: reads may return old data in wall-clock terms, but no newer committed write can exist anywhere — writes are frozen — so every read returns the latest committed value. R+W=4 > 3 still holds, and the guarantee holds with it; you paid with total write unavailability. (This is roughly "read-only mode during incidents," a real production pattern.)

1.4 W=1, R=3 optimizes for fast, always-available writes — a write needs only one node. Readers pay: every read must assemble all three replicas, so during the partition no one can read at all (no island has 3 nodes), while writes keep landing on both sides — diverging. It's the mirror image of 1.3. Write-heavy ingest systems (metrics, logs) pick shapes like this; interactive systems rarely do, because reads are what users feel.

Part 2 — Modify

2.1 W=1 (the only value ≤ the minority island's size of 1). Both islands then accept writes independently — you've enabled split-brain. The bill arrives at heal time: experiment 4's CONFLICT line, where last-write-wins discards an acknowledged write. Allowing writes everywhere during a partition and keeping every write are incompatible wishes; you get to keep at most one.

2.2 Nobody is refused. Island {A,B} has 2 nodes ≥ quorum, so L (on A) works normally. R talks to C — alone on its island, so C should be refused for both reads and writes (1 < 2)... and that's the answer: R is refused, L is fine — the outage moved to whoever is attached to the minority. The point of the exercise: which clients suffer depends entirely on where the network happens to tear, not on anything about the clients. Route-around logic (retry against the other island) is how real clients cope.

2.2b Because a client pinned to one node inherits that node's fate: a single fixed endpoint turns "one replica is on the wrong side of the tear" into "this customer is down," even when a perfectly healthy majority is sitting one hop away. Giving the client a node list (or a smart driver, as Cassandra and MongoDB do) lets it retry elsewhere and ride out the storm. What it does not buy you in a CP system: if every node the client can reach sits on the minority side, retrying finds no quorum anywhere and the client is still down — routing can move you to the majority, but it cannot manufacture one. That residual outage is exactly the A that CP gave up.

2.3 With N=5, W=3: the majority side keeps writing as long as it holds ≥3 nodes, so up to 2 nodes can be cut away. With N=4, W=3 under a 2-2 split, neither side has 3 nodes: the entire system stops accepting writes even though all 4 machines are healthy. Even N buys you extra hardware and zero extra fault tolerance (N=4 tolerates the same 1-node-loss as N=3, with a new total-stall failure mode added). Hence 3, 5, 7 everywhere in ZooKeeper / etcd deployments.

Part 3 — Debug

3.1 Flaw 1 — "a really good network" is not a property you can guarantee. Partitions come from switch firmware, misconfigured firewalls, GC pauses that make a node look partitioned, overloaded NICs, and backhoes. P is decided by physics and operations, not by the designer's optimism; a distributed system that hasn't chosen its partition behavior has still chosen — it just doesn't know which. Flaw 2 — the design already contradicts itself under its own failure: "replicate synchronously" means a write must reach both replicas, so the moment the network does hiccup, writes block — that's giving up A (it's a CP design wearing a CA badge). If it instead kept serving writes from both replicas during the hiccup, it would diverge — giving up C. The claim never survives its first partition; the only honest version of "CA" is a single node.

3.2 Mechanism: the email write landed on replica set X; the password-reset service read from a replica that hadn't received it — a stale read in an AP store. The confirmation page read its own write (likely served by the same node that took it), which masked the divergence from the user. No errors is the signature of choosing A: an AP system's failure mode is wrong answers delivered successfully, so nothing throws — the system is working exactly as configured. (A CP system's signature is the opposite: errors everywhere, data wrong nowhere.) The two knobs from this lab: raise R and W so that R+W > N (quorum reads/writes for the email field — cost: latency on every operation, unavailability of the minority side during partitions), or serve security-critical reads from the leader/primary only (cost: the leader becomes a bottleneck and its island's failure takes reads down). Ten minutes is long for replication lag — the report also hints anti-entropy wasn't running; read-repair only fixes a value after something reads it with quorum.

3.3 The sequence in 3.3 healed with only ONE island having written — nothing conflicted, so nothing was lost, and the OK read proves nothing about safety. The counterexample is experiment 4's sequence: AP → cut → L write (bal=150) → R write (bal=200) → heal. Both writes were acknowledged OK to their clients; the heal line reads CONFLICT … last-write-wins kept bal=200 … bal=150 DISCARDED. That line is the smoking gun: client L was told "OK" and its write no longer exists anywhere. "Consistent after healing" (convergence) guarantees replicas agree, not that they agree on a history that kept your write. Eventual consistency promises a value — not your value.
