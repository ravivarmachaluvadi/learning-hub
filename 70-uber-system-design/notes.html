Lab 70 · Whole Systems

Uber — how a moving city full of cars meets a moving city full of people

Tell a friend: Meera is standing outside a metro station at nine at night. Dev is sitting in an empty car eight hundred metres away. Neither of them can find the other, and that is the whole business. Two ideas solve it. File every car by which square of the map it is standing in, so "who is near me?" becomes a lookup instead of a search. Then treat a match not as an answer you looked up but as a car you grab. Only one person can win that grab, and a timer takes the car back if nobody confirms. Payments, ratings and receipts are ordinary backend work you already know. The one thing you may never do is promise the same car to two people.

Go and break it yourself: visualization.html is the Dispatch Console, also published at https://claude.ai/code/artifact/7c601922-ea5c-4d46-946a-79bf6a489ff0.

1. Why is finding each other the whole business?

In one breath: A car with nobody in it is parked near a person with nowhere to sit. Neither of them can see the other. Every hard thing in this lab is that one sentence.

Meera comes out of the metro station at 9 p.m. Somewhere within two kilometres of her there is a car with nobody in it. Dev is behind the wheel. He would very much like to earn her fare.

Neither of them can find the other.

That is the entire business. Not the app. Not the map. Not the payment. The finding.

Before anyone solved it, Meera phoned a taxi office. A person with a paper map guessed who was closest. That person radioed a driver. Meera waited twenty minutes to discover the guess was wrong. Meanwhile Dev spent something like a third to a half of his working hours driving around empty, looking for her.

If nobody solves this, three things go wrong at once. Riders wait too long. Drivers earn too little for each hour on the road. And the number of cars a city needs, to move the same number of people, goes up.

Those three are not three problems. They are one problem wearing three faces.

2. What comes apart in Rani's evening?

In one breath: One clever person with a paper map is better than any app when there are five cars. At five hundred cars she breaks, and she breaks in four separate ways that never go away.

Meet Rani. She works at a taxi office. On the wall is a paper map of the city, and beside it a box of coloured pins. Every driver radios in when they move, and Rani moves their pin. When the phone rings, she looks at the map, finds the pin nearest the caller, and radios that driver.

With five cars this works beautifully. Rani is faster and kinder than any app.

Then the company grows to five hundred cars. Rani's evening comes apart in four separate ways, all in the same hour.

One: she cannot look at five hundred pins. Finding the nearest pin to a caller means her eyes crossing the whole map, on every call, while the pins keep moving.

Two: Meera and Priya ring at the same moment. Rani is halfway through promising car 47 to Meera when Priya's call comes in. She promises car 47 to Priya as well. Both women are told a car is coming. One of them is wrong, and neither of them knows which one.

Three: a pin lies. Omar drove home an hour ago and forgot to radio in. His pin is still on the map. It is still the nearest pin to somebody. It is still being sent to people who then wait for a car that is not coming.

Four: car 47 never answers the radio. Ben is driving it, and his radio is off. Rani has mentally set his car aside for Meera, so nobody else may have it. It stays set aside until Rani remembers, which she does not, because the phone is ringing again.

Hold on to all four. Every serious idea in this lab is one of Rani's four problems, solved once, at a size where you cannot solve it by paying attention.

🔮 Predict: Asha, an engineer, buys Rani a computer and types every driver's position into a table. Which of the four problems does that fix?

Reveal **The first one only, and only for a while.** A computer scans five hundred rows far faster than Rani's eyes cross a map.

It fixes nothing else. It still has to be told what to do when Meera and Priya arrive in the same millisecond (problem two). It still has no idea that Omar's row is a lie (problem three). And it will happily leave Ben's car set aside forever (problem four).

If you guessed "all four, that is what computers are for", you have made the most common mistake in this subject. The scan is the problem people expect. The other three are the problems that actually bite. They get worse, not better, when the system gets faster and more concurrent.

3. What is the naive program, and is it actually wrong?

In one breath: Asha computerises Rani exactly as Rani works. Fifteen lines. It is the right thing to write first, and it is broken in three places.

Here is what any competent engineer writes first, Asha included. Writing it is not a mistake. Not measuring it is.

one table:  driver_id | latitude | longitude | is_free

when a ride is requested:
    best = null
    for every row in the table:                 # look at all of them
        if row.is_free and distance(row, rider) < distance(best, rider):
            best = row
    mark best.is_free = false                   # take it
    tell the rider about best

Read that as English. Look at every car. Keep the nearest free one. Mark it taken. Tell the rider.

On a whiteboard it is correct. Every rider gets the nearest free car. It is about fifteen lines. Ship it.

The third crack: how do two riders end up with the same car?

🔮 Predict: Two hundred riders, two hundred free cars, and a matcher that checks is_free and then writes. How many riders are promised a car that somebody else also got? Guess a number, not a range.

Reveal About a hundred. Half.

If you guessed "one or two, it is a rare race", that is the intuition this experiment exists to destroy. The gap between the check and the write is not a narrow window that a request occasionally slips through. Under contention it is where most requests live.

If you guessed zero because there are exactly as many cars as riders, you have assumed the riders take turns. Nothing in the code makes them.

  READ-THEN-WRITE:   riders told "your car is on the way" : 200
                     cars actually reserved               : 102
                     riders promised a car someone else has:  98

  COMPARE-AND-SET:   riders told "your car is on the way" : 200
                     cars actually reserved               : 200
                     riders promised a car someone else has:   0

Roughly half of the riders were promised a car that belonged to somebody else. 98 in the run above, 100 in the next run. The exact count depends on thread scheduling. It is never small.

Now look at Asha's code again. The bug is not in a line. It checks is_free, and then it writes. Those two lines are simply not one action. Another request fits perfectly in the gap between them.

Three different things volunteer to create that gap: a thread switch, a garbage collection pause, or a network round trip to the database.

This is Rani promising car 47 to Meera and to Priya, mechanised, and running a thousand times a second.

5. What has to be true, whatever technology you pick?

In one breath: Strip away every product name and five sentences are left. The second one is the sentence that makes this lab different from every "find nearby things" tutorial you have read.

Asha closes her laptop and writes on paper. No databases, no frameworks. Just what must be true for Meera to get a car and for Dev to get paid.

  1. "Who is near here?" must be answerable in single-digit milliseconds, while positions change hundreds of thousands of times per second.
  2. Each car is handed to at most one rider at a time. This must hold even when Meera's tap and Priya's tap land in the same millisecond, on two different machines.
  3. No car is ever stuck reserved. The reservation must release itself, whatever it is waiting on: Ben's thumb, Ben's phone, or the server that asked him.
  4. A position is trusted only as long as it is fresh. Omar's car stopped reporting, and a car that stopped reporting is not a car you can send.
  5. All of that keeps holding while pieces fail, because at this size something is always failing.

Requirement 2 is the one that separates this from every "find nearby things" tutorial. Everything after this point is the pursuit of these five sentences.

6. What are the two ideas everything else follows from?

In one breath: Stop searching for nearness and file by it instead. And stop treating a match as something you compute, because a match is something you take.

Two sentences. The whole rest of the lab is their consequences.

Idea one: stop searching for nearness, index by it. Chop the map into fixed squares. Keep a set of cars per square. "Who is near me?" stops being a scan over the city and becomes a lookup of a handful of keys.

Idea two: a match is not a query result, it is a claim. "The nearest free car" is a fact that stops being true the instant somebody else acts on it. So the match is not computed and then applied. It is taken, in one indivisible step that exactly one request can win, and it is taken on a deadline so nothing can hold it forever.

Idea one answers the first crack. Idea two answers the third crack, and its deadline answers Rani's fourth problem, the one where Ben's car sits set aside all evening.

Freshness answers the second crack. A position becomes a fact with an expiry date. That falls out of idea two the moment you stop seeing supply as something stored and start seeing it as something leased.

Say it back. Without using the words index, atomic or lease, tell a friend why "find the nearest free car and mark it busy" is two mistakes, not one.

A good answer sounds like "The first mistake is looking at every car in the city to find the near ones. That is like reading every name in the phone book to find your neighbour. Instead, file the cars by which part of town they are in.

The second mistake is looking and then grabbing. In between the looking and the grabbing, somebody else grabbed. So you have to grab in a way that either succeeds or tells you plainly that you lost. And you have to let go by yourself, on a timer, in case you forget."

7. Which words do you need now, and what did each one cost to earn?

In one breath: Ten words. You have already watched every one of them matter, which is the only reason they are worth learning.

Terminology dumped in a block is a wall. So these come in three groups, in the order the story needed them. First, the words for the two sides of the market. Then the words for the filing cabinet. Last, the words for taking a car without lying to anybody.

Group one: what do you call the two sides?

Rani does not own the cars. That sounds obvious and it changes everything.

Dev can decline a trip. He can go offline in the middle of a thought. He can accept a rival company's fare while Rani's radio call is still in the air. The cars are supply, the waiting riders are demand, and a system in which neither side is under your control is a marketplace.

The word exists to stop you modelling Dev as a row in a table. A row does what you write to it. Supply does not.

You can watch the difference in the concert scene of Sim.java. Nine people request a ride. Zero cars are claimable. No amount of correct code creates a tenth car.

The act of choosing which car goes to which rider is dispatch, also called matching. Uber's own system for it is called DISCO. Dispatch is a decision, and it is deliberately kept separate from the state of where the cars are. The index knows where Dev is. Dispatch decides what to do about it. Those two things fail differently and scale differently, so they get different names and, in section 11, different services.

Group two: what do you call the filing cabinet?

A geospatial index is a filing system where things are filed by where they are. Said precisely, it maps a region of space to the set of entities inside it, with cheap updates and proximity queries whose cost is bounded.

Why does this need its own word? Because an ordinary database index answers "which rows have this value?" Nearness is not a value. Meera and Dev can be close without sharing a single column.

One square of the map is a cell. Its id is the key a driver is filed under, and a driver is in exactly one cell at a time. This is the whole trick, and it is worth saying slowly: a cell turns a continuous question ("who is within 500 metres?") into a discrete one ("who is in these nine keys?").

Looking in your own square, then the ring of squares around it, then the next ring out, is ring search, or neighbour search. It exists because searching only the rider's own cell is a bug that looks reassuring. Section 10 puts a number on it: 44.9% of requests find nobody at all while a car sits just across the line.

Last in this group is the one that catches people out. ETA is the estimated time of arrival: how many minutes until the car is actually at the kerb. It is the ranking key, and it comes from a road graph and live traffic, not from straight-line distance.

Time is what Meera experiences. Distance is what the geometry gives you free. A car 200 m away across a river is not 200 m away.

Group three: what do you call taking a car without lying to anybody?

An offer is "this car is being held for you, briefly". Precisely: a lease over one driver, with an expiry, carrying an id that later actions must present.

The word earns itself against its rival. An assignment is permanent. An offer is not, because Ben has not agreed yet and might never agree. A plain is_free boolean cannot represent "held, pending a human", and that missing state is exactly why Rani lost car 47 for a whole evening.

A claim is taking something in a way that fails loudly if somebody else took it first. Precisely: an atomic conditional state change from AVAILABLE to OFFERED, applied only if the current state is still AVAILABLE, with the caller told whether it won. Engineers call the primitive compare-and-set.

The word names the difference between reading a fact and acting on it. Read-then-write has a gap. A claim has none. In Dispatch.java it is one line, d.state.compareAndSet(AVAILABLE, OFFERED), and that line is the whole distance between 98 double-bookings and 0.

A heartbeat is Dev's car saying "still here" every few seconds. Freshness, or TTL, is the age past which the system stops believing the last thing it heard. Supply is a lease that a location ping renews, and a record older than the TTL is simply not offered to anybody.

Why bother? Because "is this driver online?" is a question no distributed system can answer. "Did this driver report in the last 30 seconds?" is a question every node can answer on its own, from data it already holds. Swapping the first question for the second is the entire move.

Finally, surge. Prices go up where too many people want cars at once. It is a per-area multiplier from local demand over local supply, applied to the fare.

It is easiest to understand as a control signal that happens to be delivered as a price. With a fixed price, an under-supplied area has exactly one behaviour available to it: an unbounded queue. A queue does not create cars.

Which of these words have you already met under another name?

Several of these are old friends in new clothes. Naming the resemblance is worth more than memorising the definitions.

The geospatial index does the same job as the structures in btree-and-lsm: stop the scan. It answers a different question. A B-tree orders one dimension and answers ranges over it. Nearness is two-dimensional, and no ordering of a single column captures it.

The cell is the hash slot from redis Cluster and the ring position from consistent-hashing, in a different space. A key's slot is fixed by hashing. Dev's cell is fixed by geography. Both exist so a lookup costs one hop.

One difference between them matters enormously. Hash space is deliberately uniform. Geography is emphatically not. That is why the hot cell in section 12 has no equivalent of virtual nodes to save it.

The offer is the lease and the fencing token from redis §4, living in their natural habitat. An offer id fences a stale accept exactly the way a fencing token fences a stale lock holder.

The claim is the compare-and-set of java-memory-model, except across machines rather than across threads in one JVM. It is also Redis's single-threaded INCR: the same indivisibility, bought a different way. And it is the cheap cousin of two-phase-commit. There is one resource here, so there is no coordinator to run.

Freshness is the TTL from caching, and it is the central point of cap-theorem restated for cars. You cannot tell "gone" from "slow", so you decide by the clock and accept that the clock is sometimes wrong.

Supply that can refuse you is the over-admission problem from distributed-rate-limiting. Both are about a scarce resource that several requesters reach for at the same instant.

Ring search is the one genuinely new thing here. Nothing you have learned so far has it.

8. What two pictures should you keep in your head?

In one breath: A wooden pegboard with a hook for every square of the map, and a state machine with four states. They are the same machine. One of them you can hold; the other one is true.

The simple picture: what did Rani leave behind?

Rani retires. In the back room she leaves a pegboard.

The city map is drawn on it, divided into 500-metre squares, and every square has a hook. Each car has a wooden token. A car's token hangs on the hook for the square that car is standing in. When Dev drives into a new square, his token moves from one hook to the next.

Now watch four things become physical.

Asha asks "who is near Meera?" She looks at Meera's hook, then the eight hooks around it, then outward, until she has enough tokens. She never looks at the whole board.

Asha makes a match. She physically takes the token off the hook. You cannot take a token that is not there. Two clerks reaching for Dev's token is settled by whose hand arrives first, and the loser plainly sees an empty hook.

Asha makes an offer. The taken token goes into a small tray with a 15-second sand timer. If the timer runs out and nobody has confirmed, the token goes back on its hook. That happens whether or not anyone is watching the tray.

Asha deals with Omar's dark car. Every token must be re-stamped every 30 seconds. Unstamped tokens are ignored, even though they are still hanging there in plain sight.

The engineering picture: what are the four states?

Same machine, said precisely. A driver is always in exactly one of four states, and the arrows are the only legal ways to move between them.

stateDiagram-v2
    [*] --> OFFLINE
    OFFLINE --> AVAILABLE : driver goes online (enters the index)
    AVAILABLE --> OFFERED : compareAndSet by ONE matcher — the claim
    OFFERED --> AVAILABLE : declined, or the lease expires
    OFFERED --> ON_TRIP : driver accepts, presenting a valid offer id
    ON_TRIP --> AVAILABLE : trip completed (re-indexed at the drop-off)
    AVAILABLE --> OFFLINE : goes offline (leaves the index)
    note right of OFFERED
      Exactly one trip may hold a driver here.
      The lease is what makes that safe to promise.
    end note

The whole system is three pieces of state, six kinds of message, and four rules that must never be broken.

  • State: cellId → Set<driverId>, driverId → (position, lastPing, state, offer), tripId → (rider, pickup, state, driver). In words: which cars are in each square, everything known about one car, and everything known about one trip.
  • Messages: location ping, ride request, offer, accept or decline, expiry tick, trip events.
  • Invariants: four rules that may never break.
    • (i) A driver is in at most one cell.
    • (ii) A driver is in OFFERED for at most one trip.
    • (iii) Every OFFERED has an expiry in the future, or is reclaimed.
    • (iv) A trip has at most one driver.

Where does the pegboard lie to you?

🔮 Predict: You have the pegboard in your head. Name the single thing about it that is most dangerously untrue of a real system.

Reveal **It is in one room, with one pair of hands.** Everything comfortable about the pegboard comes from that.

If you said "the squares are too regular", you are right and it is the second answer below. It costs you performance. The one-room assumption costs you correctness, which is worse.

Read these four before you trust the pegboard anywhere important.

The board is not in one room. A real index is spread across many machines, and taking a token is a network round trip. So the indivisibility has to live where the state lives, in the store or in a single owner process per driver. A compareAndSet on your service's local copy of a remote driver proves nothing at all.

Tokens never lie; pins do. A token on a hook is unarguably there. Dev's last position is a claim made by a phone, on a mobile network, seconds ago, possibly out of order. Freshness rules exist because of that gap, and the pegboard cannot show it to you.

One clerk becomes hundreds. The pegboard's safety comes from physics and from Asha having only two hands. The real thing has hundreds of matchers running at once, which is why every safe step has to be made indivisible on purpose.

Squares are not neighbourhoods. A hook holds a fixed square. Real cities are wildly uneven. Experiment 5 puts 6,399 cars in one 500-metre square at an airport and zero in the square next to it. The pegboard quietly suggests a uniformity that does not exist.

v5 — why offer a car instead of assigning it?

The first is Meera and Priya again. Around 98 of 200 riders were promised somebody else's car in experiment 3, and the count varies run to run while the half does not. The second is Ben. He has not agreed to anything yet. He may decline, or leave the phone face-down in his pocket. So the taken state is not BUSY. It is OFFERED: a reservation belonging to one trip, held on behalf of a human who has not answered.

v6 — what if the matcher dies holding the reservation?

Reveal With v5 exactly as written: it stays `OFFERED` forever. It is invisible to every future match, and no code path exists to release it, because the only thing that knew about the offer has died.

Do that a few thousand times a day and supply quietly bleeds away. Rani's fourth problem, at machine speed.

The fix is not "make the matcher not crash". If you answered that, you are designing for the failure instead of around it. The fix is to make the reservation expire by itself: an offer carries expiresAt, and a housekeeping tick returns any expired offer to AVAILABLE.

Now notice what one mechanism bought. The same code path recovers from Ben ignoring the popup, from Ben's phone having no signal, from Ben's app crashing, and from the matcher dying. Whenever one deadline covers four unrelated failures, you have found the right abstraction.

The offer also carries an id, and accept must present it. Without that, Ben tapping a stale popup from a previous request starts a trip that no longer exists. Stale popups are not exotic; they are what a patchy network produces every day.

The test is an_offer_from_an_old_popup_cannot_start_a_trip. This is the fencing token from the redis lab, in its natural habitat.

v7 — why not show the request to every nearby driver?

The obvious way to fill a car fast is to buzz every nearby driver and let the first tap win.

It is also how you get every phone in the neighbourhood buzzing for every request, nine drivers driving toward a fare that eight of them will lose, and a race on every single accept.

Sequential offers with a short deadline give up a little speed and buy predictability. Real systems do sometimes broadcast on purpose, when supply is desperate, but as a deliberate degraded mode rather than as the normal path.

The decision runs down the middle. Meera's tap reaches dispatch, which asks the index, asks maps, claims a car, and creates a trip. This is the millisecond-sensitive path, and it touches as few durable stores as it can get away with.

The record runs down the right. The trip is the thing that must survive everything, so it lives in a durable, sharded store. Everything downstream is fed from a log of trip events rather than by calling those services inline. That is the transactional-outbox pattern doing exactly the job it was invented for: the trip and its "trip completed" event commit together or not at all.

The learning loop sits at the bottom right. Every event feeds models that produce the ETAs and the surge multipliers the decision path will consume next minute. Yesterday's data is today's ranking.

Why these boundaries and not others?

Location is split from dispatch because their loads differ by 150× and their durability needs are opposite. Dev's ping is worthless in ten minutes. Meera's trip is worth keeping for seven years.

The index is sharded by cell, so a query for one area hits one shard and Dev's pings hit one shard. That is also its weakness, which failure F4 below is about.

Dispatch owns the claim. If two services could move Dev's state, the invariant would need distributed agreement to hold. Instead Dev's state has exactly one owner, and "which node owns which driver?" is the ownership question from consistent-hashing applied to driver ids.

Both answers from that lab are live options here. A ring with gossip suits a fleet of matcher nodes that changes often with no coordinator to ask; Uber's Ringpop is this, packaged. Fixed slots published by a coordinator is the Redis Cluster shape, and it suits you when you want exact, hand-movable placement. The trade is the one that lab put numbers on: coordination bought with control.

Trip state is not in the index. The index is a fast, lossy, rebuildable cache of where things are. The trip store is the durable truth. Losing the whole index costs you thirty seconds of degraded matching while the pings refill it. Losing the trip store loses money.

That last point deserves the vocabulary of your earlier labs. The geo index is a cache in the exact sense of caching: a derived copy, cheap to rebuild, allowed to be stale. The trip record is the source of truth.

12. What actually goes wrong, and what survives each time?

In one breath: Nine failures. In every single one of them, no car is ever promised to two people. In almost every one, somebody waits longer. That trade is the design.

Read these as nine short stories. Each one names who it happens to, what the system does about it, and how it reaches Asha's screen. The comparison table comes after all nine, so you can see the shape they share.

F1 — what happens when Omar's phone goes dark?

Omar parks, opens a sandwich, and his phone loses signal in the underground car park. His last pin stays on the map, and it stays the nearest thing to somebody.

Run java src/Experiments.java 4, or in the console click a car, press loses signal, turn the freshness rule off, and request nearby. Sixty-five of two hundred requests were dispatched to a dark car.

The reason is a sentence worth memorising: absence of a message is not a message. Nothing distinguishes "parked with the app open" from "phone in a tunnel" from "went home an hour ago".

So supply becomes a lease. A ping renews it, and thirty seconds without one takes Omar out of matching entirely. The test is a_driver_who_stopped_pinging_is_not_dispatched.

In production this never appears as an error. It appears as rider wait time, as cancellation rate, and as "the driver never moved" complaints. Teams watch the stale-pin ratio per area precisely because every other symptom is so indirect.

F2 — what happens when the matcher dies holding Ben's car?

Between the moment dispatch offers Ben's car to Meera and the moment Ben answers, the matcher process is killed. Conceptually this sits between matchOnce and accept; SCENE 5 of Sim.java walks the same code path through the deadline.

Without a deadline, Ben is stuck OFFERED forever and vanishes from supply. The only actor that knew about the reservation is gone.

The lease expires. A housekeeping tick returns Ben to AVAILABLE and re-matches Meera's trip. The test is expired_offer_returns_the_driver_and_rematches_the_trip.

Expiry reclaims with the same compare-and-set the claim used, so an expiry racing an accept cannot corrupt anything. Ben is never handed to two trips.

This is why the offer TTL is ten to twenty seconds and not two minutes. The TTL is a direct trade between how long a crashed matcher strands a car and how long a real driver gets to reach for their phone.

F3 — what happens when Priya double-taps?

Priya's screen freezes, so she taps request again. Or her phone times out and the network layer retries on her behalf. She does not know which.

Without an idempotency key, that is two trips, two matches, two cars and two charges. Every mobile network delivers at least once, and a request that timed out on the client may well have succeeded on the server.

So Priya's app generates one key per intent. The server maps key to trip and returns the existing trip for a repeat. SCENE 8 of Sim.java shows it, and the test is double_tapping_request_creates_one_trip.

Nothing is lost to this fix. It costs one map lookup, which is exactly why it belongs in the first version of the system rather than the fifth.

In production the same key must cover the whole chain, payments included. This is straight out of communication-protocols and transactional-outbox: at-least-once delivery, plus consumers that do not mind hearing it twice.

F5 — what happens when the firehose falls behind?

This one is not modelled in code, because the lab is a single process with no queue. It is stated, not run.

The index starts lagging reality by seconds, then by minutes. Every pin is stale. Here is the nasty part: if the lag is inside the freshness TTL, none of the pins is stale enough to be excluded. Matching keeps working and quietly gets worse. ETAs are wrong. Drivers are offered fares they have already driven past.

250,000 writes per second has no slack in it. A garbage collection pause, a redeploy, or one slow shard turns into a backlog immediately.

The response is to refuse to queue pings for durability on the hot path. Drop instead of lag, because a lost ping is replaced four seconds later. Shed load by lengthening the ping interval under pressure. And watch ingest lag, not ingest rate.

Safety holds throughout. Nobody is double-booked. What degrades is match quality, smoothly and invisibly, which is the worst way for anything to degrade.

Location ingest is deliberately best-effort in production. Note the trap in the other direction: the freshness TTL must be comfortably larger than normal ingest lag, or a routine blip excludes your entire fleet at once. That is a self-inflicted outage, and it is worth designing against explicitly.

F6 — what happens when the cluster splits in two?

Also stated rather than run. The network partitions, and two halves of the dispatch cluster can each see a subset of drivers.

If both halves may claim Dev, you have section 4's third crack again, at cluster scale: Meera and Priya, one car, two machines, no error. The reason is cap-theorem, unchanged and unarguable.

The response is a split decision, and it is the most interesting design choice in this lab.

The claim is CP. Dev's state has exactly one owning node, chosen by hashing his driver id onto the ring from consistent-hashing. If that node is unreachable, Dev simply cannot be matched by anyone. Refusing to match is a mild failure. Matching twice is a severe one.

The location index is AP. A stale or partial view of where cars are yields a worse match, not a wrong one, so it stays available.

Safety holds: one car, one rider. It is bought by giving up availability for that one driver. Liveness is genuinely lost for the drivers owned by the unreachable node, until ownership moves.

Ownership rebalancing needs a ring, gossip and a handover protocol. That is why it is a first-class part of the design and never an afterthought. That lab put a number on the disagreement window too. While membership propagates, different nodes hold different views of who owns what, bounded at roughly 1/n of the keyspace for a ring. During that window, two nodes must not both believe they own Dev. Every ride-hailing dispatch system is, underneath, a membership problem.

F7 — what happens when the payment fails after the ride?

Meera is standing on the pavement. The trip is finished. The card is declined.

Stated, not run. The money and the trip live in different systems, and the trip cannot be rolled back, because Meera has already been moved across the city.

So the trip is the pivot step of a saga. Everything before it can be compensated. Once it is complete, the system may only move forward: retry the charge, fall back to another payment method, or carry a debt on the account.

Payment is driven off the trip-completed event through the transactional-outbox, so "trip completed" and "a payment will be attempted" commit together. Exactly one charge attempt lineage exists per trip, because the idempotency key from F3 is still doing its work.

The interesting engineering here is not the retry. It is the policy about what a rider with a failed payment is allowed to do next.

F8 — what happens when pings arrive in the wrong order?

Dev's ping from 09:00:04 lands after his ping from 09:00:08. On the map, his car jumps backwards.

Stated, not run. If the index takes last-write-wins by arrival time, Dev can appear to teleport into a cell he already left. Mobile networks reorder. Retries replay. Phone clocks are not trustworthy.

The response is to carry a device timestamp and a monotonic sequence number per driver, then drop any update older than the one already stored.

Dispatch still claims correctly, so safety holds. What bends is quality: a stale position is briefly authoritative, and somebody gets a slightly worse car.

This is an out-of-order event stream wearing a map, so it gets the same answer: version the writes and make the consumer idempotent. That is the projection rule from cqrs.

F9 — what happens when slowness feeds itself?

It is 2 a.m. and Asha is looking at a graph that will not come down.

Stated, not run. Dispatch p99 rises. Rider apps time out and retry. The retries add load. Dispatch gets slower. The system does not recover when the original trigger goes away, because the retry load is now the cause. Engineers call this a metastable failure.

The response is the whole toolkit from software-architecture-patterns and distributed-rate-limiting. Bound the retries, with backoff and jitter. Keep idempotency keys, so retries are harmless. Shed load at the gateway. Put circuit breakers around maps and pricing. Degrade gracefully: if the ETA service is down, rank by distance and say so, rather than failing the request.

Safety holds. Liveness is deliberately sacrificed for a subset of requests so that the rest survive.

The fallback ladder is written down in advance, per dependency, because nobody invents one at 2 a.m.

Which knobs can you turn, and who pays when you do?

Six knobs. None of them has a globally right setting, which is the point.

# The knob Turned down or short Turned up or long
1 cell size precise, but many cells to visit and more churn as drivers cross boundaries fewer lookups, more candidates to filter, worse hot-cell behaviour
2 search stop rule stop early: fast and bounded, slightly worse matches cover fully: optimal within the radius, cost scales with area and density
3 offer TTL supply recovers fast from crashes, real drivers miss offers patient with drivers, strands cars when things break
4 freshness TTL no ghosts, but a network blip un-matches your fleet robust to blips, sends cars that are not there

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 You are looking for a car that is moving, among a hundred thousand cars that are all moving, and the answer has to arrive before the person gets bored — about a second. Two things make it hard. First, "who is near me?" is a question about every car unless you have organised them by where they are in advance. Second, the answer goes stale while you are computing it: by the time you say "that one", it has moved, or somebody else has already been promised it.

1.2 Because between finding the free car and marking it busy there is a gap, and another request fits inside that gap perfectly — so both requests find the same car free and both mark it busy. The fix is not a faster check; it is making the check and the mark one indivisible action that only one request can win.

1.3 Caching addresses problem 1 (the scan) and only partly: it makes reads cheap, and creates a new correctness question about how stale the cached position is. It does nothing for problem 2 (two callers, one car — a cache makes this worse, because now several machines each hold their own copy of "car 47 is free"), nothing for problem 3 (a pin that lies — a cache faithfully caches the lie), and nothing for problem 4 (a reservation nobody releases).

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 From a fresh console, ranking by soonest arrival, the reserved car is K3 while the closest car is K2 — K2 is roughly 200 m away with a roads factor of 3.4×, so it is about 700 m of driving. The log line names both.

2.2 The inspector shows: state = OFFERED, its cell, its position in metres, how many seconds ago it reported, its roads factor, distance and arrival time to the rider, and reserved for T1 (expires t+15s).

2.3 Everything within 1.5 km shades 49 squares (a 7×7 block). Widen until 3 cars found usually shades 9 (rings 0 and 1), sometimes 1 or 25 depending on where you stand. Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 44.9% of requests find nobody at all, and 13.3% return a worse driver than the true nearest (116 m worse on average, 445 m at worst). The second number is the dangerous one: those requests succeed, log nothing, and simply make every rider wait a little longer forever.

3.2 About 98 of 200 — it lands near half every run, and the exact number moves with thread scheduling. Not a rare race — the common case, because every rider on the same corner ranks the cars in the same order and reaches for the same one.

3.3 15–25× (112 µs → 2,442 µs per query in one run), with 6,399 cars in the single busiest 500 m square. Nothing is broken; the index simply stopped narrowing, because narrowing to "nearby" only helps when nearby is a small set.

3.4 D1 gets the offer, at 316 m, even though D4's pin is 71 m away — D4 last reported 65 seconds ago and the freshness limit is 30. With ignorePingFreshness = true, D4 wins and the rider is sent a car that may be anywhere; ghostDispatches counts it.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 — 50 m cells. Measured, 100,000 drivers, 3 km radius:

   50m cells:   4.32 ms/query,  14,641 cells visited,  2,129 drivers measured
  200m cells:   0.71 ms/query,     961 cells visited,  2,221 drivers measured
  500m cells:   0.50 ms/query,     169 cells visited,  2,425 drivers measured
 2000m cells:   0.91 ms/query,      25 cells visited,  5,586 drivers measured

A finer index is slower because the cost of a search has two halves: the cells you visit and the drivers you measure. Shrinking cells trades the second for the first, and at 50 m you visit 14,641 mostly-empty squares to save measuring 300 drivers. Too coarse is bad in the other direction — at 2 km you visit 25 squares and measure 5,586 drivers. The curve is U-shaped and its minimum depends on density, which is precisely the argument for multi-resolution grids (H3/S2): one size cannot be right for an airport and a highway at the same time.

Nothing here is a safety loss. It is pure liveness/latency.

4.2 — offer TTL. At 2 seconds, real drivers lose offers they were reaching for: the offer expires, the trip re-matches, and the first driver sees a popup vanish. Acceptance rate drops, offers-per-trip rises, and riders wait longer — liveness, degraded by trying too hard to be quick. At 5 minutes, a crashed matcher takes a car out of supply for five minutes, and a driver who ignores a popup is unreachable for five minutes. Neither setting is unsafe; the TTL is a straight trade between driver reaction time and how long a failure strands a car.

4.3 — ping TTL of 2 seconds. Almost the whole fleet becomes unmatchable, because drivers ping every ~4 seconds and normal network jitter puts most of them over the limit at any instant. Real-world trigger: an ingest slowdown of a few seconds — a redeploy, a GC pause, a slow shard. This is the self-inflicted outage to design against: the freshness TTL must be comfortably larger than normal ping interval plus normal ingest lag, or a small blip un-matches your entire supply. Liveness, lost catastrophically, from a rule that exists to protect quality.

4.4 — remove the triedDrivers guard. Measured: the declining driver is immediately re-offered the same trip, forever.

first offer: A
after decline 1: offered to A
after decline 2: offered to A
...

The general name is livelock (or a retry loop with no progress): the system is busy, nothing is wrong with any individual step, and no work completes. In production it also becomes an accidental denial-of-service against one driver's phone.

Rung 5 — Implement

5.1 A correct cancelTrip:

void cancelTrip(String tripId) {
    Trip t = trips.get(tripId);
    if (t == null || t.state == TripState.COMPLETED || t.state == TripState.CANCELLED) return;
    String held = t.pendingOfferDriver;
    if (held != null) {
        Driver d = drivers.get(held);
        // compareAndSet, not set: the driver may have accepted a millisecond ago.
        if (d != null && d.state.compareAndSet(DriverState.OFFERED, DriverState.AVAILABLE)) {
            d.offer = null;
        }
        t.pendingOfferDriver = null;
    }
    t.state = TripState.CANCELLED;
    log("%s cancelled by the rider; %s released", tripId, held);
}

Why compareAndSet and not set: the concrete race is cancel racing accept. The rider taps cancel at the same moment the driver taps accept. The accept moves the driver OFFERED → ON_TRIP; if cancel then blindly writes AVAILABLE, you have a driver who is driving to a cancelled pickup and is in the pool to be offered to someone else — the double-booking bug, re-entering through the cancel path. With compareAndSet(OFFERED, AVAILABLE) the cancel simply fails to release, sees the driver is already on a trip, and the cancel becomes a "cancel after accept", which is a different (and paid) business case.

5.2 — cap the ranking. Sorting is not where the cost is; the candidate list is. Experiment 1's drivers measured per request is unchanged (the index still returned them), while ranking time drops — so with nearProgressive already stopping early, the cap changes very little. It matters most in exactly one place: dense areas, where the ring search returns hundreds and you would rank them all. The quality you lose is the tail — the best of 200 candidates is slightly better than the best of 10 — and you lose the most in dense neighbourhoods, which is also where the difference between the 1st and 10th best car is smallest. That asymmetry is why the cap is a good trade.

6.2 Invariant I1: a driver is filed under exactly one cell. Note that I2 (one rider per car) still holds — the claim is unaffected. This is a quality bug, not a safety bug, which is exactly why it survives review.

6.3 After a week: the index has grown to hold every cell every driver has ever passed through, so memory climbs steadily and search gets slower as ghosts accumulate. Riders complain that the app "sent a car that was miles away" and that ETAs are wrong. The dashboards that look fine: error rate (zero), dispatch success rate (fine — a car is always found), CPU (gradual). The dashboards that move first: index memory, candidates examined per query, and offer acceptance rate — drivers decline offers for pickups they are nowhere near.

Rung 7 — Handle a failure

7.1 — crash between the claim and the answer.

What happens today, in order: requestRide writes the idempotency key → creates the trip → claims a driver → returns. If dispatch dies after the claim and before the reply, the retry finds the key, returns the same trip, and does not match again — so the outcome is correct. That is guaranteed because the key is written before the claim. Reverse those two lines and the retry re-enters matching and claims a second car for the same rider, and now you are holding two cars for one trip.

What is merely likely rather than guaranteed: that the trip and its key survived the crash at all. In this lab both live in memory, so a real dispatch crash loses them and the retry creates a genuinely new trip while the first driver sits in OFFERED until the lease expires. The fix is that the idempotency key and the trip must be written durably, together, before the claim — one transaction — and the claim must be recoverable from the trip record. Then the retry is harmless no matter where the crash landed.

The general rule worth taking away: the record of intent must be durable before the side effect that acts on it. Same shape as the outbox.

7.2 — the driver's app dies mid-trip. Already covered: completeTrip is idempotent (I6), so a replayed completion charges once. What is missing:

  • The trip must be resumable. On reinstall, the app asks "what am I doing?" and the server answers from the durable trip record — the client cannot be the source of truth for an in-progress trip.
  • The completion must be reconstructable without the app. If the phone never comes back, the trip is stuck IN_PROGRESS forever. Real systems have a sweeper: trips in progress with no telemetry for N minutes go to a resolution path (auto-complete with an estimated fare, or a support queue).
  • Fare inputs must be durable as they accrue, not computed from a final client message — otherwise a dead phone loses the distance travelled.

7.3 — the ETA service is down.

  • Rank by: straight-line distance, with a per-area correction factor cached from the last known good ETAs — degraded, still ordered sensibly.
  • The rider sees: a car, and a range instead of a precise ETA ("about 5–8 min"). Never a spinner, never a failure.
  • Log: a distinct degraded_ranking counter, so the quality loss is visible in the same dashboards as everything else, and so post-hoc match quality can be compared.
  • Avoid the storm: a circuit breaker around the ETA client (fail fast while it is open rather than waiting for timeouts on every request), bounded retries with jitter, and a timeout budget shorter than the rider's patience.

Toolkit: software-architecture-patterns (breaker, bulkhead, graceful degradation) and distributed-rate-limiting (shedding, fail-open vs fail-closed — here, fail-open with degraded quality, because a worse car now beats no car at all).

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 — the airport. What survives: the claim, the lease, the freshness rule, the trip state machine. What must change:

  • Indexing. One 500 m cell holding 6,399 cars is not an index. Either sub-divide (a finer resolution inside hot cells — H3 exists for this) or stop treating an airport as geography at all and treat it as a queue: drivers join a FIFO on arrival and the matcher pops rather than searches. This is the right answer, and it is also the fair one — the airport's real problem is not latency but that "nearest" is meaningless when 2,000 cars are within 200 m of each other, so some other ordering has to be chosen deliberately.
  • Matching. Batched, not greedy: arrivals come in bursts as flights land, and a burst is exactly the case where batching wins most.
  • The driver sees their queue position — otherwise the ordering feels arbitrary and drivers game it by circling.
  • Monitor: queue length and wait time per terminal, join/leave rate, and matches from the queue versus from geographic search.

8.2 — three cities. Shard by city/region, because rides are local: a pickup and a drop-off are almost always in the same region, so this keeps the hot path inside one region and one failure domain.

  • Routing key: the geographic cell of the pickup — it is known at request time and it is what the index is keyed by anyway.
  • Boundary trips (rare, real: airport runs between metro areas): the trip belongs to the region that owns the pickup, for its whole life. Do not migrate a live trip between regions; you would be inventing distributed transactions for the sake of tidiness.
  • Consistency posture, per piece of state:
    • Geo index — AP. Stale or partial is a worse match, not a wrong one.
    • Driver state (the claim) — CP, single owner per driver id. A driver whose owner is unreachable is unmatchable, and that is the correct answer.
    • Trip record — CP within its region, durable, replicated.
    • Payments — CP, and idempotent end to end; money never gets AP.
  • A region outage takes down dispatch in that city and nothing else, provided the shared services (identity, payments, maps) are not single-region. The design goal is that the blast radius equals the region.

8.3 — the 10× day. Ranked by effect, best first:

  1. Surge — the only lever that changes demand. Everything else rearranges a queue; this shortens it. Cost: unpopular, needs caps and communication.
  2. Load shedding at the gateway — protects the system from the retry storm that a 10× day guarantees. Cost: some riders get a clear "try again" instead of a spinner, which is the honest failure.
  3. Batching window, widened from ~2 s to ~5 s — 10× demand means many candidates per batch, so batching pays more than usual. Cost: seconds added to everyone's wait.
  4. Ping interval, lengthened from 4 s to 8 s — halves the firehose at the cost of position accuracy. Cheap insurance if ingest lag rises.
  5. Search radius, reduced — counter-intuitively. When supply is scarce, a wide search sends cars on long empty drives, removing them from where they were needed. Cost: more "no cars available", sooner and more honestly.
  6. Offer TTL, shortened a little — recovers unanswered offers faster when every car counts. Cost: more missed offers for slow drivers; do not overdo it.

First lever: surge, and the evidence it worked is the supply/demand ratio per area moving back toward 1 — not revenue, and not the multiplier itself.

8.4 — the one metric. I would put up the fraction of ride requests that get an accepted driver within 60 seconds (a "good match rate"), sliced by area.

  • Why not dispatch p99 latency: it can be beautiful while nobody gets a car.
  • Why not match rate alone: it counts a match to a ghost car as a success.
  • Why not average wait: the average is dominated by the healthy majority and hides the airport and the outer suburbs entirely.
  • What it misses: match quality — it says nothing about whether the rider got the best car, so a matcher that quietly picks the second-best forever (the cell-edge bug, 13.3% of requests) looks perfectly healthy. That is why offer acceptance rate and mean pickup ETA sit next to it on the same screen.
