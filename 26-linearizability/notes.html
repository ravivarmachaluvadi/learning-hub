Lab 26 · Why Distribution Is Hard

Linearizability — when is "consistent" a promise you can check?

Tell a friend: Meera sees PASS on the university results page and phones her father, Raghu, who opens the same page a few seconds later and sees "not yet published". No machine crashed and nobody lied: two of the three copies simply disagreed for a moment, and Meera's phone call proves that something already seen was then un-seen. Linearizability is the rule that forbids exactly this: however many machines there are, the whole system must behave like one notebook, read and written one request at a time. This lab builds a checker that reads only the requests and their answers on a clock, points it at six replication designs, and convicts four of them.

The checker you can drag is visualization.html, also published at https://claude.ai/code/artifact/01a5e2be-a55e-470a-b3a9-ce07e3d479b1. How to run the code, and where every number came from, is in section 19 at the end.

1. Why does "consistent" need a definition?

In one breath: Every database says it is "consistent". Until you say exactly what that word promises, you cannot write a test that fails, you cannot compare two databases, and you cannot tell a real bug from a bad memory.

Every distributed store advertises that it is "consistent". This lab is about the one precise thing that word can mean, and why it is the version worth wanting. Then the part almost nobody does: how to check whether a system actually delivered it.

You already know that copies of data can disagree. You also know the counting trick from the quorums lab that stops a reader from missing a write. So here is a different question, and it is the one that actually bites in production:

When is a storage system allowed to be called correct?

That sounds like philosophy until you have to answer it for real. Someone files a bug: "I updated the setting, my colleague refreshed, and she saw the old one." Is that a bug in your service? A bug in the database? Or the documented behaviour of the database you chose? You cannot answer without a precise statement of what the database promised.

"Eventually consistent" does not help. Eventually has no deadline. Consistent is doing no work in that sentence at all.

Without a precise definition, three things happen. You cannot write a test that would fail. You cannot compare two databases. And you cannot tell a real incident from someone misremembering.

2. What happened to Meera and her father?

In one breath: Meera saw PASS and phoned her father. Raghu looked afterwards and saw nothing. Two honest machines gave one impossible pair of answers, and the phone call is what makes the pair impossible.

Meera is waiting for her exam results. The university publishes them on a website. The website is served from three machines, call them A, B and C, so that it does not fall over on results day.

At 10:00 the results are published. Meera refreshes, sees PASS, and immediately phones her father.

"It's up. Go and look."

Her father, Raghu, puts down the phone, opens the same page, and sees not yet published.

Nothing has broken. No machine has crashed. No error was logged. Nobody was lied to on purpose. Meera hit machine A, which had the new page. Raghu hit machine C, which had not received it yet. Both machines answered honestly.

Here is what makes this the interesting case, rather than an ordinary staleness complaint. Raghu looked strictly after Meera had already been told the answer. There is no way to arrange these two page loads on a single timeline such that one noticeboard could have produced both. Meera's phone call is the proof. Something that had already happened, un-happened.

🔮 Predict: Ignore the machines for a moment. What is the smallest number of facts you need about these two page loads to be sure something is wrong, without knowing anything about how the website is built?

Reveal Four. When each page load **started**, when each one **finished**, and what each one **returned**. Nothing else. You never need to know how many machines there are, how they replicate, or what happened inside.

If you said "two: what each returned", you have missed the clock. Two different answers are fine when the two loads overlap. It is the order in time that convicts.

That is the whole idea this lab is built on. Correctness here is a property of what an outside observer can see: the requests and their answers, laid on a clock. It is not a property of the implementation.

3. Why not just make the copies catch up faster?

In one breath: The obvious reading is "machine C was behind", so make it less behind. That genuinely helps. It cannot finish the job.

The obvious reading of Meera's bug is "machine C was behind". So the obvious fix is to make it not be behind. Replicate faster. Replicate synchronously. Add health checks that pull a lagging machine out of rotation.

This is what most teams try first, and it is not stupid. It genuinely reduces how often Raghu sees the old page.

4. Why does "faster" never become "never"?

In one breath: Faster replication has no finish line. Sticky routing fixes Meera and does nothing for Raghu. And the failure raises no error, so nobody knows it happened.

Three things go wrong with "just replicate faster".

It has no stopping condition. How fast is fast enough? There is no number you can put in a ticket. "Usually within 50ms" is not a guarantee. It is a description of a good day. Meera's phone call takes two seconds.

The second fix is worse. The next idea is usually "make every user always read from the same machine", which engineers call sticky routing. That does fix Meera reading her own writes. It does nothing for Raghu, because Raghu is a different person on a different machine. And the moment that machine restarts, the guarantee silently disappears with it.

And the failure is invisible. No error is raised. Your dashboards are green. This is the killer: you cannot alert on a condition you have not defined.

🔮 Predict: Suppose you make replication perfectly synchronous. A write is not acknowledged until all three machines have it. Is Meera's bug gone for good?

Reveal That specific bug, yes. But now try this: the write is **still in flight**. It has reached machine A and not yet B or C. Meera reads, hits A, sees PASS. Raghu reads a second later, hits C, sees nothing. The write has still not finished, so nobody has broken the "wait for all three" rule.

If you said "gone for good", you were fixing lag. The problem was never lag. The problem is that while an operation is in progress, different observers can be shown different pasts, and the system never reconciles them. Making the window smaller does not make it a different kind of thing.

This is exactly the case the last lab's counting rule cannot see. That rule honestly gave you 0 stale reads over 4,000 rounds. You will count the thing it missed in section 14.

5. What do we actually want?

In one breath: Not "copies agree quickly". We want a system that nobody outside could ever prove has more than one copy.

Step back from mechanisms and say what we actually want. Not "copies agree quickly". This:

The system must be indistinguishable, from outside, from a system that has exactly one copy of the data and handles one request at a time.

Read that again, because two words are carrying everything.

Indistinguishable. We are not asking for one copy. We are asking that no sequence of requests and answers could ever prove there is more than one. The implementation may have five hundred machines.

From outside. The only evidence admissible is what clients asked, when they asked, when they were answered, and what they were told. If the internals are a mess but no client can catch it, the requirement is met.

Everything else in this lab is either a way to check this, or a way to build it.

6. What is the one idea that makes this checkable?

In one breath: Every request takes a stretch of time. Pretend it happened at one instant somewhere inside that stretch. If you can pick the instants so that a single notebook explains every answer, the impersonation held.

Here is the invention, and it fits in two sentences.

A request is not an instant. It is a stretch of time, from when Meera's browser sent it to when Meera was answered. But if the system really were one notebook handling one request at a time, then somewhere inside that stretch there would be a single moment when the operation actually took effect.

Pick one instant inside each request's stretch. If you can pick them such that reading the requests in instant-order tells a sensible one-notebook story, the system's impersonation held. If no such choice exists, it did not.

That is the entire concept. Everything after this is vocabulary, algorithms, and consequences.

Notice what makes it powerful: overlapping requests are free. If Meera's read and the publish overlap in time, both answers are defensible. The instant could have fallen on either side. It is only when a request finishes before another one starts that the order is nailed down and cannot be argued with.

🔮 Predict: Two facts about the story: Meera's read overlapped the publish, and Raghu's read started after Meera's had finished. Which of the two facts convicts the website?

Reveal Only the second. Meera's read overlapping the publish is free: her PASS is explained by putting the publish's instant just before her read's instant. Raghu's read starting *after* Meera was answered is the fact that cannot be argued with. His instant must come after hers, so his answer must include whatever hers included.

If you picked the first fact, you are treating "overlapped a write" as suspicious. It never is. Overlap is where the system is allowed to choose.

7. What do engineers call all these things?

In one breath: Every word below names something that has already happened to Meera and Raghu. The phone records are a history. The moment a request "really happened" is its linearization point. The rule that makes the phone call count is real-time order.

Each of these names something you have already met in the story. Now they get their real names, their precise meanings, and their connections to the labs you have already done. The story keeps going while the words arrive.

What is the evidence called?

Imagine the university's lawyer collecting evidence. She asks for exactly four facts per page load: who asked, when they asked, when they were answered, and what they were told. That list is the history. It is the recording of what happened, as seen from outside.

Precisely: a history is the list of operations, each with the time its client sent it, the time its client was answered, and the answer it got. The term exists to name the only evidence that is admissible. Everything about internals is deliberately excluded. That is what makes the property checkable against any system, including one you did not write.

Meera and Raghu's two page loads are a two-operation history. This lab writes histories in a compact form. Here is that form beside its meaning, so you never have to decode it in the middle of a sentence.

Written In words
C1 put(1) [0,4] client 1 asked to store the value 1 at tick 0, and was told "done" at tick 4
C2 get()→0 [6,10] client 2 asked for the value at tick 6, and was told "0" at tick 10
the two lines together a two-operation history: a write, then a read that missed it

The Java class is literally called History. It is the input to everything in this lab.

Where does a request start and end?

Each page load is a bar on a timeline. The bar's left end is the moment Meera pressed refresh. The right end is the moment the page appeared. Engineers call the left end the invocation and the right end the response.

Precisely: the invocation is the moment the request left the client. The response is the moment the answer arrived back. Two operations are concurrent if their stretches overlap. A history is sequential if no two operations overlap at all; everything happened one at a time.

These terms exist because the gap between them is where all the interesting behaviour hides. A system is only ever caught out by what it does during an operation.

Written In words Concurrent?
put [0,24] and get [2,8] a write from tick 0 to 24; a read from tick 2 to 8, inside it yes, they overlap
put [0,4] and get [6,10] a write finished at tick 4; a read began at tick 6 no, the put finished first

Concurrency is permission. The more operations overlap, the more orders are defensible, and the harder the system is to catch.

When did it actually happen?

Somewhere inside Meera's bar there is one moment when her page load "really" read the noticeboard. Engineers call that moment the linearization point. It is the single instant an operation took effect.

Precisely: a linearization point is a chosen point inside [invocation, response] for each operation. The points are chosen so that ordering the operations by their points produces a legal one-copy execution.

The term exists to separate when an operation took effect from how long the request took. The point is a claim about the outcome, not about anything the code does. It is often nowhere near the middle.

History Where the put's point must sit In words
put(1) [0,24], get()→0 [2,8], get()→1 [12,18] between tick 8 and tick 12 after the read that missed it, before the read that saw it

The put took 24 units of wall clock. Its point is a four-unit window inside that. In the visualization, this is the black dot you can drag.

What would one notebook have said?

To judge Raghu's page, you need to know what a single noticeboard would have shown. That set of rules is the sequential specification, or the "one-copy story". It is the rules the thing would obey if there were only one of it, used by one person at a time.

Precisely, for a register: put(v) sets the value to v. get() returns the current value. cas(a,b) sets the value to b and returns success only if the value was a. A sequential history is legal if it obeys these rules.

The term exists because "correct" is meaningless without saying correct at what. The specification is the single-copy behaviour you are claiming to imitate. It is chosen, not discovered.

Sequential history Legal? In words
put(1), get()→1 yes store 1, then read 1
put(1), get()→0 no store 1, then read 0: no single notebook does that

In the code it is twelve lines: Checker.apply, the method named apply.

Which rule makes the phone call count?

Meera was answered before Raghu asked. The phone call proves it. Any story about the noticeboard must therefore put Meera's read before Raghu's. Engineers call this the real-time order: if one request was already answered before another was sent, the first one really did come first, and no story may claim otherwise.

In words: whenever operation a's response came before operation b's invocation, a must precede b in the chosen order. In symbols: if response(a) ≤ invocation(b) then a must precede b. Operations that overlap have no such constraint.

This rule is the only thing separating the guarantee we want from the weaker one below it. It is the thing that makes Meera's phone call count as evidence. Without it, "Raghu saw the old page" is perfectly acceptable behaviour.

Fact Time What the rule forces
Meera's read was answered 10:00:02 any explanation must put Meera's read first
Raghu's read began 10:00:05 since Meera's read returned PASS, Raghu's must too

It is one if in the checker, the method Checker.candidates. You can switch it off and watch the verdicts change.

So what does "linearizable" mean?

Now the word itself. A history is linearizable if the single-copy impersonation held. Precisely: there exists a choice of linearization points, one inside each operation, such that the resulting sequential history is legal and respects real-time order. A system is linearizable if every history it can produce is. (For operations that never got an answer, see the next term.)

Herlihy and Wing coined the term in 1990. They wanted concurrent objects to have the same kind of specification single-threaded ones have. The name means: the concurrent execution can be linearized, flattened onto a line, without anyone noticing.

History Verdict Why
put(1)[0,4], get()→0[6,10] not linearizable the put finished before the read began, and the read missed it
widen the put to [0,24] linearizable now the read sits inside the put; the point can fall after the read

No answer changed between those two rows. Only the clock did. Time is data.

This is the C in the CAP theorem you learned in cap-theorem. CAP's consistency is precisely this, and nothing weaker.

What about a request that never got an answer?

Suppose the registrar's publish request timed out. Did the results go up or not? The registrar does not know. Engineers call that a pending (or indeterminate) operation: an operation with an invocation and no response.

The definition allows a pending operation to be either linearized at any point, with any result, or discarded entirely. This is the honest model of a timeout. A timed-out write is not "failed". It is unknown, and it may become visible later. Any definition that could not express this would be useless in practice.

Scenario F shows it (situation F in the visualization, from Scenarios.java). A write times out; two later reads see its value. Perfectly linearizable. It simply happened.

The quorums lab counted 500/500 "failed" writes that were readable afterwards. This is the formal name for that situation. It is also the reason writes must be idempotent: a retry of a pending operation might be its second execution.

What if you throw the clock away?

Take the phone call out of the story. Nobody knows whether Meera or Raghu looked first. Now order the events as Raghu's read, then the publish, then Meera's read. Every answer is correct. That weaker guarantee is sequential consistency: the same idea with the clock thrown away.

Precisely: there must exist some total order producing the observed answers, respecting each client's own order of requests, but not real time. Every linearizable history is sequentially consistent. The reverse is not true.

Lamport defined it in 1979 for shared-memory multiprocessors, where "real time" between two CPUs is not observable. It is the natural weakening, and it is the exact thing most "eventually consistent but ordered" systems actually give you.

🔮 Predict: Run 600 histories from a design that reads any replica, and 600 from a design with a client cache in front of a correct store. Both fail linearizability often. How many of the failures are still sequentially consistent?

Reveal For the cache, almost all of them. For the read-any-replica design, only a few.

| Design | Sequentially consistent, not linearizable (of 600) | What it means | |---|---|---| | asynchronous replicas | 52 | a single client bouncing between replicas usually sees time go backwards, which breaks even per-client order | | client cache in front | 560 | each client sees its own consistent past; only the clock between clients convicts it |

If you guessed "about the same for both", you are missing what a cache preserves: one client's own order. That is why cache bugs feel harmless in testing. Experiment 4 in section 14 produces both numbers.

You already know its consequences under other names. Monotonic reads and read-your-own-writes are what you ask for when you cannot afford linearizability but need some order to survive.

What about transactions?

Everything so far is one operation on one object: one page, one read. Databases work in transactions over many keys. The transaction version of the same question has two names. Serializability says a set of multi-operation transactions produces the same result as some serial order of them. It has no real-time requirement. Strict serializability adds it back.

The terms come from databases, where the unit is a transaction over many keys. Linearizability comes from concurrent programming, where the unit is one operation on one object. Keeping them apart matters. Linearizability says nothing about grouping operations, and serializability says nothing about time.

A serializable database may commit your transaction and then serve a later transaction an order in which yours had not happened yet. That is legal serializability and would be an obvious bug to a user. Spanner's "external consistency" is strict serializability.

Linearizability is strict serializability where every transaction is a single operation on a single object. That one sentence places all four terms on one map.

Does it add up across keys?

The results website has one page per student. If Meera's page behaves like one notebook, and Priya's page behaves like one notebook, does the whole site behave? Yes. Engineers call this locality, or composability: if every key behaves, the whole store behaves.

Precisely: a history over many objects is linearizable if and only if its restriction to each object is linearizable.

The term exists because it is not obvious and it is not true of the alternatives. It means you can make each shard, each key, each object linearizable independently, with no coordination between them, and get a linearizable system for free.

Experiment 10 in section 14 merged 300 pairs of independently linearizable histories, and 300 of 300 were linearizable together. The same experiment shows two sequentially consistent objects combining into a history that is not sequentially consistent. This is the reason linearizability, and not sequential consistency, is the property real systems chase. It is the only one that scales with sharding.

8. Which two pictures should you carry?

In one breath: Picture one: a single notebook and a queue. Picture two: the same thing written as a set of operations, an order, and two conditions. Then three places where the notebook picture lies.

The simple model: the notebook

There is one notebook. Requests queue up. Each one, when its turn comes, reads or writes the notebook and steps away. That is all.

Your job as a judge is this. Given the bars on a timeline, can I place a dot inside every bar such that reading them left-to-right against one notebook produces exactly the answers the clients were given?

The engineer's version

The same machine, stated precisely. Words first, symbols after.

A history is a set of operations. Each operation records six things: which client, what it did, with what arguments, what it was told, when it was sent, and when it was answered. Engineers write the history as H and one operation as (client, op, args, result, invocation, response).

Some pairs of operations are ordered by real time: a was answered before b was sent. Engineers write that as a <ᴴ b, defined to hold iff response(a) ≤ invocation(b). It is a partial order: overlapping pairs are simply not related.

The history H is linearizable iff it can be extended, by giving responses to some pending operations and discarding the rest, to a history H' such that two conditions hold.

Condition In words In symbols
L1 H' can be re-read as one sequential story that a single notebook would accept H' is equivalent to some sequential history S that is legal under the object's sequential specification
L2 every "this one definitely finished first" fact in the real history survives in that story <ᴴ ⊆ <ˢ; every real-time precedence in H survives in S

S is the linearization. The point inside each operation is where it sits in S.

Where the notebook lies to you

Three places. Each one is a mistake that is easy to make and expensive to hold.

1. The dot is not a real event. Nothing in the system happens at the linearization point. There is no line of code, no log entry, no lock acquisition there. The dot is an existence claim made by an outside judge after the fact: "you behaved as if there were a moment here." Two different valid linearizations of the same history can put the dots in different places, and both are correct answers. Chasing "where is the linearization point in my code?" is often a real and useful design question. But the property does not require you to be able to answer it.

2. There is no notebook, so a request may have no place at all. If a client times out, its operation is pending. It might have taken effect, it might not, and the judge is allowed to choose whichever explanation makes the history work. The notebook picture makes it feel as though every request must land somewhere. It does not.

3. One notebook per key, not one notebook. Linearizability is defined per object. Two separate keys are two separate notebooks. The guarantee says nothing whatsoever about them being read together: no snapshot, no atomic multi-key update. The single-notebook image over-promises here. Locality is what makes the under-promise safe: per-key correctness composes into whole-store correctness for the per-key guarantee, not into transactions.

10. How does a machine decide?

In one breath: You cannot walk the history in time order, because the order is the thing you are looking for. So you search: try an operation, run it against the notebook, keep going if the notebook agrees with the client, back up if it does not. The real-time rule is one comparison inside that search.

10.1 Why does the obvious checker fail?

The obvious algorithm: walk the history in time order and check that every read returns the most recent write. Call it the checker, version 1.

Try it on this history and watch it fall apart. Read the picture first: three clients, one long write, two reads inside it.

  C1  [------------------ put(1) ------------------]
  C2       [-- get()→0 --]
  C3                          [-- get()→1 --]

What is "the most recent write" at the moment of C2's read? The write has started and not finished. Version 1 has no answer. If it says "the write counts", C2's get()→0 is a violation. But it obviously is not; the write was still in flight. If it says "the write does not count yet", then C3's get()→1 is a violation. But it obviously is not either.

The bug in version 1 is that it assumed the order is given. It is not. The order is the thing we are looking for.

10.2 What does version 2 do instead?

So: search. Try to build the order one operation at a time. Picture the judge with a stack of bars and a blank notebook. She picks a bar that is allowed to go next, runs it against the notebook, and compares the notebook's answer with what the client was actually told. If they match, she places it and continues. If not, that branch is dead and she backs up.

place(remaining operations, notebook value):
    if nothing remains:                       we found an order — accept
    for each operation that is ALLOWED to go next:
        run it against the notebook
        if the notebook's answer ≠ the answer the client actually got:
            skip it — this branch is impossible
        otherwise: place it, and recurse
    if every candidate failed:                back up and try something else

The entire content of the algorithm is the phrase "allowed to go next", and that is where the real-time rule lives. In Meera's terms: Raghu's read is not allowed to go next while Meera's read, already answered before Raghu asked, is still unplaced.

An operation may go next if and only if no still-unplaced operation had already been answered before this one was sent.

Or in code. This is Checker.candidates:

long minResp = Long.MAX_VALUE;
for (int i = 0; i < ops.size(); i++)
    if (remaining(i)) minResp = Math.min(minResp, ops.get(i).resp());
for (int i = 0; i < ops.size(); i++)
    if (remaining(i) && ops.get(i).invoke < minResp) out.add(i);
    //                 ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^ the whole guarantee, one comparison

Delete that comparison, so that every unplaced operation is a candidate, and you have a sequential consistency checker. That is not an analogy. It is the same file with one option flipped. Both checkers are in Checker.java as Opts.linearizable() and Opts.sequential().

Checking is one half. Building is the other, and it is a short story with one twist. Three attempts, all judged by the checker over 600 histories each.

Attempt 3: no leader at all. Now the interesting one. Can you get the single-copy illusion out of a plain quorum, with no machine in charge?

You already have R + W > N from the last lab. Write to 2 of 3, read from 2 of 3, take the highest timestamp. The read set and the write set must intersect, so a read cannot miss a completed write. Surely that is enough?

🔮 Predict: R = W = 2, N = 3, timestamps handed out correctly. A write is still in flight: it has reached replica A but not B or C. Reader X reads {A, B} and is told the new value. Reader Y starts after X was answered and reads {B, C}. What is Y told?

If you said "the new value, because the quorums overlap", you have the right rule for the wrong question. Y's quorum is guaranteed to overlap the write quorum only once the write has finished. It has not.

And it is a violation: X was already answered "new" before Y was even sent, so any explanation must put X first. Once it does, Y must also say "new". This is Meera and Raghu again, with X as Meera and Y as Raghu.

The counting rule guarantees you cannot miss a completed write. It says nothing about an in-flight one, and it says nothing about two readers agreeing with each other. That is precisely the gap the last lab's map entry named and this section counts.

The fix, and it is one extra round trip. Before a reader returns a value, it writes that value back to a quorum. In the story: before Meera's browser shows PASS, it tells two of the three machines "the answer is PASS". Now Raghu cannot miss it.

One more piece the writer needs. With more than one writer, a writer cannot invent its own timestamp either. It must ask a quorum for the highest one and use that plus one, tagged with its own id. Drop the tie-break and different replicas end up holding different values under the same timestamp: the register quietly splits in two. That is a planted bug in this lab (Sim.noTiebreak), and the test dropping_the_writer_tiebreak_breaks_it catches it.

11. How does it break in real systems?

In one breath: Six failures, and Meera and Raghu are in every one. A replica that is behind, a quorum read with no write-back, a leader that does not know it was replaced, a cache in front, a clock ordering writes, and a lock that expires under you.

Each of these you can make happen yourself. <lab> commands are runnable; the visualization situations are the letters in the "Show me" list. The story of each failure comes first, then one table compares them all.

F1 · What if Raghu reads from a replica that is behind?

The most ordinary failure, and the most common. The university's leader machine acknowledges the publish before machines B and C have it. Raghu's read is routed to C and sees the past. Nothing warns anybody.

Make it happen with java src/Experiments.java stores, row ASYNC. Or in the visualization: Generate from a real design → "read any of them". Almost every history is convicted: 587 of 600 (97.8%). Even sequential consistency fails in 535 of 600, because a single client bouncing between replicas sees the value go backwards.

The system's response is none; it does not know. Durability is intact and the write is never lost. What is lost is any ordering guarantee at all.

In production this is Postgres read replica, MySQL read replica, Cassandra with CL=ONE, and every "we added read replicas to reduce load" project. It is not a bug in those systems. It is what you asked for.

F2 · What if the quorum read has no write-back?

This is section 10.4's story. The publish is in flight and sits on one replica. Meera's browser reads a pair of machines that includes it; Raghu's reads a pair that does not. The later read goes backwards.

Make it happen with java src/Experiments.java writeback: 36 of 600 (6.0%) at a 15% hiccup rate, and 0 of 600 on a calm network. The system's response is none. You still never miss a completed write, so R+W>N is doing its job exactly as advertised. What is lost is the real-time ordering between two reads.

In production this is Cassandra QUORUM reads without read_repair in the read path, and any hand-rolled "read from a majority and take the newest" cache layer. Cassandra's answer when you actually need the guarantee is a lightweight transaction: a Paxos round per operation, at roughly 4× the latency.

F3 · What if the old leader has not noticed it was replaced?

A partition splits the cluster. The majority side elects a new leader, and the registrar's publish goes there. The old leader is still up, still holding Raghu's connection, and still happily answering reads from its own memory. It has no way to know it was replaced. Nobody can tell it, because it is on the wrong side of the partition.

Make it happen in the visualization with situation B, then read this story. The shape is identical to Meera and Raghu, which is the point. The old leader finds out when the partition heals, and steps down. Writes are safe: the old leader cannot get a quorum, so it commits nothing. Reads are lost. This is why stale reads are the classic split-brain symptom and lost writes are not.

In production the fix is a lease. The leader holds a time-bounded grant and must stop serving reads when it expires, before the new leader's term can begin. etcd and TiKV do this. It is why a leader lease is tied to a clock bound, and why clock skew is a correctness input, not an ops detail.

F4 · What if there is a cache in front of a correct store?

Now the store underneath is perfect. Raghu's browser, or a caching layer in front of the site, answers his refresh from a copy it saved a moment ago, without asking. He is shown a value from before the publish.

F5 · What if writes are ordered by a wall clock?

The registrar publishes PASS, then a correction. Each write takes its place in the order from the local clock of whichever machine received it. Two machines with clocks 20 ticks apart put the correction before the original. The read protocol, the write-back and the quorum are all unchanged. The only difference is where the order comes from.

Nothing meaningful is intact. Everything is lost. This is the last lab's "your clock IS the conflict-resolution algorithm", restated as an ordering failure rather than a lost-update one.

In production this is last-write-wins by timestamp, everywhere it appears. Spanner's answer is not "better clocks" but explicitly bounded uncertainty: TrueTime returns an interval, and a transaction waits out the interval before committing. It buys the ordering with latency rather than hoping.

F6 · What if a lock expires while you are still using it?

The registrar runs a nightly job that must not run twice. It takes a distributed lock with a time-to-live. The job's process is stopped by a garbage-collection pause, or a scheduler hiccup, for longer than the TTL. The lock expires. A second process takes it. Then the first process wakes up and carries on, believing it still holds a lock that is now held by someone else.

This one is not simulated in this lab; section 19 flags it as unverified. It belongs here because a distributed lock with a TTL is sold as mutual exclusion, which is a linearizable guarantee. It is not one. Nothing is intact. Mutual exclusion is lost, silently.

In production this is the core of the Redlock critique. The standard mitigation is a fencing token: a monotonically increasing number issued with the lock, which the storage checks and rejects if it is stale. Note what that does: it moves the guarantee from the lock service to the resource, where it can actually be enforced.

12. What does it guarantee, what does it refuse, and what does it cost?

In one breath: It promises every request happened once, at one moment, in an order that respects the clock, and that promise adds up across keys. It does not promise transactions, snapshots, a winner, or an answer at all. And it costs at least one round trip you cannot skip.

What is guaranteed?

  • Every operation appears atomic. It takes effect once, at one instant between its invocation and its response. Meera's refresh happened at one moment, not smeared across three machines.
  • Real time is respected. Anything already answered before your request was sent is visible to you. This is what "no stale reads" actually means. It is a stronger statement than it sounds: it holds across different clients, Meera and Raghu, not just for one client's own writes.
  • It composes. Per-object linearizability gives whole-system linearizability, with no coordination between objects. Experiment 10 counted 300 of 300.
  • It is non-blocking. A pending operation never forces another operation to wait for it in the definition. An implementation may still block for other reasons. This is a property of the specification, not a promise about your p99.

What is explicitly not guaranteed?

Not transactions. Read-then-write is two operations, and anything can happen between them. Suppose the results site keeps a counter of how many students have checked. Two threads each read the counter and write it back plus one. java src/Experiments.java lostupdate makes this physical: AtomicInteger is a linearizable register shipped by the JDK.

🔮 Predict: Two virtual threads each do 20,000 get-then-set increments on one AtomicInteger. The register is linearizable throughout. What is the final value, to the nearest 5,000?

Reveal About 20,000, not 40,000. If you said 40,000 because "AtomicInteger is thread-safe", you are right about the register and wrong about the loop. Each operation was perfect. The *pair* was not one operation.

| Threads × increments | Survived with get-then-set | Survived with compareAndSet | What it means | |---|---|---|---| | 2 × 20,000 = 40,000 | 20,112 | 40,000 | half the updates overwrote each other | | 8 × 20,000 = 160,000 | 21,051 | 160,000 | 87% of the updates simply gone |

Nothing was violated. The register was linearizable throughout.

Not a multi-key snapshot. Two linearizable keys read one after the other can straddle someone else's update. That needs a transaction.

No say in who wins. Two concurrent writes get an order, not the order you wanted. If the second overwrites the first, that is legal.

No liveness. The definition constrains what may be answered, never that anything will be. A system that never responds is vacuously linearizable. That is the formal reason the CP corner of CAP is allowed to refuse service.

What does it cost?

At least one round trip that cannot be skipped. Experiment 5 in section 14 timed each read design.

Read design Mean read latency (ticks) What it means
leader read 14.8 one machine decides, but every read travels to it
one-round-trip quorum read 11.7 fast, and not linearizable (F2)
write-back quorum read 24.4 roughly double, because it is two round trips instead of one

This is why "strongly consistent read" is a per-request option in DynamoDB and a billing line rather than a default.

A theoretical floor, not just an implementation cost. Attiya and Welch showed that in a network with message-delay uncertainty u, a linearizable read cannot beat a bound proportional to u. You cannot engineer this away with a faster protocol. (This result is cited from the literature, not tested in this lab; see section 19.)

Availability under partition. This is CAP, and CAP's C is exactly this property. On the minority side of a partition, a linearizable system must refuse to answer. That is not a bug and not a tuning failure. It is the theorem.

Which invariants must a linearizable design hold?

  1. Every operation has exactly one position in one global order per object.
  2. That order extends real-time precedence.
  3. A read returns the value of the most recent write in that order.
  4. A completed write is never invisible to a later operation.
  5. Once a value has been returned to any client, it can never be un-returned. (This is the one the naive quorum breaks, and the one write-back restores. It is Meera's PASS.)

Which switches are bugs, and which one is a real optimisation?

Two of the three switches in the code are planted bugs. Each disables exactly one invariant from section 12, and each is caught by a named test. The third switch is not a bug. Sim.lazyWriteBack skips the reader's write-back when every replica in the read quorum already agreed. That is a real optimisation used in real systems. It is correct under one condition and silently incorrect otherwise. Finding that condition is exercise 7.2.

15. What are the systems on your CV actually promising?

In one breath: Every real linearizable store is one machine deciding the order, plus a great deal of machinery to survive that machine dying. Read each vendor's consistency page as an answer to one question: which of section 11's six failures have they left open for you?

15.1 What does a real system add to this lab's leader?

The chain runs toy → real → architecture → operations.

The LEADER mode in Sim.java is, structurally, how a real linearizable store works: one machine decides the order. Everything a real system adds exists to survive that machine dying.

  • In this lab: a leader chosen at startup, ordering requests by the moment they arrive.
  • In a real system: the leader is elected by consensus. Its ordering is a replicated log. A new leader may only serve once it has caught up on that log. That is paxos and Raft.
  • In the architecture around it: clients have to discover the leader. A lease bounds how long a deposed leader may keep answering. Reads use a read-index: the leader confirms with a quorum that it is still the leader, then answers from its own memory without writing anything to the log. That is one round trip per read instead of a full log append, and it is what makes linearizable reads affordable.
  • In operations: you watch the leader-election rate, lease expiries, the read-index round-trip time, and clock skew across the cluster. Clock skew is a correctness metric in a leased system, not a hygiene metric.

15.4 What does an on-call engineer actually see?

Not an alert. Linearizability violations do not page you. They arrive as user reports that sound like user error. "I saved it and it didn't save." "It showed the old value and then the new one." "Two people got the same seat."

Meera's father would file the third kind of ticket, and someone would close it as "cannot reproduce".

The tell is a non-monotonic observation: a value going backwards. That is the one symptom neither a stale cache nor a stale replica can disguise. If two people compare notes and one of them saw the future, you have this bug.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Your colleague has described convergence: given enough quiet time, the replicas stop differing. That says nothing about any particular moment. Meera was told PASS and Raghu, looking afterwards, was told "not published" — both replicas will agree eventually, and the system still showed a later observer an earlier world. The guarantee your colleague needs a word for is about now, not about the limit.

1.2 It means the system can be summarised. If every operation took effect at one instant, then everything that ever happened can be written down as a single list, and any question about the data has one answer. "Reads return recent data" is weaker in two ways: recent has no definition, and it is a promise about one read at a time — it never says two different readers are looking at the same world. The instant rule does, because two operations cannot both be "the one at that instant".

1.3

  • Sequential consistency — same idea, minus the requirement that an operation already answered before yours began must come before yours.
  • Serializability — orders whole transactions rather than single operations, and has no real-time requirement at all.
  • Strict serializability — serializability with the real-time requirement put back; linearizability is its single-operation, single-object case.
  • Eventual consistency — a promise about the limit with no promise at any moment; it cannot be violated by any finite observation, which is why it cannot be tested.
  • Read-your-own-writes — a promise to one client about its own operations; linearizability holds between clients, which is the part that is hard.

1.4 Because it makes the property checkable against a system you did not write and cannot see inside, and because it is what users actually experience. Practical consequence: you can run a black-box audit of a commercial database and produce a defensible bug report — which is exactly what Jepsen does. A second consequence, less obvious: your code is part of the observation. Putting a cache in your client turns a linearizable store into a non-linearizable system, and no change was made to the store.

The counter-intuitive result is the second row: making writes wait for everyone barely helps. The violation is caused by a write in flight, and a slower write is in flight for longer, not less.

Making reads ask everyone does fix it, and here is why: if reader X was shown timestamp T, X saw T on some replica j. Replica j's timestamp never goes down. A later reader that asks every replica necessarily asks j too, at a later moment, so it sees at least T. Reads can no longer go backwards.

That is also why it is not a fix you can ship: R=N means a single unreachable replica blocks every read. You have bought ordering with the availability that made you use quorums in the first place. Both dequeues are concurrent with each other, so real time does not order them — but no queue containing one 1 can hand it out twice, so no order works and the checker rejects it. The point of the exercise: the specification is a parameter. Linearizability is not a property of registers; it is a property of any object against any one-at-a-time definition of that object. Change apply and you have a checker for sets, queues, locks or counters.

5.2 (minimum overlap). After minimising to the smallest broken subset, for each operation try widening its interval by 1, 2, 4… ticks and re-check; report the smallest widening that makes the history acceptable.

Why it is useful in a bug report: it converts "this is a consistency violation" into "the two reads were separated by 6 ms, and the system needed them to overlap". That tells the reader whether they are looking at a genuine protocol bug or at a measurement artefact — see 6.4.

5.3 (explain). Run both checkers. If linearizable fails and sequential consistency passes, the culprit is a real-time edge; find it by scanning the minimised subset for a pair (a, b) with response(a) ≤ invocation(b) where extending b's invocation back to before response(a) makes the history acceptable. That pair is the sentence you print. If both checkers fail, no real-time edge is involved — some client saw its own value change and change back, and the message should say that instead.

Rung 6 — Debug

6.1 Caught by buggy_checker_raises_a_false_alarm, which is paired with good_checker_accepts_the_timed_out_write so the contrast is visible. It is a false alarm: the checker rejects history F, which is perfectly explainable by "the write did happen, the client just never heard back".

False alarms are the more damaging failure for an auditing tool, and it is worth being clear why. A missed violation leaves you where you started. A false alarm sends an engineer to hunt a bug that does not exist in a database they did not write — and when they eventually discover the tool was wrong, every genuine finding it ever produced becomes arguable. The credibility of the tool is the product.

6.2 Caught by dropping_the_writer_tiebreak_breaks_it. Physically: two writers query at the same moment, both get "the highest timestamp is 5", and both write timestamp 6 — with different values. Replicas now accept whichever arrives first, so replica A holds (6, "x") and replica B holds (6, "y"). Neither can ever displace the other, because acceptance requires a strictly higher timestamp. The register has silently become two registers.

What a user reports: "the value keeps flipping between two things". Reads alternate depending on which replicas answered, forever, with no error anywhere and no way for the system to notice — the two replicas both believe they are current. This is the same failure as the previous lab's sibling conflict, except that with last-write-wins-by-timestamp there is no sibling to surface.

6.3 Two checks fail: overlapping_write_read_before_and_after_is_ok and same_answers_overlapping_in_time_are_legal. The direction is a false alarm — the invocation-order checker rejects histories that are genuinely linearizable.

This is version 1 from stage 10.1, and the failure is exactly the one predicted there: it assumes the order is given by when requests were sent. In scenario C the write is sent first and takes effect last, which is completely normal and which the invocation-order rule cannot express.

6.4 Whether the two timestamps came from the same clock.

The entire case against this history is the one-tick gap: C1 was answered at 10, C2 asked at 11. If those two numbers were recorded on two different client machines whose clocks differ by more than one tick, the "edge" is an artefact of the measurement, not a fact about the system.

This is why Jepsen records every operation's times on a single node, and why 5.2's "how much overlap was needed" number matters: if the answer is "6 ms" and your clients' clocks are synchronised to within 20 ms, you have not found a bug, you have found your clock skew.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 (deposed leader). The shape: replica 0 serves everything until T; from T, writes go to replica 1 while replica 0 keeps answering reads from its own state for lag ticks. The checker convicts it, and the minimised violation is the same three-bar shape as Meera and Raghu — a completed write on the new leader, and a later read from the old one returning the old value.

Adding a lease means replica 0 stops serving reads at T even though nothing has told it to, because its grant has expired and it has not been renewed. The violations go to zero.

What the lease cost you, and it is three things.

  1. Availability: there is now a window where nobody serves reads — the old leader has stopped and the new one has not started. That window is the lease length, and it is why lease lengths are a tuning fight.
  2. A clock assumption: the lease is a duration, so the two leaders must agree on how long a second is. If the old leader's clock runs slow, it holds its lease past the point where the new one begins, and the violation comes back. Clock skew has become a correctness input.
  3. Latency on renewal: the leader must keep renewing, which is traffic that exists purely to hold the guarantee.

7.2 (a cheaper write-back). The correct optimisation: skip the write-back when every replica in the read quorum already agreed. If all R replicas returned the same timestamp, then that value is already on R replicas, and if any two read quorums must intersect — 2R > N — a later read must touch one of them and cannot go backwards.

Measured (Sim.lazyWriteBack, 300 histories each, and locked in as tests):

configuration violations
N=3, R=2, W=2 — always write back 0
N=3, R=2, W=2 — skip when the quorum agreed (2R = 4 > 3) 0
N=5, R=2, W=4 — skip when the quorum agreed (2R = 4 ≤ 5) 6 of 600
N=5, R=2, W=4 — always write back 0

So: the assumption is 2R > N, not R + W > N. When it is violated — a configuration tuned for fast reads, R=2 on N=5, which is a perfectly sensible thing to want — the optimisation silently stops being correct while the counting rule you were relying on still holds. Two read quorums that do not intersect each other cannot keep each other in order.

The other cheap scheme is the leader lease from 7.1: one machine answers reads locally with no quorum round trip at all. The assumption there is bounded clock drift, and 7.1 lists what breaks when it fails.

7.3 (retry after a timeout). A pending put(1) may or may not have taken effect, and the client cannot tell. Retrying put(1) is safe because the second execution leaves the value exactly where the first one would have: the operation is idempotent, so "once" and "twice" are indistinguishable in the final state.

increment is not. If the first attempt succeeded and the client retries, the counter goes up twice, and the client — which never got either answer — has no way to detect it. This is the previous lab's "a failed write is never rolled back" finding, arriving from the other direction.

What the server must add: a per-operation identity that survives the retry. The client generates a request id, sends it with the operation, and the server records "I have already applied request abc123" and returns the original result instead of applying it again. Note where the state has to live — on the server, durably, ordered with the write itself, which is why an idempotency key table is written in the same transaction as the payment.

Rung 8 — Design

These are discussions, not single answers. What follows is one defensible position with its reasoning exposed.

8.1 — Payments, three regions

Read Linearizable? Why
dashboard balance No a human reading a number; a second of staleness is invisible and the extra cross-region round trip is not. Show a timestamp ("as of 10:04:12") and the staleness stops being a lie
balance check inside an authorisation Yes this read decides whether money moves. A stale balance authorises an overdraft, and the loss is real money. This is the read you pay for
statements page No historical data; immutable once written. Nothing to be stale about
fraud feature lookup No a model input, already probabilistic, already computed from a window. Making it strongly consistent adds latency to the hot path for no decision quality
idempotency-key check Yes, and more this decides whether a payment already happened. Getting it wrong is a double charge. It also needs to be atomic with the capture itself, which means a compare-and-set or a transaction — not just a linearizable read (experiment 8 is the reason)

Placement. Shard by account, and put each shard's leader in the region that account is used from — this is what "region affinity" buys, and it means the common case never crosses an ocean. The consensus group still spans three regions for durability, so a write pays one cross-region round trip to reach a majority.

Numbers. With regions ~110 ms apart, a majority for a leader in Mumbai is Mumbai plus the nearer of the other two: roughly 110–120 ms for a write, and for a linearizable read a lease read is local (single-digit ms) while a read-index read costs one round trip to a majority (~110 ms). Budget p99 higher than that; queueing under load is what actually sets p99, not the speed of light.

What to monitor. Leader elections per hour, lease expiries, read-index round trip, replication lag, and clock skew across the fleet — the last one is a correctness metric because leases depend on it. Alert on non-monotonic reads observed by a canary client if you can afford one; that is the only symptom that a stale cache and a stale replica cannot disguise.

What you tell the product team. Not "consistency is slow". Say: the dashboard reads from the nearest copy and is up to a second behind, which is why it is fast. Authorisation reads from the copy that decides, which is why it takes an extra 110 ms. If we made the dashboard read the deciding copy, every page load would cost that 110 ms and the number on screen would still be stale by the time it reached the browser. That last clause is the one that ends the argument, and it is true: linearizability is a guarantee about the moment the answer was produced, not about the moment the human read it.

8.2 — A lock you can trust

Do not rely on the lock for correctness. Rely on the resource.

  • The lock is a lease with a TTL, used to make double-running rare — a performance optimisation, not a guarantee.
  • The token: acquiring the lock returns a monotonically increasing number.
  • The guarantee: every write in the capture path carries the token, and the storage layer rejects any write whose token is lower than the highest it has seen. Or, equivalently and often more simply, every capture is written against an idempotency key so a second attempt is a no-op.

Clock assumption: none for correctness. The TTL affects only how long you wait before a second worker starts; if the clocks are wrong you get more double-runs, not incorrect ones.

A 90-second pause mid-run: the lease expires, worker B starts and captures with token 8. Worker A wakes up believing it still holds the lock and tries to write with token 7. The storage rejects it. A discovers it has been fenced and stops. Nothing incorrect happened, and no clock had to be right.

The general shape is worth carrying: a guarantee enforced at the place the damage would occur beats a guarantee enforced at the place the decision was made.

8.3 — The read that must not be linearizable

A read whose job is to be available. The clearest example in a payments system is the card authorisation path during a regional partition, and the answer is a business decision, not a technical one.

If the region holding the leader for an account is unreachable, a linearizable read must refuse — that is the CAP theorem, not a design flaw. The result is that every card transaction for those accounts declines. For a small window and a large exposure, declining is right. For a large window and small transactions, declining every coffee purchase in a country is worse for the business than accepting a bounded overdraft risk — which is why real card networks have offline authorisation floor limits: below a value threshold, approve locally without contacting the issuer, and reconcile later.

What you break by making it strongly consistent: availability during exactly the failure the design exists to survive, in exchange for eliminating a risk you had already priced and capped. The second clearest example is a rate limiter or fraud counter — both are already approximations, and paying a consensus round trip per request to make an approximation exact is spending latency on the wrong thing.
