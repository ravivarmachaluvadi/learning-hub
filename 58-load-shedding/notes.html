Lab 58 · Running It In Production

Load Shedding — choosing who to disappoint, before the choice is made for you

Tell a friend: Dana can finish 15 passport applications an hour, and this morning 60 people walk in, each with an 11:00 train to catch. She works flat out all morning and about one person catches their train, because she spends the rest of it filling in forms for people who already left. Put Ben on the door with a clipboard, turning people away once the waiting room is full, and the same Dana at the same speed sends home roughly five hundred times as many passports. Your service is Dana's desk: offered 60 requests a second when it can finish 50, it serves 0.7% of them, not 83%, while its throughput graph reads a flat and healthy 50/s. One line of admission control turns that 0.7% into 83.6%, and this lab is about which line, and where it goes.

BEGINNER MODEL — stages 1 to 6

1. Why does an outage start when nothing has broken?

In one breath: More work arrived than the service could finish. No component failed, no alert fired, and out of every thousand customers, one got an answer.

It is 09:58 and Meera is on call. Her orders service is healthy. It has four worker threads. Each request takes about 80 ms, so the service finishes about 50 requests per second. Traffic is 35/s. Median response 82 ms. No errors.

At 10:00 a promotion goes live and traffic goes to 150/s.

Nothing crashes. No process dies, no disk fills, no dependency returns an error, no deploy went out. Every worker thread stays busy. The throughput graph, the one on the wall that the alert is wired to, goes to 50 requests per second and stays there, flat and level, for forty minutes.

And during those forty minutes, out of every thousand customers who press the button, one gets an answer.

Before the whole incident, stop on the least dramatic row of it. Traffic has not tripled yet. It has gone up by a fifth.

🔮 Predict: The service can finish 50 requests a second. Offer it 60 a second — one fifth more work than it can do, sustained. What share of those customers get an answer: about 83%, about half, or something else?

Reveal

0.7%.

If you said 83%, you did the arithmetic everyone does, and the arithmetic is right about the work: 50 of every 60 requests can be finished, so 10 are lost. It is wrong about which 50, because the ten that cannot be finished do not politely leave. They join the queue, wait, and push everybody behind them past the point where the answer is still wanted. The service finishes 50 requests a second all morning. Almost none of them belong to a customer who is still listening.

That is a 20% overload producing a 99.3% failure. Stage 6 puts a doorman in front of the same service at the same 60/s, and the number becomes 83.6%.

Here is the whole incident in one table. Read the third column against the second.

offered load what the throughput graph shows customers who got an answer what the row means
35/s (normal) 34.9/s 100.0% spare capacity; everyone served
45/s 44.9/s 99.3% 90% of capacity, still comfortable
50/s (exactly capacity) 50.0/s unstable — see below not an operating point at all
55/s 50.1/s 1.2% 10% too much work, almost total loss
60/s 50.0/s 0.7% a fifth too much work, not a fifth of the loss
150/s 50.1/s 0.1% the promotion; one in a thousand

Three things in that table are worth stopping on.

The throughput column is useless. It reads 50/s during total success and 50/s during total failure. It is the single most-watched number in most production dashboards, and here it cannot distinguish a healthy service from a dead one.

The collapse is not proportional to the overload. Going 20% over capacity does not cost you 20% of your customers. It costs you 99.3% of them.

There is no bug to find. The service is doing exactly what it was built to do, as fast as it can, on every single request. That is the problem.

And one row of that table refuses to hold still. Offer the service exactly what it can do, 50 a second against a capacity of 50 a second, and the same configuration gives a different answer every time you run it.

exactly at capacity: twenty runs of one configuration figure what it means
best run of the twenty 70.6% a good day, decided by luck
worst run of the twenty 1.1% the same settings, the same load
mean over ten minutes 28.6% the average is not a promise
mean over one hour 11.7% the longer it runs, the worse it gets

That spread is not noise in the measurement. It is the answer. Your queueing-theory lab named the ratio of arriving work to work you can finish — busy-ness, written ρ. At ρ = 1, arrivals and departures balance exactly, so the queue is a random walk with nothing pulling it back down. A random walk has no steady state to settle into. Whether you are having a good day is decided by luck, and the longer the day lasts the worse it gets. Every other figure in this lab is a stable mean over five runs. This one cannot be, and that is the point: "we are running at exactly 100% of capacity" is not an operating point. It is a coin toss that gets worse with time.

What goes wrong if you do not know this? You spend the incident looking for a component that failed, and there isn't one. You add capacity, which helps, and you conclude the answer was capacity. So it happens again next quarter, at a different service, at a different scale. Meanwhile the thing that would have turned that 0.1% into 33% was a change you could have shipped in an afternoon, and it has been available the entire time.

2. What happens at Dana's desk when everyone has a train to catch?

In one breath: One clerk, sixty people, one train. She works the whole morning without a break and almost nobody gets a passport.

Forget servers for a moment. You met Dana in your queueing-theory lab, at the only desk in a passport office. Today she is on the long form, and we are going to break her morning differently.

Each application takes Dana exactly 4 minutes. She is fast, accurate, and never stops. That is her capacity: 15 people an hour. It is a fact about Dana, and no decision anyone makes today will change it.

This morning, because of a deadline in the news, 60 people arrive in the first hour. Four times what Dana can do. Priya is one of them, and she is somewhere in the middle of the crowd.

There is one more fact, and it is the one that matters most. Every person here has a train to catch at 11:00. If Priya is still in the queue at 11:00, she leaves. She walks out with nothing — no passport, no partial passport, nothing. Her hour in your building bought her exactly zero.

That deadline is not a detail. It is the entire subject. A queue without a deadline is a waiting room. A queue with one is a countdown on every person in it, and the countdown runs whether or not Dana is working hard.

🔮 Predict: Dana works flat out for the full hour with no breaks and no mistakes. She physically processes 15 applications. Of the 60 people who walked in, how many walk out with a passport?

Reveal

About one.

Not fifteen. Dana processes fifteen applications, but she processes them in the order people arrived. By the time she reaches the fifth person that person has been waiting 16 minutes. By the fifteenth they have been waiting nearly an hour, and they left for the train some time ago. Dana spends most of her morning filling in forms for people who are no longer in the building.

The service at 3× capacity does the same thing: 0.1% success, 100% utilisation, and 100% of its working time spent on requests whose client had already given up.

Hold on to the shape of that: she did all the work, and almost none of it counted. Stage 4 walks through exactly how the count gets to one.

3. What is wrong with letting everyone in?

In one breath: The office manager's plan is four decent-sounding rules. Your web framework already ships all four as defaults, and together they are the outage.

Before we fix anything, let us be fair to the design that fails. It is the design almost every system ships with — not through carelessness, but because it is the decent thing to do.

Arjun manages the office. You met him in queueing-theory writing capacity on a whiteboard. Here is his plan for the morning:

  1. Let everyone in. Turning people away when the desk is right there and Dana is willing to work feels like a failure of service.
  2. Serve them in the order they arrived. That is fair, and everyone understands it.
  3. Keep the queue in the corridor if the waiting room fills. Space is cheap.
  4. Ask Dana to hurry.

Every one of those four is defensible on its own. Three of them are things you would say out loud in a meeting without anyone objecting.

Here is what Arjun's plan looks like in a service, and you have almost certainly shipped this configuration:

// A thread pool with an unbounded queue: "let everyone in"
new ThreadPoolExecutor(4, 4, 0L, MILLISECONDS, new LinkedBlockingQueue<>());

// Spring Boot's default task queue capacity
spring.task.execution.pool.queue-capacity = 2147483647

(Illustrative, not runnable. LinkedBlockingQueue with no capacity argument is unbounded, and that Spring default is Integer.MAX_VALUE. Both are step 3 of Arjun's plan, shipped as a default.)

Nobody chose this. It is what you get by not choosing.

4. Where does the morning's work actually go?

In one breath: Dana does every minute of work she is capable of. After 10:20, not one of those minutes belongs to somebody still in the building.

Let us watch the morning minute by minute. Dana opens at 10:00. Sixty people arrive spread over the hour — call it one per minute. Everyone leaves at 11:00. Read the third column: it is how long that person has been in the building when Dana finally calls them.

clock who Dana is serving how long they have waited what happens
10:00 person 1 0 min ✅ served, catches the train
10:04 person 2 3 min ✅ served
10:08 person 3 6 min ✅ served
10:20 person 6 15 min ✅ served
10:40 person 11 30 min ✅ served
10:56 person 15 42 min ✅ served — the last one who makes it
11:00 — — the other 45 people walk out

So far so good. Dana served 15 and 45 left. That is bad, but it is the honest arithmetic of 60 people and a 15-person capacity. Nothing is surprising yet.

Now change one thing to match reality, and the floor falls out.

Real queues are not one-per-minute. People arrive in clumps. And crucially, in a real service the deadline belongs to the client, not to the server. Priya's phone gives up after one second and shows her an error. The server never finds out. Her request is still sitting in the queue. Its work still gets done. Its response is still written, to a connection nobody is listening on.

So replace "people leave the building" with what actually happens: Priya leaves for her train, and Dana still fills in her form. Dana has no way to know the chair is empty.

Now walk the morning again. By 10:20 the queue is 20 deep and growing by 45 people an hour. Anyone joining at 10:20 will not reach the desk until well after 11:00. So from 10:20 onward, every single person who joins the queue is guaranteed to have gone before Dana reaches them. Dana keeps working at exactly 15 applications an hour, all morning. After 10:20 not one of those applications belongs to a person who is still in the building.

The service does the same thing, and it is worth seeing the exact numbers. This is one run at 3× capacity with nobody on the door.

what you would watch the run at 3× capacity what the number means
offered 150 requests/s what arrived at the door
throughput 50.1/s — full capacity, all day requests finished per second
goodput 0.1/s finished in time to matter

Read the last row twice. During a total outage, the latency of the requests that did succeed looks fine. The only ones that succeed are the lucky few that arrived when the queue happened to be short. Your latency dashboard is computed over survivors. A metric computed only over survivors cannot detect that everyone else died.

And here is the part that makes this an emergency rather than a curiosity: that queue of 60,047 is a linked list in your heap. It grows at 100 entries per second, each holding a request object, its headers, its body, its trace context. The outage that started as "slow" ends as an OutOfMemoryError, a restart, and a cold cache. At which point the still-overloaded traffic hits an empty cache and the whole thing happens again, faster.

This is the moment the lesson turns. Everything up to here is a restatement of what queueing-theory proved. What follows is the response.

5. What are we actually asking for, in plain words?

In one breath: At four times the work she can do, Dana was always going to disappoint most of the room. The question is which people, and how long they stand there before they find out.

Look at what actually went wrong. It was not that requests were lost. At 150/s against a capacity of 50/s, 100 requests per second were always going to be lost. That is arithmetic, not a design flaw. No configuration, no rewrite, no language change makes 4 workers finish 150 requests a second.

What went wrong is which ones were lost, and when they found out.

Which: the system lost almost all of them, including the ones it had capacity to serve. Every request was allowed to consume a share of a resource that none of them could get enough of.

When: Priya found out after a full second of waiting. She had occupied memory and a connection the whole time, and in many designs her phone immediately tried again.

So the requirement is this:

When more work arrives than we can finish, decide at the door who is not getting served, and tell them immediately. Then spend everything we have left on the ones we said yes to, so that the yeses actually complete in time.

Three separate demands hide in that sentence, and the rest of the lesson is three mechanisms answering them.

At the door. The decision must happen before the request consumes a worker, a connection, or a megabyte. A rejection that costs as much as the work is not a rejection.

Immediately. Priya must learn quickly. A client that is still waiting is still holding resources on both sides. And a fast "no" is something she can act on (fall back, degrade, try elsewhere), while a slow "no" is not.

The yeses complete. Admitting fewer requests is only worth doing if the admitted ones then succeed. Which means the number you admit must be tied to what you can actually finish, and that number changes.

Now we can name the metric the throughput graph was missing. Throughput is how much work you finish per second. Goodput is how much work you finish per second that somebody still wanted. In stage 4's table they were 50.1/s and 0.1/s. Throughput is a fact about your CPU. Goodput is a fact about your business, and it is the only one of the two that can tell a healthy service from a dead one.

6. What does putting one doorman on the door do to the numbers?

In one breath: Ben stands at the door and turns people away once the waiting room is full. Dana works no faster. Roughly five hundred times as many people catch their train.

Here is the whole idea, and it is genuinely this small:

Before a request is allowed to join the queue, something looks at how backed up we already are and says yes or no. A no is returned instantly and costs almost nothing.

That is load shedding. The thing making the decision is called an admission controller. In the office, it is Ben, with a clipboard, standing at the door.

The counterintuitive part, the reason this is a lesson and not a footnote, is what Ben does to the numbers.

Same office. Same Dana. Same 4-minute applications. Same 60 people at the door. Now Ben turns people away whenever the waiting room already holds more than a handful.

3× overload, capacity 50/s, best achievable = 33.3% goodput success rate queue depth
no admission control 0.1/s 0.1% 60,047
queue capped at 20 50.0/s 33.3% 20

Turning away two-thirds of the arrivals increased the number of people served by roughly five hundred times.

And look at the right-hand column of that table. 33.3% is not an improvement on the way to something better. 50 requests per second is what the service can finish, 150 are arriving, so 33.3% is the mathematical ceiling. A twenty-slot queue reaches it exactly. There is nothing left on the table.

That is the first counterintuitive result. Here is the second, which is the one that changes how you feel about shipping this. Read the two right-hand columns against each other at every level of traffic, including the ordinary ones.

offered load no shedding queue capped at 20 what the row settles
35/s (normal) 100.0% 100.0% the doorman costs nothing when it is quiet
45/s 99.3% 98.9% at 90% of capacity, four tenths of a percent
60/s (a fifth too much) 0.7% 83.6% stage 1's headline, answered
150/s (3×) 0.1% 33.3% the ceiling, reached exactly

The 60/s row is the one to remember. The arithmetic ceiling there is 83.3%, because 50 of every 60 requests can be finished. The capped queue lands on 83.6%, which is that ceiling plus the ordinary scatter between runs. The unprotected service, same hardware, same traffic, gives 0.7%.

(50/s is left out of that table on purpose — see stage 1: at exactly capacity the unshielded number is not a number. The capped column is stable there at around 96%, which is itself the point: admission control gives ρ = 1 a defined behaviour it does not otherwise have.)

Ben costs nothing when the office is not busy. He is not a big red button you press during an incident. He stands there every day, waves everyone through every day, and the one morning in two years when he is needed, he is already at his post and already calibrated. A shedder that only gets switched on during incidents is a shedder that has never been tested, configured by people under pressure, on the worst day of their quarter.

Say it back. Without using the words shed, goodput or admission, explain to Arjun why turning away two-thirds of the people at the door means more people go home with a passport.

A good answer sounds like

"Dana can do fifteen an hour and sixty are coming, so forty-five of them are going home empty no matter what we do. The only thing we choose is when they find out. If we let them all queue, they all wait. The ones at the front have already left for the train by the time Dana calls them, so she fills in forms for empty chairs and almost nobody gets served. If we tell forty-five of them at the door, they lose two seconds instead of an hour. And everyone still in the room is close enough to the front that Dana's work reaches a real person."

Checkpoint. You can now explain why load shedding exists, why it increases the number of requests served, and why goodput is the metric that shows it. What you cannot yet do is build one — because "how backed up are we" turns out to be a genuinely hard question with at least four defensible answers, three of which break in production. That is the next layer.

INTERMEDIATE MODEL — stages 7 to 10

7. Which five words are being used as if they were one?

In one breath: Five different mechanisms all end with somebody not being served. They are not interchangeable, and the difference is the whole design.

Your distributed-rate-limiting lab put "rate limit vs quota vs throttling vs load shedding" in a single glossary line. That line was hiding the most important distinction in this subject, so let us pull it apart. The question that separates them is: whose interest is it protecting, and what does it know?

Rate limiting protects you from a particular client. Ben checks a list: has this travel agent already brought in 1,000 people this month? That is a question about identity and a promise, answered the same way whether the office is empty or on fire. It is a contract, and its threshold is a business decision. It cannot save you from overload, because overload usually arrives as a million clients each behaving perfectly.

Quota is the same thing over a longer window and usually with money attached.

Throttling is the general verb for slowing a client down — usually rate limiting's enforcement half.

Load shedding protects the service from all clients at once. Ben looks over his shoulder at the waiting room and asks: can Dana finish what she already has? That question is about your own internal state, its answer changes second by second, and it has nothing to do with who is asking. Priya is a perfectly well-behaved client and Priya gets shed. That feels unfair, and it is. It is also the only thing that works, because the alternative is that everyone gets nothing.

Rate limiting asks "are you allowed?" Load shedding asks "can I?"

A system can have both. It needs both. They fire at different times, for different reasons, and a rate limiter tuned tight enough to prevent overload would have to be so tight that it rejects your legitimate traffic every day.

Backpressure is the fourth, and it is shedding's opposite number. It means refusing to accept work and letting the pressure propagate backwards to whoever is producing it. TCP's receive window, a bounded queue that blocks its producer, Kafka consumer lag. It is the better answer when it is available, because nothing is thrown away; the producer simply slows down. It is available when the producer is something that can wait. It is not available when the producer is forty thousand phones that will each retry in two seconds. Shedding is what you do when there is nowhere to push back to.

Admission control is the neutral, precise name for the machinery: the component that decides yes or no at the entrance. Load shedding is what it is called when it says no because you are overloaded.

Here are the five side by side. The last column is where you have already met the thing.

mechanism protects the question it asks fires when first seen
rate limiting you, from one client are you allowed? a contract is exceeded distributed-rate-limiting
quota the same, over a longer window have you paid for this much? a billing period fills distributed-rate-limiting
throttling the enforcement half of both how do I slow you down? the limiter says no distributed-rate-limiting
load shedding the service, from everyone can I finish what I have? your own queue backs up stage 6, Ben at the door
backpressure the whole chain, losing nothing can the producer wait? the producer can be slowed queueing-theory

Four more terms you now need, each introduced because you have already felt the thing it names.

Goodput — work completed in time to be useful, as opposed to throughput, which is work completed. Stage 4's 50.1/s versus 0.1/s.

Offered load — what arrives, as opposed to what you serve. In stage 4 the offered load was 150/s while throughput was 50/s. The difference is not visible anywhere on a server-side graph unless you deliberately count rejections.

Overload — the state where offered load exceeds the rate at which you can finish work. Note it is defined against your completion rate, not your CPU. A service can be 100% overloaded at 3% CPU if its workers are blocked waiting on a slow database. This matters enormously in stage 11.

Deadline — how long an answer is still worth having. Usually the client's timeout. Priya's 11:00 train. Almost always known to the client and unknown to the server, which is the single largest missing piece of information in this entire subject, and the reason stage 13 spends time on deadline propagation.

8. Which two pictures of the doorman should you carry?

In one breath: One picture is Ben glancing at the waiting room. The other is a thermostat. They are the same machine, and the second one tells you what to change.

The simple picture: what does Ben actually look at?

One door, one waiting room, one desk. Ben does not know anything about who is arriving, why, or how important they are. He knows one thing: how many people are already inside waiting. If it is more than some number, the next person is told "not today" before they sit down, and they are told it in two seconds rather than fifty minutes.

That picture is enough to get every result in stage 6 right, and you should keep it.

The engineer's picture: why is Ben a control loop?

Ben is a feedback controller, the same shape as the thermostat on your wall. Naming its four parts is what lets you reason about every design in the rest of this lab.

        ┌─────────────── measured signal ───────────────┐
        │      (queue depth, queue delay, in-flight)    │
        ▼                                               │
   ┌─────────┐                                    ┌──────────┐
   │ compare │  ── error ──▶  ┌────────────┐      │ THE      │
   │ against │                │ actuator:  │─────▶│ SERVICE  │

The signal is what you measure to decide whether you are overloaded. The set point is the value of that signal you are trying to hold. The actuator is the only thing this loop can do: admit, or reject. The plant is the service itself, whose behaviour changes what you measure next.

Every load-shedding design in production is a choice of signal and set point. That is genuinely all that varies. Stage 10 is four different choices of signal, in the order that the failures of each one force the next.

Where does the doorman story lie to you?

An analogy that is never corrected becomes the learner's actual model, so here is where this one stops being true. Each break is a real failure mode later in the lab.

A rejected person goes home. A rejected client comes straight back. This is the big one. Ben's job gets easier as he turns people away. Your shedder's job can get harder, because a 503 arrives at a client library configured to retry, and a retry is a new arrival. Stage 11 puts a number on it: a correct shedder at 150 offered requests/s was processing 394 admission decisions per second. Its own rejections had become 62% of its input.

Ben's "no" is free. Yours is not. Saying no to an HTTP request still costs a TCP accept, a TLS handshake, header parsing, a log line and a response write. Small — but multiplied by an overload. Stage 11 shows a service that became completely unable to serve anyone purely from the cost of saying no, at an offered load it could otherwise have shed comfortably.

Ben can see the whole waiting room. Your service cannot see the deadline. He knows about the 11:00 train. Your server does not know Priya's timeout unless someone sends it. The most valuable single upgrade in this whole lab is deadline propagation, in stage 13. It is the act of telling the server what Ben could simply see.

Dana's speed never changes. Yours changes constantly. A passport takes 4 minutes today and 4 minutes next year. Your 80 ms request becomes a 400 ms request the moment a downstream database gets slower. When it does, your capacity silently drops from 50/s to 10/s, and every fixed threshold you configured is now wrong by a factor of five. Stage 10's v2 exists entirely because of this, and it is where most real shedding configurations fail.

10. What should the doorman actually look at?

In one breath: Ben needs one number to decide on. Four different numbers are defensible, each one is broken by a specific morning, and the break is what produces the next.

We now build the shedder properly. Each version is a real design that real systems ship. Each one is broken by a specific scenario, and every scenario is the same office with one thing changed.

Throughout: 4 workers × 80 ms = capacity 50 requests/s. Client timeout 1 second. Offered load 150/s unless stated. At 150/s offered against 50/s capacity, 33.3% is the best any design can possibly do.

v1 — what if Ben just counts the room?

The simplest signal there is: how many requests are waiting? Ben glances at the chairs, counts heads, and stops letting people in above some number.

if (queue.size() >= MAX_QUEUE) return HTTP_503;
queue.add(request);

This is one line, it is available in every framework, and it is genuinely excellent. The only question is what MAX_QUEUE should be.

Your queueing-theory lab derived a rule for exactly this. A request arriving behind q others, where there are c workers each taking S seconds, waits q·S/c. In words: everybody ahead of you, times how long each takes, shared between the desks. For it to finish inside a deadline D:

q_max = c · (D/S − 1) = 4 × (1000/80 − 1) = 46

Three findings, and all three matter more than the winning number.

A cap of 100 is worth exactly nothing. Same 0.1% as no cap at all. This is the most common way shedding is shipped and then found not to work. Somebody bounds the queue at a number that sounds small next to 60,047. They have bounded it at eight times too large. "We have a bounded queue, so we're protected" is false. There is a specific number and 100 is not it.

The formula is a ceiling, not a setting. 46 is the cap at which the average admitted request just barely makes its deadline — which means about half of them don't. The sweep says 18.7%, a bit over half of optimal. Any deadline-derived cap needs dividing by two or three before use.

There is no sharp peak — there is a plateau. Anything from 30 down to 2 is within a whisker of optimal. You do not have to tune this well. You have to tune it small. Which is queueing-theory's "shed hard or don't bother", arriving from the other direction.

What breaks v1? The number 80 was not a constant

Every threshold above was derived from S = 80 ms. That number is not a property of your code. It is a property of your code and everything it calls.

So leave the traffic exactly where it is, at a comfortable 60/s, and let a downstream database get slower. Replica lag, a bad query plan, a noisy neighbour, a GC pause. Service time goes from 80 ms to 400 ms. Nothing else changes; the same requests arrive at the same rate.

In the office: the passport system starts taking five times as long to load each record. Dana is not slower. Her desk is slower, and it happens between one person and the next.

This is the single most important failure in the lab, because it is not a bug you can find by reading the config. Any threshold expressed in units of work, such as queue entries, in-flight requests or connections, silently encodes an assumption about how long a unit of work takes. When service time changes, every one of those thresholds is wrong by the same factor.

The fix is to stop counting the proxy and measure the thing you actually care about.

v2 — what if Ben measures time instead of heads?

You never cared how many people were in the chairs. You cared whether somebody joining now would still be there when their turn came. So measure queue delay: how long are requests currently waiting between being accepted and being picked up by a worker?

Ben stops counting heads. He starts timing how long the last person sat before Dana called them.

That signal is self-correcting by construction. It does not need to know S, the time one application takes. If requests get slower, the delay Ben measures grows, and he sheds harder, with nobody redeploying anything.

There are two ways to build it, and the difference is instructive.

v2a predicts the wait. Keep a running estimate of service time and multiply: predictedWait = queueLength × Ŝ / workers. In words: heads in the room, times your best guess of how long each takes, shared between the desks. Reject if that exceeds a target. This is v1 with the constant replaced by a measurement.

v2b measures the wait you already caused. Don't predict. Look at how long the request you just dequeued actually sat there. This is the approach Facebook published in "Fail at Scale" (ACM Queue, 2015), adapting the CoDel algorithm from network queueing. Their rule, paraphrased:

If the queue has been continuously non-empty for a whole interval (say 100 ms), it is a standing queue: a backlog, not a burst. The tolerated waiting time then collapses from the interval down to a small target (say 5 ms). Anything that waited longer than the current tolerance is dropped when it reaches the front.

The elegance is in the two-mode behaviour. A queue that keeps draining to empty is absorbing a burst, and is left alone. A queue that never drains is a backlog, and gets cut aggressively. It tells "busy" from "underwater" using a signal that costs one timestamp per request.

Now the test that v1 failed: service time changing from 80 ms to 400 ms under constant traffic. The right-hand column is the one that matters — it asks whether anybody had to tell the design that the world had changed.

v3 — can the service find its own limit?

If a good limit is discoverable from measurements, why configure one at all? Let a controller search for it, continuously, the way TCP searches for the right window size. Arjun stops giving Ben a number, and Ben starts finding his own.

AIMD (additive increase, multiplicative decrease) is the shape you already know from TCP congestion control: creep the limit up while things are going well, cut it hard the moment they are not.

on a fast success:   limit += 1/limit      // gentle, additive
on a congestion hit: limit *= 0.85         // sharp, multiplicative

What breaks v3? An absolute threshold is a disguised constant

The AIMD above needs to know what "a congestion hit" means, and the obvious implementation is a latency threshold: slower than 400 ms means congested.

Which is a constant. With units of time. Chosen when S was 80 ms.

When S becomes 400 ms, every completion is slower than 400 ms. So the controller receives a congestion signal on literally every request and grinds its limit down to the floor. It delivered 10.1% against a 16.7% ceiling while running its workers at only 66% utilisation. It was shedding requests it had the capacity to serve, because it could not tell "I have a backlog" apart from "this work is just slower now".

The fix production libraries use is to make the signal relative: compare the service's recent latency to its own longer-run latency. A ratio has no units and no built-in assumption about how fast the work is. This is the gradient family in Netflix's concurrency-limits library:

gradient   = clamp(longWindowLatency / shortWindowLatency, 0.5, 1.0)
newLimit   = limit × gradient + sqrt(limit)     // sqrt = the standing queue we allow
limit      = smooth(limit, newLimit)

An honest report on building this. Three versions of that ratio were tried while writing this lab, and the first two were wrong in ways worth knowing about.

The first compares the minimum latency to the mean latency. That looks natural and is broken. With realistically variable service times the minimum is near zero, so the ratio is pinned at its 0.5 floor and the limit sticks at 4 forever. Bad at both ends, as the table below shows.

The second compares a short window to a long window. That fixes the healthy case and then runs away under sustained overload, because the long window is an average of the same bad samples and slowly accepts the overload as normal. The gradient converges to 1, the limit grows without bound, and goodput reaches zero.

The third adds a hard multiplicative decrease on an actual drop — a completion whose client had already given up. That fixes the runaway. Netflix's library does exactly this, and it was not an embellishment.

version of the ratio healthy 35/s (ceiling 100%) 3× overload (ceiling 33.3%) what went wrong
minimum vs mean latency 82.3% 30.3% ratio pinned at its floor, limit stuck at 4
short window vs long window 99.9% goodput 0 limit ran away to 346 under sustained load
plus a decrease on a real drop 96.9% 31.9% behaves everywhere; Netflix ships this

The corrected version also beats absolute-threshold AIMD on the changed-service-time test: 14.5% against 10.1%. But it did not beat a well-chosen static limit at steady load. And under permanently sustained overload it stays pinned at its minimum, because the drop signal never stops arriving, and no AIMD-shaped controller can distinguish "I should let more in" from "I am still drowning".

The honest conclusion, which the marketing for these libraries does not volunteer. Adaptive concurrency control is genuinely better when your service time moves, and genuinely harder to get right than a queue-delay rule. Its failure modes are subtle enough that all three of the versions above look correct in code review. If you are choosing one thing to ship, ship v2b and measure queue delay. It gets most of the benefit for a fraction of the risk, which is why CoDel-shaped designs are what Facebook and Envoy converged on.

v5 — what are the other two levers?

Everything so far controls who gets in. Two other decisions are made after that. They are independent of admission control, and one of them produces the most surprising number in this lab.

Lever 2 is what order Dana calls people in. First-come-first-served is fair and it is the default everywhere. Under overload it is also the worst possible order. The person at the front of a backed-up queue is by definition the one who has been waiting longest, which makes them the one most likely to have left for the train already. Engineers call that order FIFO, and under overload FIFO serves the corpses first. LIFO means calling the newest arrival, so it always picks the freshest person in the building.

Lever 3 is who Dana gives up on. If Priya has been waiting 990 ms and her deadline is 1,000 ms, starting 80 ms of work on her form is pure waste. Check before you start.

ENGINEERING MODEL — stages 11 to 12

11. What are the eight ways this goes wrong around a working shedder?

In one breath: Ben is doing his job perfectly in every one of the next eight stories. The service still falls over in all eight.

F1 — is a rejection really free?

Every design so far assumed a 503 costs nothing. It does not. A TCP accept, a TLS handshake, header parsing, a routing decision, a metric increment, an access log line, a response write. Call it 2 ms of CPU, a deliberately modest number for a JVM service behind TLS.

In the office: every person Ben turns away still needs greeting, checking and a note in the book. It takes him a few seconds. He is one man.

Four workers give you 4 milliseconds of work per millisecond of wall clock. If a rejection costs 2 ms, then 2,000 rejections per second consumes the entire machine. Not 2,000 requests. 2,000 refusals.

Here is the service with an in-flight limit of 12, doing its job correctly the whole way down the table. The last column is what is left for Dana.

And treat "cost per rejection" as a number you have actually put a stopwatch on, because your capacity to shed is a real capacity with a real ceiling. A shedder is a service, and it can be overloaded.

F2 — what happens when your own 503s become your traffic?

Return a 503 to a client library configured with three attempts, and you have not removed a request. You have scheduled two more.

Dev wrote the phone app. His retry policy is three attempts with a 200 ms pause, which is the default in most client libraries and looks entirely sensible in review. Here is what it does to Ben's door at 150/s offered, with a queue cap of 20.

Two results here, and the second one contradicts advice you have probably given.

The offered load nearly tripled and goodput did not move. All that extra work is pure overhead. Combined with F1, that is the mechanism: 394 decisions/s at 2 ms each is 0.8 of your 4 workers, gone, to no purpose.

Backoff and jitter made no difference at all. 393 against 394 against 395. This is worth being precise about, because "add exponential backoff with jitter" is the standard advice, and it is not wrong. It is aimed at a different problem. Backoff and jitter fix synchronisation: they stop a thundering herd of clients retrying in lockstep at the same instant. Under sustained overload there is no herd to de-synchronise. Every client will exhaust its attempts eventually, and spreading them over a second rather than 200 ms changes when they arrive, not how many. Jitter is a fix for correlation, not for volume.

What actually reduced the volume was the last row: not retrying the rejection at all. Which makes sense once stated plainly. A 503 from a load shedder is the one error in your system that carries a guarantee: the work was definitely not done, and the service is definitely already at its limit. Retrying it is the one case where you know in advance the retry cannot help.

What to do about it. Distinguish 503-from-overload (Retry-After present, back off substantially or fail immediately) from 502/504, which is worth one retry. Use a retry budget rather than per-request attempt counts, because a budget is a global constraint and an attempt count is not. Google's SRE book suggests capping retries at ~10% of a client's request volume. And your distributed-rate-limiting lab's warning applies with full force here: a dishonest Retry-After makes your own header the thing that synchronises the next wave. If you say 1 second, mean it, and add jitter on the server side so that every client you rejected in the same millisecond does not come back in the same millisecond.

This is the failure that makes shedding non-optional, and your queueing-theory lab measured its first half.

A healthy service at 35/s. Workers 69% busy, comfortable. At t = 120 s, in one instant, 3,000 extra requests arrive at once. A cache expired, a mobile app woke up, a partner batch fired. It is a single burst. The traffic rate never changes again.

F4 — why is CPU the wrong signal, when it is the one everybody reaches for?

The instinct is to shed on CPU: "if CPU > 90%, start rejecting." It is available on every host and needs no code.

Be careful here, because the most authoritative source in the field disagrees with the simple version of this advice. Google's SRE book, chapter 21, states that their per-task overload protection uses CPU rate as its primary utilisation signal, sometimes combined with memory. CPU rate there means current CPU consumption divided by the CPU reserved for the task. So "never shed on CPU" would be wrong.

The reconciliation is in that phrase reserved for the task. Google's signal is CPU against a quota, on RPC servers whose work is CPU-bound and whose thread pools are tightly controlled. In that setting CPU rate is a direct measure of how close you are to your capacity to finish work. The failure below is what happens when you copy the signal into a setting where that is not true.

Where CPU cannot see the overload. A service whose workers are blocked waiting on a slow database is completely overloaded, at near-zero CPU. Every worker occupied, queue growing, nothing completing. This is exactly the v1-breaks scenario in stage 10, where service time went 80 ms → 400 ms: worker occupancy was 100% while the extra 320 ms per request was pure waiting. Dana is sitting at her desk staring at a spinner, and she is as unavailable as if she were flat out. A CPU-triggered shedder sits silently through the entire incident. Your microservices lab already showed you this shape from the caller's side: a slow dependency is far more dangerous than a dead one, and every dashboard stays green. (That last claim is reasoning rather than a run; stage 17 says exactly which part was observed.)

Most services you will write are IO-bound. A request that calls a database and two other services spends the overwhelming majority of its latency waiting. So this is the common case, not the exotic one.

Two further problems, which apply even to CPU-bound work. CPU is a lagging indicator: by the time a one-minute load average crosses a threshold, the queue has been growing for a minute. Queue delay responds in milliseconds, because it is the thing you care about. And CPU is not monotonic in what you want. GC, a noisy neighbour on the same host, or a co-located sidecar all move CPU without moving your capacity to serve.

What to use instead, in order of preference: queue delay first, then in-flight count against a limit, then queue depth. Queue delay is the time between accepting a request and starting work on it, and it is the single most valuable number to add to a service that has none. Reach for CPU when your work is genuinely CPU-bound and measured against a reservation, as Google's is. Otherwise treat it as a last-resort backstop with a high threshold, and know that it will not fire during the incident you are most likely to have.

F5 — where should Ben stand?

You have several places to put the doorman, and the choice is not free. Omar is on the street door of the building; Ben is at the door of Dana's room.

At the edge (load balancer, gateway) a rejection is cheapest. F1's ceiling is highest here, and you never spend backend capacity on a request you refuse. But Omar does not know which room is in trouble, or how expensive a particular application is. If one of your six services is sick, an edge shedding 30% of everything degrades the five that are fine and still lets through more than the sick one can take.

At the service the signal is accurate, because it is your own queue. But by the time the request arrives you have already spent the edge's accept, the TLS handshake, the routing decision, and a network hop. And a service that sheds cannot protect the dependency it is hammering.

In the client, per dependency, is the third option and the one people forget. This is your microservices lab's quartet: a bulkhead caps how much of your worker pool one dependency may occupy, and a circuit breaker stops calling a dependency that is failing. Those protect you. They do nothing for the dependency. A concurrency limit on the outbound side is the piece that protects what you are calling.

The rule that resolves it: shed as early as the decision can be made correctly, and no earlier. Concretely, that usually means all three, doing different jobs. A coarse global limit at the edge, sized to protect the edge. A per-service adaptive limit inside each service, which is where the accurate signal lives. And outbound limits per dependency. What you must avoid is two layers independently shedding the same traffic against uncoordinated thresholds, which multiplies your shed rate and is very hard to see on a dashboard.

F6 — what if the shedder eats the recovery path?

A load shedder that treats all requests identically will, under overload, shed the very requests that would have ended the incident.

It sheds health checks. So your load balancer concludes the instance is dead and removes it, moving its traffic to the remaining instances, which are now more overloaded, and shed harder, and are also removed. That is a cascading failure that the mitigation caused.

It sheds the configuration or control-plane call that would have let you turn a feature off. It sheds the deploy or rollback endpoint.

And it sheds the cache-fill request, so the cache stays cold, so more traffic reaches the origin, so the overload deepens. The one request that would have reduced future load is the one you rejected.

Every one of those is a request whose value is negative to shed. This is why Google's criticality scheme has a level above "critical": some traffic must never be shed even when everything else is.

What to do about it: health checks and control-plane endpoints go on a separate port, or a separate thread pool, or are explicitly exempt from admission control. That is a bulkhead, from your microservices lab, applied to the paths that are cheap and load-bearing. And your health check must report "am I able to serve?" rather than "am I alive?". A service shedding 90% of its traffic is alive and should say so, or the load balancer will make it worse.

F7 — is a shedder that ignores identity actually fair?

Load shedding is blind to identity, which was the point. It also means that when one tenant sends 80% of your traffic, they get 80% of your admissions, including during the overload they caused. Every other customer is degraded so that the one who broke it can continue at four-fifths strength.

This is the exact seam where load shedding needs rate limiting, and where your distributed-rate-limiting lab's material becomes load-bearing rather than adjacent.

Rate limiting enforces "you may have 1,000/s" — a promise, per identity, independent of your health. It is what stops one tenant from being 80% of the offered load in the first place. Load shedding enforces "I can only do 50/s right now" — a capability, across everyone, independent of identity.

Neither substitutes for the other. Rate limits that are loose enough to be generous will not prevent overload. A shedder tight enough to protect you will not protect tenant B from tenant A. Ship both. If you can only ship one thing this quarter, ship the shedder, because it is the one that prevents total loss, and know that you have not solved fairness.

There is a middle option, for when you need fairness under overload without a full quota system. Use per-tenant queues with weighted round-robin, or key the shed decision on (tenant, current share) so that the heaviest sender is shed first.

12. What are you actually guaranteed?

In one breath: Four promises, five things people assume are promises and are not, and five assumptions that each turn into one of stage 11's failures when they stop being true.

What load shedding guarantees, given the assumptions below:

  1. Bounded work in progress. The queue cannot exceed the limit, so memory is bounded and the OutOfMemoryError ending of stage 4 cannot happen.
  2. Bounded latency for admitted work. If the limit is derived from a deadline via Little's Law, admitted requests complete inside it. That is the whole content of inflight ≤ μ · D.
  3. The system stays in a recoverable state. F3's numbers: with shedding, full service returns 5 seconds after the cause ends; without it, never. Shedding is what makes the outage's length a function of the event's length.
  4. A fast, unambiguous answer to the client. A 503 in 1 ms is information Priya can act on. A timeout at 1,000 ms is not. It does not even tell her whether the work happened.

What it does not guarantee, and these are the ones that get promised anyway:

  1. It does not create capacity. Every table in this lab is bounded by capacity ÷ offered. Shedding gets you to that ceiling. Only more workers, faster work, or less work raise it. A shedder is not a substitute for capacity planning; it is what keeps you honest about the plan you have.
  2. It does not make the shed requests succeed. They failed. Fast, cheaply, and legibly — but they failed, and Priya did not get her passport.
  3. It does not choose the least valuable requests unless you told it what valuable means (v4). By default it sheds by arrival time, which correlates with nothing.
  4. It does not protect your dependencies. Admitting 50/s means calling your database 50 times a second. If the database is what is overloaded, an inbound limit does not help it. You need an outbound one.
  5. It is not fair (F7).

Every one of those guarantees rests on an assumption, and each assumption is a failure mode when it is violated. Read the middle column as "the morning where this stops being true".

assumption violated by consequence
rejection is much cheaper than service expensive 503 path F1 — you can only reject
clients respect the rejection retry-on-503 F2 — your output becomes your input
the signal responds faster than the overload grows CPU averages, 1-min metrics F4 — you shed after it matters
service time is roughly stable, or the signal is time-based a slow dependency v1 breaks — 154× worse
some traffic is exempt shedding health checks F6 — the mitigation cascades

Safety and liveness, stated properly. The safety property is "work in progress never exceeds the limit". It holds at every instant, under every load, and is trivially checkable. The liveness property is "every admitted request eventually completes". Note that LIFO violates it: under permanent overload, the person at the bottom of the stack is never called. That is a real cost, not a rounding error, and it is why LIFO is an overload mode rather than a default.

The two ways of failing two-thirds of your customers are not equivalent, even when the success rate is identical:

at 3× overload success how the other 67% failed
LIFO, no admission control 33.4% waited the full second, then got nothing
queue cap 20, FIFO 33.3% told "no" in about a millisecond

Identical goodput. Completely different product. The second one lets Priya fall back to a cached page, try another region, or see an honest error immediately. The first burns a second of her life and a second of the client's connection before delivering the same nothing. Failing fast is a feature you are shipping to your callers, not just a saving for yourself.

Say it back. Two services both fail 67% of their customers. One says "no" in a millisecond; the other says nothing for a full second. Without using the words safety, liveness or bounded, explain why the first is the better product.

A good answer sounds like

"Both of them serve the same people, so the difference is not who wins. It is what everyone else gets to do next. A fast no is an answer: the app can show a cached page, try the other region, or say sorry while the customer is still looking at the screen. A slow nothing is not an answer, and it costs the customer a second of their life and us a second of a connection we could have used. And nobody can even tell whether their order went through."

What do the four production designs actually do?

These are the real implementations, with what is known about each one.

Facebook builds Ben out of a stopwatch: adaptive LIFO plus CoDel. Published in "Fail at Scale" (ACM Queue, 2015). Requests are served FIFO while the queue is short and LIFO once a queue starts to form. CoDel drops requests that have waited beyond a tolerance which collapses when the queue stops draining. The published constants are M = 5 ms (target) and N = 100 ms (interval), described as working well across a wide set of use cases. Implementations exist in HHVM and in Facebook's Wangle library, used by Thrift. This is the design stage 10's v2b models, and stage 10's table shows why the 5 ms constant needs scaling to your own service time.

Envoy builds Ben out of a ratio: the adaptive concurrency filter. It is a gradient controller, and its formula is very close to what stage 10 built:

gradient  = (minRTT + B) / sampleRTT        B = minRTT × buffer_pct
limit_new = gradient × limit_old + headroom  headroom = sqrt(limit), not configurable

Documented defaults: sample latency taken at the 90th percentile, sampling window 100 ms, minRTT recalculated every 60 seconds with 10% jitter, using 50 requests.

The detail worth stealing is how Envoy solves a problem stage 10 hit head-on. This lab found that a minimum-latency baseline is unreliable, because under sustained load you never observe an uncontended request. Envoy's answer is to periodically pin the concurrency limit down to min_concurrency in order to force an uncontended measurement. It deliberately creates the idle conditions needed to measure minRTT, then goes back to normal. In office terms: Ben empties the waiting room for a few seconds so he can time how long one application takes when Dana is not rushed. The documentation is candid that this causes a noticeable increase in 503s during those windows, and recommends enabling retries because of it. That is a real, honest trade: the controller pays a small periodic outage to keep its baseline true.

Envoy also ships a separate admission control filter, which sheds based on observed success rate rather than latency.

Netflix ships Ben as a library: concurrency-limits. A Java library of pluggable limit algorithms, gradient and AIMD variants, with interceptors for gRPC and servlets. This is where stage 10's v3 comes from, including the necessity of a multiplicative decrease on an actual drop alongside the gradient's gentle adjustment. This lab found that last part the hard way.

Google gives Ben a guest list and teaches the callers to stay home: criticality plus client-side adaptive throttling. Chapter 21 of the SRE book. Two pieces.

Criticality is carried in the RPC and comes in exactly four levels: CRITICAL_PLUS, CRITICAL, SHEDDABLE_PLUS, SHEDDABLE. It propagates through the call tree, so a request marked sheddable at the edge is sheddable everywhere it goes. That is what stage 10's v4 models, and the propagation is the part that is easy to leave out and expensive to omit.

Client-side adaptive throttling is the piece that fixes F2 properly, and it is the most under-used idea in this whole subject: the client throttles itself. Dev's app stops calling before Ben has to say no. Each client tracks requests (attempted at the application layer) and accepts (accepted by the backend) over a recent window. Once requests exceeds K × accepts it starts rejecting its own outbound calls locally, with probability

max(0, (requests − K × accepts) / (requests + 1))

The book recommends K = 2 as the default, and notes that reducing K to 1.1 means only one request is rejected by the backend for every ten it accepts. Tighter protection for the server, at the cost of the client giving up sooner.

Why this matters: it moves the cost of rejection off the overloaded server entirely, which is the only real answer to F1. A server that is shedding has a ceiling on how fast it can shed. A client that has stopped calling costs it nothing at all.

Deadline propagation — why is it the highest-value thing to add?

Stage 8's third analogy break was that your server cannot see the client's deadline. Ben can see the clock on the wall and knows about the 11:00 train. Your service does not. Fix that, and several things become possible at once.

The mechanism: the caller sends the time remaining with the request. gRPC does this natively as the grpc-timeout header, and every service in the chain subtracts the time it has already spent before calling the next one.

What it buys you:

  • Drop-on-dequeue becomes correct rather than a guess. Stage 10 showed that dropping against deadline − expected service time is worth 22.5% against 3.8% for dropping against the deadline, and 32.1% with a healthy slack. With a propagated deadline you know the real number instead of estimating it.
  • You stop doing work nobody is waiting for, across the whole call tree at once, not just at the entry point.
  • Timeouts stop being independently guessed at every hop. The classic bug this removes: service A times out at 1 s and calls B, which has its own 3 s timeout and keeps working for two seconds after A gave up.

What do you configure, and in what order?

  1. Bound every queue you own. Start with the accidental ones — an unbounded LinkedBlockingQueue, spring.task.execution.pool.queue-capacity at Integer.MAX_VALUE, a connection-pool acquire timeout of 30 s (which, as your queueing-theory lab put it, is an unbounded queue with a 30× multiplier). Stage 10's plateau says the exact number barely matters as long as it is small; being off by 10× does.
  2. Add queue delay as a metric before you add any controller. You cannot tune what you cannot see, and this one number would have caught every failure in stage 11.
  3. Ship a static in-flight limit derived from Little's Law and then divided by two or three. Stage 10 put the undivided value at 18.7% against a 33.3% ceiling.
  4. Make the rejection cheap and make it honest. Put a stopwatch on your 503 path. Return Retry-After and mean it. Do not log shed requests at INFO.
  5. Stop retrying rejections. Retry budget, not attempt counts; never retry a shedder's 503.
  6. Then consider criticality classes, remembering they must ride on an adaptive limit or they become a permanent tax on your low-priority traffic: 79.0% against 95.3% at healthy load.
  7. Then consider an adaptive controller, knowing what stage 10 found about how easy they are to get subtly wrong.

14. Which comfortable sentences are wrong?

In one breath: Eight sentences you will hear in an incident review. Each one sounds like caution, and each one is contradicted by a number you now have.

"Load shedding means we lose requests, so it's a last resort."

Backwards. Under overload the requests are lost either way. Stage 6 put it at 0.1% served without it and 33.3% with it, at the same offered load on the same hardware. Shedding does not cause the loss. It converts undirected loss into directed loss, and directed loss is 500× better. The requests you shed were going to fail; the ones you saved were not.

"It's the same as rate limiting."

Rate limiting asks are you allowed? — a question about identity and a contract, answered identically whether you are idle or on fire. Shedding asks can I? — a question about your own state right now, answered identically for every client. A rate limiter set tight enough to prevent overload rejects your legitimate traffic every day. A shedder does not protect tenant B from tenant A. You need both, they fire at different times, and neither is a configuration of the other.

"We have a bounded queue, so we're protected."

Stage 10 swept caps of 400, 200 and 100, and all three delivered the same 0.1% as no cap at all. The bound must be small enough that a request joining at the back can still finish in time, which is a number in the range 8–30 for that service, not 100. Bounded and correctly bounded are different claims, and only one of them protects you.

"We'll set the limit once and it'll be right."

It was right for the service time you had when you chose it. Stage 10's central result: the same cap of 20 that recovered 500× at 80 ms per request recovered nothing at all when a dependency pushed service time to 400 ms. That is 0.1% against a 16.7% ceiling, with no config change, no alert, and no traffic increase. Any limit in units of work silently encodes an assumption about how long work takes.

"Throughput looks fine, so we're fine."

This is Meera's sentence from stage 1. Throughput read 50.1/s during a total outage and 50.0/s during optimal operation. Median latency read better during the outage than under load, because it is computed over survivors. Both of your default dashboards are blind to the thing that is happening.

"Returning a 503 is free."

Stage 11's F1: at 2 ms per rejection, a four-worker service becomes completely unable to serve anyone at around 1,800 requests/s offered, purely from the cost of refusing them. Your capacity to shed is a finite capacity. It has a computable ceiling, workers ÷ cost per rejection, and past it the shedder is the outage.

"Retries make the system more reliable."

They make an individual request more likely to succeed when failures are independent. Under overload failures are not independent. They are caused by load, and retries are load. A service at 45/s against a capacity of 50/s is comfortably under capacity. It still dropped to a 24.2% success rate with a 38,663-deep queue, purely because its clients retried three times. And a single 3,000-request burst into a healthy system was absorbed in four minutes without retries, and had grown to a 52,206-deep queue thirteen minutes later with them.

"Exponential backoff with jitter fixes retry storms."

It fixes synchronisation, which is a real and different problem. Under sustained overload, 200 ms backoff, 1 s backoff, and 1 s backoff with jitter produced 394, 393 and 395 attempts per second. Indistinguishable. The thing that worked was not retrying the rejection at all, which took the door back to 150/s. A shedder's 503 is the one error that guarantees both that the work did not happen and that retrying cannot help.

"Autoscaling means I don't need this."

Autoscaling responds in minutes. Stage 11's F3 showed a system going from healthy to unrecoverable in about sixty seconds. Shedding is what covers the gap. And worse, an autoscaler reading the wrong signal will scale down a shedding service, because its CPU looks fine and its latency looks great.

16. What does each word mean?

Grouped by what the word is about. Each entry names the stage where you met the thing, so a definition you have forgotten is always two clicks from the story that earned it.

What are the words for the thing you are trying to see?

Throughput — work completed per second. A fact about your machine. Read 50/s during both total success and total failure in stage 1.

Goodput — work completed per second that was still wanted when it finished. A fact about your business, and the only metric in this lab that could tell a healthy service from a dead one.

Offered load — requests arriving per second, as opposed to requests served. Invisible on server-side graphs unless you count rejections.

Capacity (μ) — completions per second the system can sustain. workers ÷ service time. Not a constant: it fell 5× in stage 10 when a dependency slowed.

Overload — offered load exceeding capacity. Defined against completion rate, not CPU.

Shed rate — fraction of arrivals rejected. Meaningless without a criticality breakdown.

Wasted work — worker time spent on requests whose client had already given up. 100% of it in stage 4, which is Dana's whole morning.

Queue depth — number of requests waiting. A count, and therefore carrying a hidden assumption about service time.

Queue delay (sojourn time) — how long a request waited between being accepted and being started. The best single signal, because it is denominated in the units the deadline is denominated in.

What are the words for the machinery at the door?

Admission control — the component deciding, at the entrance, whether a request joins the queue. Load shedding is what it is called when it says no because you are overloaded. Ben, in stage 6.

Signal / set point / actuator — the three parts of the control loop that every shedder is. Designs differ almost entirely in the choice of signal. Stage 8.

In-flight / concurrency limit — a cap on requests inside the service, queued plus being worked on. Little's Law's natural variable: inflight ≤ μ × deadline.

Little's Law — L = λW. Here it converts a deadline into a concurrency limit. An identity, not a model.

AIMD — additive increase, multiplicative decrease. TCP's shape, applied to a concurrency limit. Stage 10's v3.

Gradient controller — an adaptive limit driven by the ratio of recent latency to the service's own baseline latency, so that it carries no assumption about how fast the work is. Envoy: limit = gradient × limit + sqrt(limit).

minRTT — the uncontended latency baseline a gradient controller compares against. Envoy re-measures it every 60 s by deliberately pinning concurrency to its minimum.

Criticality — a priority class carried in the request and propagated through the call tree. Google's four levels: CRITICAL_PLUS, CRITICAL, SHEDDABLE_PLUS, SHEDDABLE. Arjun's guest list, in stage 10's v4.

Client-side adaptive throttling — the client rejecting its own calls once requests > K × accepts, with probability max(0, (requests − K×accepts) / (requests + 1)). Google's default K = 2. Moves the cost of rejection off the overloaded server.

What are the words for what happens after the door?

Deadline — how long an answer is still worth having. Usually the client's timeout; usually known to the client and not the server. Priya's 11:00 train.

CoDel (Controlled Delay) — a queue rule that tolerates delay while the queue keeps draining and cuts hard once it stops. Facebook's published constants: target 5 ms, interval 100 ms.

Standing queue — a queue that never drains to empty. The distinction CoDel is built on: a burst is fine, a backlog is not.

Adaptive LIFO — FIFO while the queue is short, LIFO once it forms. Facebook's pairing with CoDel; on its own it took goodput from 0.1% to 33.4% in stage 10.

Drop-on-dequeue — discarding a request when it reaches the front if it can no longer finish in time. Must be evaluated against deadline − expected service time, which is worth 22.5% versus 3.8% for the naive version.

Deadline propagation — sending the remaining time budget with the request, so every hop can decide whether the work is still worth doing. gRPC's grpc-timeout. The thing that would let your server see what Ben can see.

What are the words for what goes wrong, and for the neighbours?

Metastable failure — a system with two stable states, knocked into the bad one by a transient, which then sustains itself after the trigger is gone. Stage 11's F3: a burst absorbed in four minutes without retries, permanent with them.

Retry budget — a global cap on retries as a fraction of a client's traffic, as opposed to a per-request attempt count. The only retry control in this lab that reduced offered load.

Backpressure — refusing work and letting the pressure travel back to the producer, discarding nothing. Shedding is the answer when there is no producer to push back on.

Bulkhead — a reserved share of a resource, so one dependency or one class cannot consume all of it. What keeps health checks from being shed.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Because the resource being fought over is time, and time spent on a request that nobody is waiting for any more is gone. When everything is let in, every request gets a share of a queue too long for any of them to finish inside their deadline — so the work is done and thrown away. Turning most of them away concentrates the same amount of work on a small enough group that the group actually finishes in time. Measured: 0.1% served without it, 33.3% with it, identical hardware and identical offered load.

1.2 "A rate limiter enforces a promise about who you are; a shedder enforces a fact about what I can currently do." The rate limiter's answer does not change when the service is on fire, and the shedder's answer does not change when the caller is important.

A rate limiter running correctly and not helping: ten thousand mobile clients, each with a limit of 60 requests/minute, each sending 4. Every client is well inside its limit; the aggregate is 40,000/minute and it buries you. Overload is usually an aggregate phenomenon and rate limits are per-identity.

1.3 Throughput counts requests the server finished. Under overload the server finishes them at full speed — it just finishes them long after the client hung up, so they count as work done and nothing delivered. The number to watch is goodput: completions that arrived before their client gave up. Equivalently, successes divided by requests offered, which requires counting rejections and timeouts, not just responses.

1.4 Because it assumes the loss is caused by shedding. At 150/s against a 50/s capacity, 100 requests/second are lost no matter what anybody does — the only question is whether they are lost cheaply and immediately or after consuming a worker, a connection and a second of the user's life. Shedding does not add loss; it directs it.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 About 0.7%. Not 83%. The queue grows by 10 requests/second forever; within about a minute everyone joining is already certain to time out, and the server keeps doing their work anyway. Wasted work reaches 99%.

3.2 0.1% — exactly the same as no cap at all. A queue of 100 at 80 ms across 4 workers is 2,000 ms of waiting, twice the client deadline. Bounding a queue at a number that merely sounds small does nothing. Measured: caps of 400, 200 and 100 all return 0.1%; the cap has to reach about 30 before anything changes.

3.3 (a) Goodput does not move — 50/s in every case. (b) Nor do the admission decisions: 394/s at 200 ms, 393/s at 1 s, 395/s at 1 s with jitter.

This is the one most people get wrong. Backoff and jitter fix synchronisation — clients retrying in lockstep at the same instant. Under sustained overload there is no herd to spread out: every client will exhaust its attempts whenever you schedule them. Jitter is a fix for correlation, not for volume. The thing that worked was not retrying the rejection: back to 150/s.

3.4 Enormously, and no. Twenty runs of the identical configuration produced success rates from 1.1% to 70.6%. And running longer makes it worse, not more reliable: mean 28.6% over ten minutes, 11.7% over an hour. At exactly ρ = 1 the queue is a random walk with nothing pulling it back down, so there is no steady state to converge on. "We run at 100% utilisation" is not an operating point.

3.5 Success rate 0.1% → 33.4%, the theoretical ceiling. Median latency 532 ms → 66 ms. Wasted work 100% → 0%. And queue depth does not improve at all — still 60,047 and still growing. LIFO fixes who you serve and does nothing about memory, which is exactly why the production design pairs it with a drop rule.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Capacity 4 workers ÷ 0.4 s = 10/s, down from 50/s — a 5× fall with no traffic change. The correct cap scales with the same factor: about 4, from c·(D/S − 1) = 4 × (1000/400 − 1) = 6, then divided down for the same reason the original 46 became 20. Your configured cap of 20 is roughly 5× too large — and it therefore delivers 0.1% against a 16.7% ceiling, the same as having no shedder.

4.2 Capacity doubles to 100/s.

  • (a) queue cap 20 and (b) in-flight limit 12 — both still protect goodput perfectly (measured 33.2% and 33.4% against a 33.3% ceiling at 3× the new capacity), but they now correspond to half the queueing delay they used to, so if you sized them for a latency target you should double them. They are counts, and counts are per-worker quantities in disguise.
  • (c) CoDel target 50 ms — unchanged. It is denominated in time, and the time you are willing to wait did not change because you bought more machines. This is the whole argument for time-based signals in one line.
  • (d) the client's 1-second timeout — unchanged. It is a property of the caller's patience, not of your capacity.

4.3 The ceiling is workers ÷ cost per rejection. At 2 ms: 4 ÷ 0.002 = 2,000 rejections/s. At 0.4 ms: 4 ÷ 0.0004 = 10,000/s — a 5× improvement. Measured: goodput survives to 5,000/s offered (25.0/s) and reaches zero between 9,000 and 10,000/s, exactly as predicted.

4.4 allow = 0.25 of a fixed limit of 12 is a hard limit of 3 concurrent recommendation requests, enforced at every moment, including when the workers are 69% busy and there is capacity for everything. The fraction inherited the fixed limit's flaw: it encodes "a quarter of capacity" as a constant, and a constant cannot know that the service is currently fine.

  • Changing a number: raise the fractions until they only bind under real overload. This works and is fragile — the right value depends on capacity, which changes.
  • Changing the design: apply the fractions to an adaptive limit, so when the service is fast the limit is large and a quarter of it is plenty. Measured: 79.0% → 95.3% at healthy load, with identical protection under overload (checkout 96% vs 97% at 3×). This is the right answer.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 CPU cannot see the most common overload. Smallest failing scenario: your database slows from 80 ms to 400 ms. All four workers are occupied waiting on network I/O, the queue grows without bound, goodput goes to zero — and CPU is near idle the whole time, because waiting is not computing. The shedder never fires. Broken guarantee: the signal must respond to inability to finish work, and CPU measures something else. (Note the honest exception: Google's SRE book does use CPU-against-reservation as its primary signal, for CPU-bound RPC servers with controlled thread pools. Copying the signal without that context is the error.)

6.2 A shedder that is off by default has never been tested. Smallest failing scenario: the first time it is switched on, in an incident, the threshold is in the wrong units and the value chosen under pressure is 100 — which the lab measured as worth 0.1%, identical to nothing. The deeper flaw is that everything around it is untested too: the 503 path allocates, the request gets logged at INFO with its body, the client retries it immediately, and /healthz is shed. None of that is visible in code review. Correct model: shedding runs always, waving everyone through on a normal day, so that its calibration is a steady-state property rather than a decision made at 3 a.m.

6.3 You have built a synchroniser. Smallest failing scenario: an overload begins; in one 50 ms window you reject 4,000 clients; all 4,000 are told exactly 1 second; all 4,000 return inside the same 50 ms window one second later. You have converted smooth overload into a periodic thundering herd of your own manufacture, timed by your own header. Fixes: jitter the value server-side, and make it honest — if you say 1 second and are still overloaded in 1 second, the next wave is worse. This is your distributed-rate-limiting lab's warning about Retry-After arriving in a new place.

6.4 Uniform edge shedding degrades the healthy and does not save the sick. Smallest failing scenario: six services behind the edge, one of them overloaded. Shedding 30% of everything cuts the five healthy services' traffic for no reason, while the sick one still receives 70% of a load it cannot take — so it is still down, and now five other things are worse. The edge's signal is aggregate; the overload is local. Broken guarantee: the decision must be made where the accurate signal is.

6.5 The Little's Law number is a ceiling, not a setting — the same error as 5.1(d), in the other units. 50 in flight is the concurrency at which the average request finishes exactly at the deadline, so half do not. Measured: an in-flight limit of 50 returns 18.7% against a 33.3% ceiling, while 12 returns the full 33.3%.

6.6 You have made the mitigation into the outage. Smallest failing scenario: one instance becomes overloaded and sheds; its /healthz is shed too; the load balancer marks it dead and moves its traffic to the remaining instances; they become more overloaded and shed harder; their health checks are shed; the whole pool is removed. Health checks and control-plane endpoints must be exempt — on a separate port, pool, or criticality class — and a health check should report "can I serve?" rather than "am I alive?".

6.7 True about goodput, false about memory, and false about the client. Two smallest failing scenarios:

  • Memory: at 3× overload, LIFO reached 33.4% with a queue of 60,047 and growing. The service dies of OutOfMemoryError, restarts cold, and meets the same traffic with an empty cache.
  • The client: LIFO and a queue cap deliver the same 33% goodput, but they fail the other 67% completely differently — under LIFO they wait a full second and get nothing; under a cap they are told "no" in about a millisecond and can fall back, degrade, or try another region. Failing fast is a feature you ship to your callers.

Also worth naming: LIFO violates liveness. Under permanent overload the request at the bottom of the stack is never served at all. It is an overload mode, not a default.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 You have hit the rejection ceiling (F1): offered load has risen to the point where the cost of saying "no" consumes the machine. workers ÷ cost per rejection is your ceiling — 4 ÷ 2 ms = 2,000/s in the lab's service.

Confirming it from metrics: CPU or worker occupancy at 100% while goodput falls toward zero and the rejection rate has plateaued rather than continuing to track offered load. That plateau is the signature — you are refusing as fast as you physically can. Compare rejection rate against workers ÷ measured 503 cost.

Options in five minutes, in order of leverage:

  1. Move the rejection upstream — shed at the load balancer or CDN, where a refusal is far cheaper, or drop before TLS termination.
  2. Make the rejection cheaper — turn off access logging for shed requests, drop the response body, stop emitting a per-request metric with a high cardinality label. Halving the cost doubles the ceiling.
  3. Client-side adaptive throttling (Google's max(0, (requests − K·accepts) / (requests + 1)), K = 2) — the only option that removes the cost from your machine entirely, because a client that has stopped calling costs you nothing. Not deployable in five minutes, which is exactly why it should already be there.
  4. If the traffic is abusive rather than organic, drop at a layer that answers with a TCP reset rather than an HTTP response.

7.2 The mechanism. The burst pushed the queue past the point where anything in it can finish in time. Every request now times out; every timeout produces a retry; each retry is a new arrival. The retries alone are now enough to keep offered load above capacity, so the system sustains its own overload with no external cause. This is a metastable failure — two stable states, and you are in the bad one. Measured: an identical burst without retries was absorbed in about four minutes; with retries the queue was at 52,206 and growing thirteen minutes later.

Waiting does not work because the input keeping you down is generated by the state you are in. There is no decay term.

Minimum intervention: break the loop by refusing work cheaply — turn on admission control, or if you have none, drop traffic at the load balancer for long enough for the queue to drain. That is precisely why "turn it off and on again" works on this failure when nothing else does: it is the only tool most teams have for emptying a queue. Measured: with a queue cap of 20 in place, the same 3,000-request burst never takes the queue above 1.

7.3 What A and B see: C returns fast 503s. B sees a sharp rise in errors and falling latency (rejections are quick). A sees whatever B chooses to do — and if B retries, A sees B get slower while C gets three times the load.

mechanism lives in protects
timeout A→B and B→C the caller — bounds how long its worker is held
retry (budgeted, not on 503) A and B the request, when failures are independent
circuit breaker B, keyed on (C, endpoint) the caller B — stops holding workers on a dead dependency
bulkhead B B's other endpoints — caps how much of B's pool C can occupy
inbound shedder C C
outbound concurrency limit B, per dependency C

Only the last two protect C. Everything else is B looking after itself. That asymmetry is the point of the exercise: four of the five patterns most teams ship protect the caller, and a service that is being crushed is not helped by any of them.

7.4 Three hypotheses, all consistent with what the lab measured while building these controllers:

  1. An absolute latency threshold that is now always exceeded. If the controller calls anything over 400 ms "congested" and production service time is 400 ms — because production calls a real database and staging did not — then every completion is a congestion signal and the limit grinds to the floor. Distinguishing measurement: compare the threshold against the p50 service time in production. If p50 is anywhere near it, this is your answer. Measured analogue: 10.1% against a 16.7% ceiling at 66% worker utilisation — shedding work it had the capacity to serve.
  2. A bad latency baseline. A controller comparing against a minimum observed RTT never observes an uncontended request under sustained load, so the ratio pins at its floor and the limit sticks. Distinguishing measurement: graph the baseline the controller is using; if it never moves, or sits implausibly low, it is not being re-measured. Fix: Envoy's approach — periodically pin concurrency to its minimum to force an honest measurement.
  3. A drop signal that never stops. Under genuinely permanent overload every AIMD-shaped controller receives "congested" forever, and the multiplicative decrease beats the additive increase indefinitely. The controller is not broken; it cannot distinguish "let more in" from "still drowning". Distinguishing measurement: is offered load actually above capacity? If yes, the limit is correct and the problem is that you needed a fixed floor under it.

The third is the uncomfortable one, because the fix is to admit that an adaptive controller needs a sanity floor — and a floor is a constant, which is what you adopted the controller to avoid.

7.5 What is true: nothing is broken, the shedder is working, and the customer's burst genuinely exceeds what you can absorb.

What you do:

  1. Check which class was shed and whether the shedding was correct. If their batch is SHEDDABLE and your checkout traffic survived, the system did its job and you should be able to show that.
  2. Give them a shape they can use. A burst of 20,000 is a scheduling problem, not a capacity problem. Offer an async/batch endpoint, a documented requests-per-second ceiling with an honest Retry-After, or a queue they can submit to. The best outcome converts a burst into a stream.
  3. Add jittered scheduling advice — if fifty customers all fire at 09:00:00, the aggregate is the problem, not any one of them.
  4. Price it. If they need guaranteed burst capacity, that is a capacity commitment, which is a commercial conversation with a reserved-quota answer.

The argument against just raising the limit: the limit is not a preference, it is a measurement of what you can finish. Raising it does not create capacity — it converts fast, honest rejections into slow timeouts, and the measured cost of that conversion is everyone else's traffic. You would take one customer's complaint about being refused and turn it into every customer's outage. If they genuinely need the capacity, buy the capacity; do not fake it by disabling the thing that tells you the truth.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 — A payments API

30,000/min = 500/s, of which ~20/s are authorisations and ~480/s are polls.

The central observation: the two request types differ in service time by nearly two orders of magnitude (1,200 ms vs 15 ms). A single limit counting requests is therefore meaningless — 100 in-flight polls is 1.5 seconds of work, 100 in-flight authorisations is two minutes of it. Separate pools, separate limits, and no shared thread pool. This is a bulkhead, and it is not optional.

Where to shed: at the edge for gross protection and cheap rejections; a per-pool adaptive limit inside the service, which is where the accurate signal lives; and — most importantly — an outbound concurrency limit on the card network, since it is the real constrained resource and an inbound limit does nothing for it.

Signal: queue delay per pool. For authorisations the relative signal matters because the card network's latency legitimately varies; an absolute threshold would shed correct traffic on a slow day.

Priority: authorisations CRITICAL_PLUS — they are the product and they carry money. Polls SHEDDABLE. Note the asymmetry: polls are 96% of traffic and approximately 0% of the value.

What to tell the polls: 503 (or 429) with an honest jittered Retry-After, plus a Cache-Control so the dashboard shows a slightly stale status rather than an error. Better still, move them off polling entirely — webhooks or SSE remove the load rather than shedding it, and 480/s of polling for a status that changes once is the actual design flaw.

Monitor: authorisation goodput and shed rate (alert on any non-zero CRITICAL_PLUS shedding), outbound in-flight against the card network, queue delay per pool, and the card network's own latency as an input rather than a symptom.

If the card network's latency doubles to 2.4 s: authorisation capacity halves. The outbound limit must fall — an adaptive one does this by itself, a fixed one does not. The 3-second SLA now has almost no headroom, so deadline propagation matters more than any limit: a request with 600 ms of budget left should never be started. And because polls are on a separate pool, they are unaffected — which is the entire return on the bulkhead.

8.2 — Choosing one

The defensible answer is (b) adaptive LIFO + CoDel, and the case is:

  • It self-tunes to service time, which is the failure that actually happens. Measured through a 5× dependency slowdown: static cap 0.1%, hand-tuned static limit 11.2%, adaptive LIFO + CoDel 15.4% against a 16.7% ceiling — better than the setting chosen by someone who knew the answer in advance.
  • It is far simpler than an adaptive concurrency controller, and this lab measured three separate ways to get one of those subtly wrong, all of which look correct in code review.
  • It has a published production pedigree (Facebook, Wangle, HHVM) and one parameter with a clear physical meaning.

When it would be the wrong choice: when your service time is genuinely stable and your team has no appetite for a queue-order change — then (a) is 90% of the benefit for a tenth of the thought, and shipping (a) this week beats shipping (b) next quarter. Also when your work is CPU-bound and uniform, where a fixed limit is both correct and provable. And note the measured cost of (b) that (a) does not have: at healthy load CoDel with a badly-scaled target shed 10% of traffic that did not need shedding. It is not free, and (a) is.

8.3 — The dashboard

Metrics (four):

  1. Goodput / success rate over offered — detects F8, the outage invisible to throughput. This is the one that would have caught it.
  2. Queue delay, p50 and p99 — detects the v1 failure (service time moving) and F4 (CPU not firing), and it responds in milliseconds.
  3. Shed rate split by criticality — distinguishes "working as designed" from "out of promised capacity", which no aggregate can.
  4. Rejection rate against workers ÷ 503 cost — detects F1, the rejection ceiling.

Alerts (two):

  1. Any sustained shedding of the top criticality class. Shedding SHEDDABLE is a healthy system; shedding CRITICAL means you have run out of the capacity you promised.
  2. Queue delay p99 above the deadline budget for 60 seconds. Fires before goodput collapses rather than after.

Why a p99 latency alert would not have fired: p99 latency is computed over requests that received a response. During the outage the only requests receiving responses in time were the lucky few that arrived when the queue was briefly short — so the p99 of that population was better than normal. Once shedding is added it improves further, because a 503 is fast. Every latency metric you own is conditioned on survival, and the thing you are trying to detect is deaths.

8.4 — Perfect deadline propagation

Becomes unnecessary:

  • Guessing the drop threshold. The lab's measured awkwardness — that dropping at the deadline is worth 3.8% while dropping 250 ms before it is worth 32.1% — exists only because the server is estimating. With a real budget you compare remaining < expected service time and the estimate disappears.
  • Deriving a queue cap from an assumed deadline. Every request carries its own.

Becomes simpler:

  • The signal. "Would this request make its deadline?" is answerable per request rather than inferred from aggregate queue delay. Admission control becomes almost trivial: admit if remaining > predicted wait + service time.
  • The whole call tree. Every hop can drop work nobody is waiting for, instead of only the entry point knowing.
  • Priority. Deadline and criticality are different axes, but earliest-deadline ordering is a well-understood scheduling discipline and becomes available.

Still required:

  • Admission control — yes, still, and this is the crux of the question. A deadline tells you whether this request can be served; it does not bound how many requests are inside your process. Ten thousand requests each with a generous 30-second budget are all individually servable and will still exhaust your memory. Deadlines solve the wasted-work problem, not the resource-bound problem — which is why the safety property in stage 12 is stated about work in progress and not about time.
  • Cheap rejections (F1 is unaffected — you still have to say no, and saying no still costs).
  • Retry control (F2 is unaffected; a deadline does not stop a client asking again).
  • Priority — deadlines rank by urgency, and urgency is not value. A recommendation carousel with 50 ms left is more urgent and less important than a checkout with 2 seconds left.

8.5 — Multi-tenant fairness

The mechanism to add: make the shed decision a function of (tenant, that tenant's current share of admissions) rather than of arrival order alone. Two practical shapes:

  1. Weighted fair queueing — a queue per tenant, served round-robin by weight. Strong isolation, and the cost is real: per-tenant queues fragment your capacity, and your queueing-theory lab measured what that costs — four separate lines at 80% busy wait 5.2× longer than one shared line. You are buying fairness with latency.
  2. Shed the heaviest sender first — when overloaded, reject in descending order of each tenant's share of recent admissions. Much cheaper, no fragmentation, and only approximately fair, which is usually enough.

Prefer (2) unless a contract requires (1).

What it costs when nothing is wrong: option 2 costs essentially nothing — it only ranks when the shedder is already firing. Option 1 costs latency all the time (the pooling result above), which is why "shard the queue per tenant for isolation" is so often a latency regression dressed as a feature.

The failure mode of the fix: tenant identity is an input you do not always have or cannot trust. Unauthenticated traffic, a shared gateway that collapses many tenants into one identity, a single tenant behind many API keys, or an attacker rotating credentials — all of these defeat share-based shedding, and it degrades to the blind version exactly when you need it most. So it is a fairness improvement layered on top of an identity-blind shedder, never a replacement for one.

Also worth saying plainly: this exercise is where load shedding hands off to rate limiting. A shedder made fair is an approximation of a quota system built under duress. If the noisy-neighbour problem is chronic rather than occasional, the real fix is per-tenant limits negotiated in advance, and the shedder goes back to doing the thing only it can do — surviving.
