Lab 68 · Whole Systems

MongoDB — one document, and the line that decides what is real

Tell a friend: Asha orders a thali on a Tuesday night and gets an e-mail saying her order is placed, but Omar's kitchen never sees it and the order is gone for good. Nobody crashed and nothing was misconfigured. The copy of the order book that took her order was simply cut off from the other two for a few seconds. When it came back, the rule said it had to throw away everything the others had never seen. MongoDB keeps each order as one document, and the document is the unit of everything — atomicity, storage, replication and distribution. Every safety knob it offers asks one question: where should this write or read stand, relative to the line that a majority of the copies have agreed on? That line is the majority commit point, and it is the line between an opinion and a fact.

The trainer for this lab is visualization.html, a step-by-step replica-set simulator also published as The Order Book. What the lab is made of, where every number came from, and where it sits among your other labs are in section 17 at the end.

1. Why does MongoDB exist at all?

In one breath: Two problems live inside one database. The first is that your data does not look like rows. The second is that the machine holding it will one day die.

Thali is a food-delivery marketplace in Pune. Asha orders a thali from Omar's kitchen. Priya's team runs the service that stores that order.

Hold two problems in your head. They look unrelated. MongoDB answers both with the same idea, and that is the whole design.

Why is one order seven rows across four tables?

An order in your application is one thing. A customer, an address, four line items, a payment reference, a delivery status.

In a relational database it is seven rows across four tables. Every read reassembles it with joins. Every write is a transaction across those tables. Every schema change is a migration that touches all of them.

That mismatch has a name. Engineers call it the object–relational impedance mismatch, and for thirty years the answer was an ORM layer that hid it.

MongoDB's answer is to stop hiding it. Store the order as the one thing it already is: a document, a nested JSON-like value with fields, arrays and sub-documents, kept together on disk and moved together over the network.

What does the second problem, failure, ask of you?

A database that runs on one machine has one machine's availability. The moment you want to survive that machine dying, you need copies.

Copies raise every question the last two weeks of labs answered. Which copy is allowed to accept writes? What does "written" mean when two of three copies have it? What should a client be told, and when? What happens to a copy that accepted writes nobody else saw?

A database that also wants to grow past one machine's disk has to cut the data up. That raises the other set of questions. Which pieces live together? How does a query find them? What happens while a piece is moving?

What single idea answers both problems?

The document is the unit. Say it once and the rest follows.

It is the unit of atomicity: one document changes all-or-nothing, always, with no transaction needed. It is the unit of storage locality: one document is one contiguous value in the storage engine. It is the unit of replication: one write becomes one entry in the replication log. It is the unit of distribution: a shard key inside the document decides where the whole document lives.

Almost every strength and every limit in this lab follows from that one choice. Embed what you read together and you get atomicity and locality for free. Cross the document boundary and you pay for it with a transaction. Grow a document without bound and you hit a wall at 16 MB.

The second half of the design is about copies, and it is where your prior labs cash in. A replica set is a raft-derived group with one primary and a replicated operations log. Every consistency knob MongoDB exposes is a way of saying where, relative to the majority commit point, this read or write should stand.

Get that one picture and the eleven-page reference for read and write concern collapses into a single sentence.

This lab is a capstone. Almost nothing in it is a new mechanism; almost everything is a mechanism you already own, wearing MongoDB's name for it. Section 17 lists the sixteen labs it assembles, one by one, and says what each of them contributes.

Everything the checkout screen, Omar's kitchen screen and Dev's courier app need about this order is inside that one value.

Three details of the shape matter later. The items array is bounded, because a restaurant order has a handful of lines. The payment sub-document is one-to-one with the order. And the _id is a 12-byte ObjectId.

An ObjectId is built from three pieces: 4 bytes of seconds since the epoch, 5 random bytes fixed per process, and a 3-byte counter. So it is roughly, but not exactly, ordered by creation time. The docs are careful about that word: "approximately ordered … not perfectly ordered", because clients generate them with their own clocks and one-second resolution.

What are the four demands on this one document?

Four things happen to an order, and each one is a demand on the database.

  1. Checkout creates it. Asha sees "Order placed" and gets an e-mail. That confirmation must not turn out to be false.
  2. Omar's kitchen flips status to cooking, and Dev later flips it to delivered. Two different services write the same document, and neither may lose the other's change.
  3. Asha refreshes the page immediately after checkout. She must see her own order.
  4. Thali grows. Three cities become thirty, and the order collection outgrows one machine.

Hold onto demand 1. It is the one that will fail first, and the way it fails is the whole lesson.

3. What would a careful engineer build first?

In one breath: Keep each order in one place, write it to a journal before answering, and keep two copies that follow along. It is a good design. It has exactly one flaw.

The obvious solution is genuinely good, and it is what most careful people build. It is two moves.

Move one: store the order as one document on a single server. A write changes the document in memory and appends a record to a write-ahead journal. The journal is flushed to disk every 100 ms, and a checkpoint of the whole data set is written every 60 s. Those are WiredTiger's real numbers, and section 10.1 is where they come from. A crash loses at most the last 100 ms of work, unless the write asked to wait for the flush.

Move two: add two copies that follow the server's log, so a dead machine does not mean downtime. The first server acknowledges the write as soon as it has applied the change. The copies pull the log and apply it a heartbeat later. If the first server dies, the copies hold an election and one of them takes over. You know exactly how that works from raft.

That is a replica set with the write concern w: 1. Read the symbol as a sentence: tell me as soon as one machine has it. It is fast, because it is one machine's latency. It survives one dead machine. And it is how MongoDB shipped for its first decade.

It has one flaw. The flaw is invisible until a specific sequence of events.

🔮 Predict: One machine has written Asha's order and has told her so. Two more copies are pulling the log a heartbeat behind. What sequence of events could still leave that order nowhere at all?

Reveal The machine that said yes has to be cut off from the other two, and then stop being the one in charge. If you said "a crash", you are close but not there. A crash while everyone can still see each other is survivable, because a new leader pulls the missing entries from the old one first (section 10.3). What you need is the old leader alive but unreachable while the others move on without it. Section 4 is that sequence, tick by tick.

4. How can an acknowledged order simply vanish?

In one breath: The one clerk who said "written" was cut off a moment later. The other two carried on without her. When she came back, the rule said her lonely line had to go — and Asha's order went with it.

This is the failure the whole lab is built on, and it is scenario 2 in the simulator. The trace below is copied from it, so open the page and step alongside if you like.

Three copies of the order book exist. Meera keeps m1, Ben keeps m2, Arjun keeps m3. Meera is the one taking orders, so she is the primary, and she holds that job in term 1. One tick is one heartbeat, two seconds.

An earlier order, ord-1, was placed with a majority write, so every clerk already has it. That shared line is what makes the damage visible later.

🔮 Predict: At tick 2 Asha's order ord-2 is acknowledged with w: 1 — one machine has it. Before the next heartbeat, Meera's copy m1 is cut off from m2 and m3 by a network fault. The application servers can still reach m2 and m3. After the fault heals, where is ord-2? On all three copies, only on m1, or nowhere in the collection?

Reveal Nowhere. If you said "only on m1", you have the right instinct and the wrong ending: m1 does hold it alone for six ticks, and then deletes it from the collection itself. If you said "all three", you are assuming the copies catch up after the heal — they do catch up, but in the other direction, because by then m2 is in charge and m2 never saw ord-2. Step the table.

The write is not destroyed. It is sitting in a file called removed.2026-09-05T…bson under m1's data directory, which is where Priya will go looking in section 11.1.

The docs say it plainly. With w: 1, "data can be rolled back if the primary steps down before the write operations replicate to any of the secondaries".

Three things are worth noticing here, because the rest of the lab is built on them.

Why was the acknowledgement true and useless?

w: 1 means "the primary applied it". That statement was correct at tick 2 and it stayed correct forever.

What it did not say was whether any future primary would agree. The failure is not a bug in acknowledgement. It is that order-service asked the wrong question.

Why was Meera right both times?

Meera (the primary, m1) did nothing wrong by accepting the write, and nothing wrong by discarding it.

A Raft-style log has exactly one rule for what survives: entries that a majority holds and that a leader of the latest term has committed. ord-2 was on one member. When Ben's copy m2 won term 2 with Arjun's vote, Meera's unreplicated tail became, by definition, the losing branch.

Your raft lab called the losing half "harmless". MongoDB's word for the act of making it harmless is rollback.

Why did two clerks call themselves head clerk at once?

Look again at ticks 7 and 8. For one full tick, m1 still says PRIMARY in term 1 while m2 says PRIMARY in term 2.

Your split-brain lab predicted that window and measured it. Scenario 4 of the simulator revisits the same window from the reader's side, and section 11.4 is what it does to Priya.

5. What must an acknowledgement actually promise?

In one breath: "Done" has to mean that no future failover can undo this. Say that in plain words first; the vocabulary comes after.

Here is the real requirement, before any MongoDB word is allowed in.

An acknowledgement must mean no future failover can undo this write. A read must be able to ask for only data that no future failover can undo. And a client that just wrote something must be able to read it back, from whichever copy it lands on.

Three demands. Each of them is a position relative to a single line in the log.

That line is the highest entry that a majority of the voting, data-bearing members have durably written. MongoDB calls it the majority commit point.

Below the line, the raft argument you already know applies. Any future election is won by a member whose log contains that entry, so it can never be dropped. Above the line, an entry is one machine's opinion.

Now let the vocabulary in. Each demand becomes one knob.

  • A write should be acknowledged when it falls below the line. That is w: "majority".
  • A read should be able to return only what is below the line. That is read concern "majority".
  • A read that must be fresh, not merely durable, has to check that the member answering is still on the majority's side of the world. That is read concern "linearizable".
  • A client that must see its own write from a lagging copy has to carry the position of that write, and let the copy wait until it has caught up. That is a causally consistent session.

Everything in sections 7 to 10.5 is those four sentences, in MongoDB's vocabulary.

6. What is the key idea, in two sentences?

In one breath: MongoDB is two decisions. One document is the unit of everything. One line in the log is the difference between an opinion and a fact.

Decision one: the document is the unit. One document is changed atomically, stored contiguously, replicated as one log entry, and placed on one shard by a key inside it.

Design your data so that the things you read and write together are inside one document, and you never need a transaction, a join, or a cross-shard query. Design it so they are spread across documents, and you pay for every one of those.

Decision two: durability is a line in a replicated log, and every consistency knob is a position relative to that line. The knobs are not a menu of speeds.

They are answers to one question: how far below the commit point must this operation stand? Once you see them that way, the tables in the reference manual stop being tables to memorise.

The rest of the lab is the consequences. What happens inside a document is WiredTiger, section 10.1. Between the copies is the replica set, sections 10.2 to 10.5. Across documents is transactions, section 10.6. Across machines is sharding, section 10.7. And how a query finds a document is indexes and planning, section 10.8.

7. What are these things called, now that you have felt them?

In one breath: Nine words carry the rest of the lab. Every one of them names something you have already watched happen to Asha's order.

This is the lab's terminology, and it is earned rather than dumped. Each word arrives attached to the moment it was needed. The precise, one-line definitions of all of them live in the glossary, section 16.

What exactly is a document, and what is the 16 MB wall for?

Asha's order is a document: a JSON-like value with named fields, arrays and nested values. A collection is a bag of documents. A database is a set of collections. That is the entire storage vocabulary.

On disk a document is not JSON. It is BSON, a binary encoding of JSON with extra types that JSON does not have.

Extra type What it holds Where you have already seen it
ObjectId the 12-byte id, roughly time-ordered Asha's _id, section 2
Date a point in time placedAt, section 2
Decimal128 exact decimal money the amount field, section 2
Binary raw bytes not used by Thali
Timestamp an internal 64-bit value: 32 bits of seconds plus a 32-bit counter the ts on every line of the trace in section 4

Two hard limits come with the unit. A document may not exceed 16 mebibytes, and it may not nest deeper than 100 levels. A collection has no enforced schema unless you add one, with $jsonSchema validation, section 13.4.

Do not read the 16 MB limit as a nuisance to route around. It is the boundary of the unit itself. It is what makes "one document is atomic, contiguous, and replicated as one entry" possible at all. Section 11.9 is what happens to someone who forgets that.

Who are Meera, Ben and Arjun to the database?

The three clerks of section 4 are a replica set: a group of servers holding the same data. One of them, the primary, takes writes. The others, secondaries, copy from it. A fourth kind of member, an arbiter, votes in elections but stores nothing at all.

The precise limits: a set may have up to 50 members, of which at most 7 vote. Exactly one member is primary per term. A member with priority: 0 can never be primary. A hidden member is invisible to clients. A member with secondaryDelaySecs set is deliberately kept behind, so that an operator's mistake can be recovered from it.

This is raft's cluster wearing MongoDB's names: leader is primary, follower is secondary. It adds one member type Raft does not have at all. The arbiter exists so that a set with only two data members can still elect. Section 11.3 is the trap that buys.

What is the oplog, and how can a clerk fall off the end of it?

The oplog is the replication log: every change, in order, written as an operation that can be applied more than once without harm.

Mechanically it is a capped collection called local.oplog.rs, present on every member. Each entry carries ts (its Timestamp), t (the term it was written in), the namespace, and the operation itself.

Entries are idempotent — "oplog operations produce the same results whether applied once or multiple times". That is why $inc: 5 is recorded as the resulting value rather than as the increment. The translation is an implementation detail; the idempotence is documented.

The oplog's size on WiredTiger defaults to 5 % of free disk, with a minimum of 990 MB and a maximum of 50 GB. It is allowed to grow past its own cap rather than delete the majority commit point.

The time span between its oldest and newest entry is the oplog window. A secondary that falls behind by more than the window cannot catch up by replication at all. It must re-copy everything, which is called an initial sync, and section 11.6 is what that costs.

This is Raft's log, with one change: it is pulled, not pushed. Section 10.3 is why.

Where exactly is the line between an opinion and a fact?

The majority commit point is the newest entry that enough members hold for no election to be able to lose it.

Precisely: it is the highest optime, the pair (ts, t), written to the oplog and journal of a calculated majority of voting, data-bearing members. "Calculated majority" is the smaller of two counts: the voting majority, and the number of voting data-bearing members.

It can only advance over entries from the current primary's own term. That is Raft's Figure 8 rule, and it is exactly why a new primary writes a no-op the moment it wins — you watched m2 do that at tick 7 in section 4. Priya can see the line in rs.status(), where it is called lastCommittedOpTime.

This is the line from section 5. Every other word in this lab is defined relative to it.

What may a client ask for before it is told "done"?

Write concern is what must be true before the client is told the write succeeded. It is written as one small object:

{ w: <1 | n | "majority">, j: <bool>, wtimeout: <ms> }

Read the two values you will actually use as sentences. w: 1 means the primary applied it in memory. w: "majority" means a calculated majority "have durably written the change to their local oplog".

"Durably" is doing real work in that sentence. Because writeConcernMajorityJournalDefault is true by default, durably means on the on-disk journal — so j: true is implied and you do not have to ask for it.

The implicit default since 5.0 is w: "majority". There is one exception, and it is the nastiest thing in this lab. If the set contains an arbiter, and the number of data-bearing voters is not greater than the voting majority, then the default silently becomes w: 1. Section 11.3 is that story.

wtimeout turns a wait into an error. It never undoes the write, which is a sentence worth repeating to yourself before section 11.2.

How far below the line must a read stand?

Read concern answers exactly that question, and it has five settings.

Read concern In words The catch
"local" the member's latest state, whatever it holds the default everywhere; may be rolled back
"available" the same, minus one sharding safety check may return orphaned documents, section 10.7
"majority" the state at the member's known commit point durable, and possibly stale
"linearizable" the freshest majority-committed state, or nothing primary only; needs maxTimeMS
"snapshot" one majority-committed snapshot at one cluster time used by transactions, and since 5.0 by some non-transactional reads

Two of those rows need a longer sentence. "linearizable" reflects every majority-acknowledged write that completed before the read began, and it may wait for in-flight writes to reach a majority before answering. "snapshot" is the concern a transaction runs at, which is why it turns up again in section 10.6.

Who answers the read, and why is that a different question?

Read preference decides which member the driver sends a read to: primary (the default), primaryPreferred, secondary, secondaryPreferred or nearest. It can be narrowed further with tag sets and with maxStalenessSeconds.

Carry the docs' own sentence: "All read preference modes except primary may return stale data because secondaries replicate operations from the primary in an asynchronous process."

Read preference chooses who answers. Read concern chooses what they are allowed to answer with. They are orthogonal, and confusing them is wrong model 14.4.

What are the four sharding words?

These four arrive properly in section 10.7, when Thali reaches thirty cities. One line each, so the word is not a surprise when it appears.

A shard key is the field or fields that decide which server a document lives on. A range, also called a chunk, is a contiguous span of shard-key values that moves as one piece. mongos is the router that clients talk to. The config servers keep the metadata that says which range lives where.

If those sound familiar, they should. They are partitioning-and-rebalancing's partition key, partition, routing tier and partition map, with MongoDB's names on them.

8. Which two mental models should you carry, and where does the nice one lie?

In one breath: Carry a picture of three clerks with ink and pencil, and carry the precise version underneath it. Then learn the five places where the nice picture will quietly mislead you.

What does the simple model look like?

Thali's orders are recorded by three clerks: Meera, Ben and Arjun. Each of them keeps an identical ledger.

Only one clerk, the head clerk, takes dictation from the shop floor. The other two copy her ledger, line by line, whenever they can see it. Every line is numbered with the page and line, which is ts, and with the head clerk's badge number, which is t, the term.

A line is in ink once two of the three clerks have written it. Two of three can always out-vote one, so no future head clerk can be someone who lacks that line. A line only one clerk has is in pencil: real, visible, and erasable.

The shop floor can ask for two kinds of receipt. "Tell me when you've written it" is answered as soon as the head clerk's pencil leaves the page — that is w: 1. "Tell me when it's in ink" is answered only when a second clerk has it — that is w: "majority".

Anyone reading a ledger can ask for whatever is on the page, which is read concern local, or for only the ink, which is majority. And if they must have the very latest ink, the head clerk first shouts across the room to check she is still the head clerk. That shout is linearizable.

If the head clerk is locked in a back room, the other two wait ten seconds and elect a new head clerk. The one with the longer ledger wins, and she starts with a fresh badge number.

When the old head clerk is let out, she compares ledgers. She finds her pencilled lines missing from the new head clerk's book, and rubs them out into a scrap file. That is section 4, told without a single MongoDB word.

Where does the story lie to you?

Read this before the analogy hardens. Five lies, each with the truth that replaces it.

  1. Clerks never write in pencil by default. MongoDB primaries did for ten years. Until 5.0 the implicit default write concern was w: 1. The analogy makes ink the obvious choice. The history, section 13.1, is a decade of people not choosing it — and a Primary-Secondary-Arbiter set still does not, section 11.3.
  2. The head clerk knows when a line is in ink. A client does not, unless it asked. In the analogy the ink is visible on the page. In MongoDB a w: 1 client is told nothing more, ever. The state of a write it did not wait for is simply unknown to it.
  3. "Only the ink" is not "the latest ink". A locked-in head clerk's ledger still has plenty of ink in it — old ink. Read concern "majority" on a stale primary returns durable data that a newer primary has already superseded. Scenario 4 is exactly this, and it is why "linearizable" exists as a separate level rather than as a stronger "majority".
  4. The ledger has one page order. A sharded cluster has many ledgers. Nothing in the analogy covers the moment a range moves between shards. Section 10.7 does.

10.1 What does one document cost, and what does a crash lose?

Start where Thali started: one server, one question. What does "the document is the unit of atomicity" actually buy, and what does a crash take away?

Since 3.2 the default storage engine is WiredTiger. Five things about it matter for this lab, and all five come from the manual's WiredTiger and journaling pages.

Documents are the unit of concurrency. The manual: "WiredTiger uses document-level concurrency control for write operations. As a result, multiple clients can modify different documents of a collection at the same time." At the global, database and collection level there are only intent locks.

Two writers on the same document are resolved optimistically. "When the storage engine detects conflicts between two operations, one will incur a write conflict causing MongoDB to transparently retry that operation."

That is cockroachdb's posture, proceed and detect, and it is the posture from optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking. It decides how transactions fail in section 10.6, so remember it.

Every operation reads a snapshot. "At the start of an operation, WiredTiger provides a point-in-time snapshot of the data to the operation." This is MVCC, exactly as mvcc-and-vacuum taught it.

Register one difference. The snapshot is for a single operation. A find that returns a cursor and iterates over many batches is not one snapshot.

The read-isolation page says a cursor "could return the same document more than once" if a concurrent update moves it in the index being scanned. It adds that such a cursor may see a document updated after the read began.

So a point-in-time view across a whole cursor needs read concern "snapshot" or a transaction. Nothing weaker gives it to you.

Durability is journal, then checkpoint. A checkpoint writes the snapshot to disk "at intervals of 60 seconds". Between checkpoints, every write goes to the journal, which is the write-ahead log. That journal is buffered in memory.

That buffer is synced "at every 100 milliseconds" (storage.journal.commitIntervalMs). It is also synced immediately when a write carries j: true, and on a secondary after each applied batch.

Read the manual's warning exactly as written: "In between write operations, while the journal records remain in the WiredTiger buffers, updates can be lost following a hard shutdown of mongod." Journaling cannot be turned off at all since 6.1.

🔮 Predict: A single MongoDB server loses power 40 ms after acknowledging one of Omar's status updates with w: 1. When it comes back, where is that update — applied, in a rollback file, or gone with no trace?

Reveal Gone, with no trace at all. If you said "in a rollback file", you are applying section 4's ending to the wrong failure: a rollback file exists only when a member has to reconcile with a different history. Here there is no other history — the update never reached the journal on disk, so nothing anywhere ever knew about it. That is the honest difference between losing 100 ms to a power cut and losing an acknowledged write to a partition.

Memory is mostly cache. The internal cache is "the larger of either: 50% of (RAM − 1 GB), or 0.256 GB". Everything else goes to the filesystem cache, in on-disk compressed format. Collections are block-compressed with snappy by default, zstd for time-series, and indexes use prefix compression.

On disk it is B-trees. WiredTiger stores collections and indexes as B-trees. A collection's B-tree is keyed by an internal record id, not by _id. The _id index is a separate B-tree that maps to it.

So in btree-and-lsm's vocabulary, MongoDB is "heap plus index", not InnoDB-style clustered — unless you create a clustered collection, which is a 5.3 feature.

Version 1 gives you atomic, snapshot-read documents, with a durability window of up to 100 ms for a write that did not ask to wait. It fails the moment the machine dies: downtime, and no copy.

Checkpoint. A single-document write is atomic and isolated with no transaction. A crash inside the journal interval loses unjournaled writes with no rollback file, because they were never anywhere. Copies next.

10.3 How do the copies elect a new head clerk, and what is MongoDB's one big change to Raft?

The NSDI 2021 paper Fault-Tolerant Replication with Pull-Based Consensus in MongoDB (Zhou and Mu) describes MongoDB's protocol as derived from Raft, with one structural change: replication is pull-based.

In Raft the leader pushes AppendEntries to every follower. In MongoDB "a replica pulls new data from another replica, which can be initiated by any replica and can happen between any two replicas".

So a secondary chooses a sync source and tails its oplog. That source is the primary, or another secondary that is ahead of it, because the default setting is chainingAllowed: true.

The primary does not learn who has what by pushing. Secondaries report their positions back with replSetUpdatePosition, and the primary computes the commit point from those reports.

In the simulator that is the one-tick gap between "m2 pulls" and "commit point advances". Scenario 1 acknowledges the majority write at tick 2, not at tick 1.

Why pull at all? The paper gives two reasons that survive a design review.

A sync source can be any member, which spreads replication fan-out across the set and works across slow links — a secondary in Mumbai can pull from a secondary in Mumbai. And a member that is far behind, or has just restarted, can catch up from whoever is convenient rather than loading the primary.

The rest is Raft with MongoDB's numbers. All of these come from the elections and configuration pages. First, the timers and the votes.

Why does catch-up not save Asha's order?

That catch-up row looks like it contradicts section 4, and it is worth one paragraph.

If Meera's copy m1 had been reachable when Ben's copy m2 won, m2 would have pulled ord-2 from m1 during catch-up, and ord-2 would have survived.

So w: 1 writes are lost only when the old primary is unreachable during the election. A partition guarantees that. A clean crash does not, because a crashed member has no writes anyone could have pulled anyway.

This is what the docs mean by "rollbacks … should be rare. When a rollback does occur, it is often the result of a network partition."

What stops a fast primary from starving the commit point?

A primary that races ahead of its secondaries makes the commit point lag, and every w: "majority" write then waits behind that lag.

Since 4.2 the primary throttles itself. The manual: "as the lag grows close to the flowControlTargetLagSeconds, writes on the primary must obtain tickets before taking locks". Engineers call this flow control; it is enabled by default, and the target's default is 10 s.

Version 3 elects correctly and never has two primaries in one term. It still tells clients "done" one machine too early.

10.4 What does it take to make an acknowledgement true?

The commit point now exists. So redefine the acknowledgement around it.

w: "majority" "requests acknowledgment that the calculated majority of data-bearing voting members have durably written the change to their local oplog". Three words in that sentence carry weight.

Durably. With writeConcernMajorityJournalDefault: true, which is the default, the acknowledgement waits for the journal on each of those members. j: true is implied. Set it to false and "majority write operations could possibly roll back in the event of a transient loss (e.g. crash and restart) of a majority of nodes".

Calculated. The count is min(voting majority, number of voting data-bearing members). In a Primary-Secondary-Arbiter set that is min(2, 2) = 2, meaning both data members. The arbiter is worthless for the count. Section 11.3.

Acknowledged by whom. Only the primary that accepted the write can acknowledge it. If that primary loses its role while the client waits, the client gets an error rather than an acknowledgement, and it does not know what happened. Scenario 3 shows both possible outcomes from one identical error: the first write is rolled back, and the second survives.

Why does the retry not create a second order?

That last row is retryable writes, and it has been in MongoDB since 3.6. It is on by default in drivers compatible with 4.2+.

Every single-document write carries two things: a logical session id, and a transaction number that counts up within that session. The docs: "Retryable writes let drivers retry specific write operations once after network errors or if they cannot find a healthy primary".

The server keeps the outcome of each (lsid, txnNumber) pair in a replicated table. So a retry that reaches a primary which already applied that write is answered from the record, instead of being applied a second time.

This is your idempotency lab's key idea, built into the wire protocol rather than into your application.

Three limits are worth knowing before you rely on it. Only single-document writes, and bulk writes made of them, are retryable — updateMany and deleteMany are not. Writes inside a transaction are not individually retryable. And since 6.1, a retry that never performed a write returns the NoWritesPerformed label, so the application can tell "nothing happened" from "unknown".

Two more sentences from the write-concern page end arguments before they start.

The first is about wtimeout. It "causes write operations to return with a write concern error after the specified limit, even if the required write concern will eventually succeed … MongoDB does not undo successful data modifications". And: "If you do not specify the wtimeout option and the level of write concern is unachievable, the write operation will block indefinitely."

The second is about the default. "Starting in MongoDB 5.0, { w: "majority" } is the default write concern for most MongoDB deployments". Read that word most twice. Section 11.3 is the rest.

What does a majority acknowledgement still not give you?

It does not make a write visible to a client reading somewhere else at any particular moment. Scenario 5 is that.

It does not make a read on the old primary fresh. Scenario 4 is that.

It only makes the acknowledgement true. Version 5 is the read side.

10.5 What can a read ask for, and who answers it?

Four readers, four different answers, one line.

Read concern "local" is the default. It returns the member's latest applied state. On a primary that includes writes above the commit point; on a secondary it is whatever that member has pulled. The docs' warning is three words long: "Data may be rolled back."

Read concern "majority" returns "the data acknowledged by a majority of the replica set members. Returned documents are durable, even if a failure occurs."

Mechanically, each member keeps a WiredTiger snapshot at its known commit point and serves majority reads from it. There is no network round trip, which is why it is cheap — and why it can be stale.

Since 5.0 the storage support for this, enableMajorityReadConcern, is always on and cannot be disabled. That also retired the old 300 MB rollback limit: the "recover to a timestamp" rollback algorithm has no size limit.

Read concern "linearizable" is primary only, and single-document filter only. The docs: "unlike "majority", "linearizable" read concern confirms with secondary members that the read operation is reading from a primary that is capable of confirming writes with { w: "majority" } write concern."

That confirmation is a round trip to a majority before answering. So "always use maxTimeMS" — a partitioned primary would otherwise wait forever. The cost is real: the docs say such reads "may be significantly slower".

This is linearizability's write-back, turned into a read mode. The read does not return until the world agrees who is primary.

10.6 What does crossing the document boundary cost?

Thali's design keeps an order in one document precisely so that placing, cooking and delivering it never need a transaction.

But now Omar wants stock reserved. A write must touch two documents atomically: decrement dal-tadka in inventory, and create the order. The document boundary is crossed.

MongoDB has had multi-document transactions since 4.0 on replica sets, and since 4.2 on sharded clusters. The manual's own framing appears twice on the transactions page, and it is the right framing.

The first: "multi-document transactions are not necessary for many practical use cases". The second: "the availability of distributed transactions should not be a replacement for effective schema design."

What is a MongoDB transaction, exactly?

It is a WiredTiger snapshot held open across several operations, with writes buffered in the storage engine and made visible atomically at commit.

The isolation level is snapshot isolation: every read in the transaction sees the same majority-committed snapshot. It is not serializable, and the difference is not academic.

Your isolation-levels lab's write-skew example applies unchanged. Two transactions each read "courier Dev has no active order". Each assigns Dev. Both commit. Dev now has two.

Nothing in MongoDB prevents that. You prevent it the way you did in that lab: make the two writes touch a common document, or use a unique index, or use a findOneAndUpdate with a conditional filter on one document.

🔮 Predict: A transaction reads the dal-tadka stock document. Before it writes, Omar's kitchen service runs a plain updateOne on the same document. The transaction then tries its update. Which party waits, which party fails, and which party never notices anything?

Reveal The kitchen's plain write never notices anything and succeeds immediately. The transaction fails. Nobody waits. If you said "the plain write waits for the transaction", that is true only in the other order — a transaction that has already taken its lock does block outside writers. Here the transaction had only read the document, so it held nothing, and it is the one that dies with a write conflict.

How does a transaction fail?

Write conflicts are the optimistic engine from section 10.1 surfacing at a level you can see. The production-considerations page states both directions exactly.

"If a transaction is in progress and a write outside the transaction modifies a document that an operation in the transaction later tries to modify, the transaction aborts because of a write conflict."

"If a transaction is in progress and has taken a lock to modify a document, when a write outside the transaction tries to modify the same document, the write waits until the transaction ends."

So a transaction loses to any non-transactional writer that got there first, and it blocks any that come after. Two transactions on the same document: the second to touch it gets a WriteConflict.

The clocks are tight. Transactions "wait up to 5 milliseconds to acquire locks required by the operations in the transaction" and abort otherwise. A transaction "must have a runtime of less than one minute" (transactionLifetimeLimitSeconds, 60).

Every abort carries the TransientTransactionError label, which means "run the whole function again". A commit whose outcome is unknown carries UnknownTransactionCommitResult, which means "retry the commit only". Drivers wrap this as the callback API, withTransaction, which retries on those labels for you — and only on those.

What does a transaction cost?

Three costs, all documented, and Priya will meet all three.

Cache pressure. A long transaction pins old versions that WiredTiger cannot evict. "If you have an uncommitted transaction that causes excessive pressure on the WiredTiger cache, the transaction aborts and returns a write conflict error". A transaction that can never fit returns TransactionTooLargeForCache. The advice is blunt: abort what you abandon, and never leave a transaction open across a user interaction.

Oplog space. Since 4.2 a transaction is written as as many oplog entries as needed, each under 16 MB. The old 16 MB whole-transaction cap is gone. The per-entry cap is not.

Sharded commit. "Regardless of the write concern specified for the transaction, the commit operation for a sharded cluster transaction includes some parts that use {w: "majority", j: true}". And: "transactions that span multiple shards will error and abort if any involved shard contains an arbiter".

A multi-shard commit is a two-phase commit coordinated by one of the shards: prepare on each participant, then commit. Outside reads during that commit split two ways. Reads at "snapshot" or "linearizable", and reads in a causal session, wait until the whole transaction is visible. Other read concerns "read the before-transaction version".

That is distributed-transactions' atomic visibility problem, solved the timestamp way.

Which rule did Jepsen find missing?

Read and write concern inside a transaction are set on the transaction, not inherited. The docs say what happens if you get that wrong.

With a w: 1 commit, "transaction-level "snapshot" read concern provides no guarantee that read operations in the transaction used a snapshot of majority-committed data", and the transaction "can be rolled back if there is a failover".

The May 2020 Jepsen report on 4.2.6 tested exactly that. The then-defaults were local and w: 1, with transactions ignoring database-level settings. Under those settings it observed lost writes and read skew. Even at snapshot and majority it observed anomalies, traced to a retry bug: SERVER-48307, fixed in 4.2.8.

Two things changed as a result. 5.0 made w: "majority" the implicit default, and the manual now carries the sentence above.

The rule for you: set both concerns on every transaction explicitly. Do not rely on inheritance.

Version 6 gives you atomic, snapshot-isolated writes across documents at a real cost. It still lives on one replica set. Thali now has thirty cities.

How do you choose a shard key without regretting it?

A shard key is "either a single indexed field or multiple fields covered by a compound index that determines the distribution of the collection's documents among the cluster's shards."

There are two flavours. { customerId: 1 } is ranged: contiguous key values sit together, so a range query on the key is targeted. { customerId: "hashed" } is hashed: adjacent values scatter, which is the cure for a monotonically increasing key.

🔮 Predict: Thali wants the nightly "orders placed today" report to be fast, so someone proposes sharding orders on placedAt. What happens to the write path the day that ships?

Reveal Every insert goes to one shard: the one holding the range that ends at MaxKey. So a three-shard cluster writes at one shard's speed. If you said "the balancer will spread it out", the balancer moves the hot range away and the next inserts pile into the new end range, so you get the same bottleneck plus constant migration traffic. Section 11.7 is the full story.

The choose-a-shard-key page names three properties. Each one has a Thali failure attached.

Then there is the property that decides latency: does the query contain the shard key?

The manual: "mongos routes queries to only the shards that contain the relevant data if the queries contain the shard key. When the queries do not contain the shard key, the queries are broadcast to all shards." That broadcast is scatter-gather, and such queries "do not scale linearly when more shards are added".

For Thali, find({customerId}) is targeted. find({courierId}) scatters to every shard, and the sort is performed on each shard before mongos merges. A prefix of a compound key targets too.

Single-document updates are stricter. "All updateOne(), replaceOne() and deleteOne() operations must include the shard key or _id in the query document." updateMany is always broadcast unless the full key is present.

What is a range, and who decides it should move?

"Each chunk has inclusive lower and exclusive upper limits based on the shard key", and the default range size is 128 MB.

Ranges are no longer split as they grow: "a sharded cluster only splits chunks when chunks must be migrated". A range that cannot be split because it holds a single key value is jumbo.

The balancer "runs on the primary of the config server replica set". A collection counts as balanced when the largest and smallest shard differ by less than three times the range size. In the manual's numbers: "for the default range size of 128MB, two shards must have a data size difference … of at least 384MB for a migration to occur".

A shard takes part in at most one migration at a time, so a cluster of n shards runs at most ⌊n/2⌋ migrations concurrently.

The migration itself is your handover protocol, in the manual's seven steps. moveRange goes to the source. The destination builds missing indexes. The destination copies the documents in the range. The destination pulls the delta of changes made during the copy. The source enters a critical section and updates the config metadata. The source deletes its copy once no cursor is open on the range.

That last step is asynchronous and queued, so a shard can hold orphaned documents for a while. Which is exactly why read concern "available" exists: "local" on a sharded cluster filters orphans by checking ownership, and "available" skips the check and may return them.

Two more details. Since 8.2 a long-running secondary read may be terminated before orphan deletion runs. And _secondaryThrottle makes the migration wait for a write concern per document.

What if you chose the wrong key?

You are not stuck, but nothing here is free.

Since 4.2 a document's shard-key value may be updated, though not if the key is _id. refineCollectionShardKey adds suffix fields to an existing key, and it is the documented cure for jumbo ranges caused by low cardinality. reshardCollection, from 5.0, changes the key entirely and rewrites the collection in the background.

Version 8.0 added moveCollection for unsharded collections between shards, built on the resharding machinery; it "has a 5 minute minimum duration" and ends with a write block of about two seconds. It also added unshardCollection, and it let a shard double as the config server. That config shard "combines the config server role into an existing shard's replica set, so your cluster needs one replica set instead of two".

Version 8.3 restricts DDL on sharded clusters to mongos, and replaces removeShard with explicit draining commands.

What breaks without the config servers?

"If the config server replica set loses its primary and cannot elect a primary, the cluster's metadata becomes read only. You can still read and write data from the shards, but no chunk migration or chunk splits will occur." And: "if all config servers become unavailable, the cluster can become inoperable."

A mongos "has no persistent state". It caches the routing table and refreshes it when a shard tells it the version is stale. That is your MOVED/ASK retry from consistent-hashing, under another name.

Version 7 scales writes and storage. The last version is how any of this finds a document at all.

10.8 How does a query actually find a document?

Indexes are B-trees, one per index, with the mechanics btree-and-lsm gave you. Four things are MongoDB-specific.

The limits. Every collection has a unique index on _id. A collection may have at most 64 indexes, a compound index at most 32 fields, and a sort may use at most 32 keys.

Compound indexes obey the ESR rule. "Ensure that equality fields always come first", then sort fields, then range fields.

The manual's worked example is the query find({directors: "David Lynch", runtime: {$lt: 130}}).sort({year: 1}) with the index {directors: 1, year: 1, runtime: 1}. Equality on directors narrows to one key prefix. The index is already in year order, so there is no in-memory sort. And runtime is the range that walks the tail.

Put the range before the sort and the engine must sort in memory, which is a blocking sort. Omar's kitchen screen runs find({restaurantId, status: "cooking"}).sort({placedAt: 1}), and the index it wants is {restaurantId: 1, status: 1, placedAt: 1}.

Three traps the page names: $ne, $nin and $regex are range operators, and $in behaves as equality up to 200 elements and as a range from 201.

The other index kinds, one line each. Multikey indexes each element of an array field. Unique rejects duplicates. Partial indexes only documents matching a filter, and is cheaper than sparse. TTL deletes by a date field in the background, and the 8.3 serverStatus now reports TTL work. Hashed backs hashed shard keys. Then wildcard, text and 2dsphere. A query answered entirely from the index, without fetching documents, is covered.

Index builds, since 4.4, run "simultaneously across all data-bearing replica set members". They hold an exclusive collection lock "only at the start and end of the build". They finish when a commit quorum has built the index, and that quorum defaults to "votingMembers". They use 200 MB of memory per createIndexes by default, and can resume once after a clean restart.

How does MongoDB choose a plan, and why is that unusual?

This is the one place MongoDB differs from every relational planner you know.

The classic engine is empirical. For a new query shape it runs the candidate plans for a short trial. Then it picks: "the winning plan is the query plan that produces the most results during the trial period while performing the least amount of work".

The winner goes into the plan cache for that query shape. It arrives as Inactive, carrying a work estimate, and is promoted to Active when a later planning round confirms it. It is evicted by LRU, on any DDL such as an index create, drop or hide, or when its measured work stops meeting the criterion, which is called replanning.

8.3 adds a cost-based ranker. "Multi-planning with a cost-based ranker backup is the default plan selection mechanism for eligible queries." The trial runs first, and only if it cannot find a plan that returns a result in time does the ranker score plans by cost and cardinality estimates. The manual is candid about the scope: "Currently, MongoDB only invokes the CBR for a small amount of queries."

Priya reads the result with explain(). COLLSCAN is the smell. IXSCAN then FETCH is normal. SORT after FETCH is the blocking sort that ESR avoids. And totalKeysExamined versus nReturned is the ratio to watch.

Aggregation is the read path for anything beyond find: a pipeline of stages such as $match, $group, $lookup, $unwind, $project and $sort. At most 1 000 stages, and each stage is allowed 100 MB of memory before spilling to disk. Since 6.0 allowDiskUseByDefault is true, so spills happen unless you forbid them.

$lookup is the join you designed away by embedding. It exists, it is fine for reporting, and it is the wrong tool for the hot path in a schema that was built around documents.

Checkpoint. The machine is complete. One atomic document, a pull-replicated Raft log with a commit line, four read positions and a session. Then snapshot-isolated transactions across documents, ranges of documents moved between shard replica sets by a config-server balancer, and B-tree indexes chosen by trial. Now break it on purpose.

11. What are the twelve failure scenarios, and what survives each one?

In one breath: Twelve ways Thali's cluster goes wrong. Priya is on call for all of them, and only two of them lose data that was ever acknowledged.

The first four are in the simulator, so you can watch them rather than take my word for them. Each one below is a short story; the side-by-side comparison is the table at the end of the section.

11.1 What happens when the primary is partitioned after a w: 1 acknowledgement?

This is scenario 2, traced tick by tick in section 4. Asha's order is gone.

What Priya should watch for: the old primary keeps calling itself PRIMARY for a full election timeout, and keeps accepting writes from anyone who can still reach it.

Everything at or below the commit point survives. Everything above it is lost, into <dbpath>/rollback/<collectionUUID>/removed.<timestamp>.bson — with one exception worth knowing before you go looking. "If the operation to roll back is a collection drop or a document deletion, the rollback … is not written to the rollback data directory."

In production rs.status() shows a member in ROLLBACK, and the log says so plainly. A rollback that spans more than rollbackTimeLimitSecs of oplog fails outright, and the member must then be re-synced. That limit is 24 h.

Those rollback files are the only copy of the acknowledged-and-lost data. Somebody, in practice Priya, has to decide by hand what to do with them. Exercise 7.1 is that runbook.

11.2 What happens to a majority write when the majority is unavailable?

This is scenario 3's first half, and scenario 6.

The client waits. Without wtimeout it waits forever. With wtimeout the client gets an error — and the entry stays in the primary's oplog.

Now the important part: that error means nothing. The write may replicate a second later and survive, or it may be rolled back. Nobody knows yet, least of all the client.

Priya sees a spike in write latency, wtimeout errors in the application log, and replSetGetStatus.optimes.lastCommittedOpTime frozen at an old value.

The right client behaviour is a retryable write, section 10.4, or an idempotent retry of your own. The wrong behaviour is the natural one: "it errored, so it did not happen."

11.3 What does an arbiter do to your default write concern?

🔮 Predict: A Primary-Secondary-Arbiter set. The one secondary is down, and order-service writes with the implicit default write concern. Does the write hang, get acknowledged, or get rejected?

Reveal Acknowledged — by one machine, with no second copy anywhere. If you said "it hangs", you have applied the 5.0 default correctly and missed the exception that MongoDB makes precisely to avoid that hang. If you said "rejected", nothing in MongoDB rejects a write for being unsafe; it quietly redefines what safe means.

This is scenario 6, and it is the nastiest configuration in this lab.

The arbiter is cheap. It votes, it stores nothing, and it lets a set with two data members elect a primary when one of them dies. The price is one clause in the write-concern page: "if the number of data-bearing voting members is not greater than the voting majority, the default write concern is { w: 1 }".

Work through it. With one secondary down, w: "majority" cannot be satisfied at all, because the calculated majority is both data members. MongoDB's 5.0 default would therefore make every write hang. Rather than hang, it keeps the old default.

Every write is then exactly as safe as section 4 — which is to say, not.

Then the second failure arrives. The surviving primary dies too, and the secondary comes back. The secondary wins the election with the arbiter's vote, and the old primary's un-replicated writes, acknowledged ones included, roll back on rejoin.

In production this looks like a healthy cluster right up to the double failure. The tell is db.adminCommand({getDefaultRWConcern: 1}) reporting w: 1 with defaultWriteConcernSource: "implicit".

One more consequence: multi-shard transactions refuse to run on a shard that contains an arbiter. The docs' own advice is three data-bearing members.

11.4 What happens when a stale primary serves reads?

Scenario 4. The client and the old primary are on the same side of a partition. The driver sees one member that says PRIMARY, and believes it.

For up to electionTimeoutMillis, plus its own timer's phase, that member answers reads. local returns its latest and possibly about-to-be-rolled-back state. majority returns its frozen commit point. Only linearizable refuses, because it must hear from a majority before it answers.

In production this is a brief window of stale reads during every partition-induced failover, visible only if you look for it. It is the class of anomaly the 2015 Jepsen post found, and nothing at local or majority prevents it.

11.5 What happens when the read lands on the lagging secondary?

Scenario 5. Asha refreshes, and her own order is not there.

Watch what counted. The majority acknowledgement counted Meera's m1 and Ben's m2. The read went to Arjun's m3.

Everything survives here. Nothing is wrong. The read is merely early, which is the most infuriating kind of correct.

Three fixes exist. A causal session makes the read wait. maxStalenessSeconds makes the driver avoid members it estimates as too far behind, and its floor is 90 s. Or read from the primary.

In production this arrives as "read-after-write" bug reports that reproduce only under load, when a secondary falls behind. Priya watches rs.printSecondaryReplicationInfo() and the replication lag metric.

11.6 What happens when a secondary falls off the oplog?

A secondary is down for longer than the oplog window. Or the primary writes faster than the window can absorb.

The tell is that the secondary's last optime is older than the oldest entry the primary still holds. Replication cannot resume from there at all.

The member goes to RECOVERING and needs an initial sync: a full copy of all data, which on a large set is hours of work and a heavy load on whichever member it syncs from.

Prevention is documented. oplogMinRetentionHours keeps entries even past the size cap, and replSetResizeOplog grows the oplog without a restart.

The metric is the oplog window in hours, from rs.printReplicationInfo(). The alert Priya wants is "window shorter than the longest maintenance you would ever do to one member".

11.7 What happens when the shard key only goes up?

Thali shards on _id, an ObjectId, or on placedAt. Both only ever increase.

Every insert lands in the range that ends at MaxKey, so one shard takes every write. The balancer moves that range away, the next inserts go to the new end range, and the cluster is a single-writer system with extra steps.

The fix is hashed sharding on that key, or a compound key whose first field spreads writes, such as {customerId, placedAt}.

In production one shard's write ops and CPU are pinned while the others idle. Since 7.0, analyzeShardKey reports monotonicity directly, so you can check before you commit.

11.8 What does a scatter-gather with a sort and a limit cost?

Dev's courier queue runs find({courierId: "k-19"}).sort({placedAt: -1}).limit(20) on a collection sharded by customerId.

mongos sends the query to every shard. Each shard sorts and returns its top 20. mongos merges them.

Latency is the slowest shard's latency. Adding shards makes this query worse, not better.

The fix is a shard key that matches the dominant query, or a separate collection or view for the courier's queue — which is the cqrs move.

In production explain shows a SHARD_MERGE over every shard, and p99 latency tracks the unhealthiest shard in the cluster.

11.9 What happens when an array grows without bound?

Thali embeds every status-change event in the order. That is fine, because it is bounded.

Then someone embeds every review in the restaurant document. Omar's restaurant is popular, so his document grows without bound.

Each update rewrites the whole document. Every read fetches every review. And one day an insert fails with BSONObjectTooLarge, which has been error code 10334 since 8.3.

The fix is the manual's embedding-versus-referencing rule: reference when "your embedded data grows without bounds". Or use the bucket pattern, one document per restaurant per month.

In production the warning arrives long before the error: document size p99 climbing in the profiler, updates getting slower, and then the hard failure.

11.10 What happens to a transaction that loses every race?

A transaction reads a stock document, makes a slow external call, and then writes it.

Every non-transactional writer that touches the document in the meantime causes the transaction to abort with WriteConflict, because the transaction loses to a prior outside write. Every writer that arrives after the transaction's write blocks on it.

Under contention the transaction retries forever, and the callback API's retry loop looks exactly like a hang.

The fix is to make no external calls inside a transaction, keep it under a few milliseconds, or redesign so the hot document is updated with a single conditional findOneAndUpdate.

Priya's tells are serverStatus().transactions.totalAborted climbing, and TransientTransactionError in the logs.

11.11 What causes cache pressure, and what does it look like?

Two causes, one symptom.

A long-open transaction or snapshot pins history that WiredTiger cannot evict. A lagging majority commit point does the same thing for majority reads, because the storage engine must keep the state at the commit point available.

The symptom is WiredTiger cache "dirty" and "bytes in cache" climbing toward the cache size, application threads waiting for cache eviction, and eventually transactions aborted with write conflicts or TransactionTooLargeForCache.

In production the numbers live in serverStatus().wiredTiger.cache. For the lag case, the flowControl metrics show the primary being throttled — which is the cure doing its job, not a second fault.

11.12 What causes elections nobody asked for?

A garbage-collection pause. A stalled disk. A rolling restart. Any of them makes the primary miss heartbeats for 10 s.

The secondaries elect. The old primary returns, steps down, and any w: 1 tail it accumulated rolls back.

In production Priya sees rs.status() term numbers climbing, and about 12 s per event with no writes. She also sees NotWritablePrimary errors in the application, which retryable writes absorb once.

There is a trap in the obvious fix. Lowering electionTimeoutMillis makes failover faster and makes this happen more often. That is the trade the docs describe under "failover sensitivity", and exercise 4.1 is where you price it.

12. What is correctness here, and what does it cost?

In one breath: Eight promises, seven non-promises, four assumptions underneath all of them, and a bill. Every one of the guarantees is conditional on something the application had to ask for.

12.1 What can you actually rely on?

These are the guarantees, and each one is either something you watched or something the simulator refuses to break.

  • A single-document write is atomic, always, with no transaction: "a read operation will never see the document with only some of the fields updated".
  • A write acknowledged with w: "majority" (and journaled voting members) is never rolled back. This is the Raft argument: any future primary must hold it. In the simulator's free play you cannot make a rollback file appear using only majority writes. Its engine asserts the stronger form: no entry at or below any member's commit point is ever in a rollback file.
  • Read concern "majority" never returns data that will be rolled back.
  • Read concern "linearizable" on a single document, combined with w: "majority" writes, is linearizable: it "enables multiple threads to read and write a single document as if a single thread performed these operations in real time".
  • A transaction committed with w: "majority" and read concern "snapshot" is snapshot isolated, and on a sharded cluster the snapshot "is synchronized across shards".
  • A causally consistent session with majority reads and majority writes gives read-your-writes, monotonic reads, monotonic writes, and writes-follow-reads.
  • Change streams emit only majority-committed changes — "notifications trigger only for majority-committed changes that are durable in failure scenarios".
  • At most one primary per term, and "a replica set member cannot become PRIMARY unless it has the highest optime of any visible member".

12.2 What is not guaranteed?

This list is shorter to read and more expensive to forget.

  • Nothing about a w: 1 write beyond "the primary applied it". Nothing at all about a write whose acknowledgement never arrived.
  • Freshness of a "majority" read. Freshness of any read on a secondary.
  • Serializability. Transactions are snapshot isolated, and write skew is allowed.
  • Atomicity across documents without a transaction — even inside one collection, even for a single updateMany. That is atomic per document, not as a whole.
  • A point-in-time view across a cursor's batches, without "snapshot" or a transaction.
  • Causal guarantees at any concern combination other than majority with majority.
  • The fate of a write that was in flight during a multi-shard migration's critical section. It is retried by the client against the new owner, and correctness depends on that write being retryable.

12.3 What has to be true underneath for any of it to hold?

Four assumptions. Break one and the guarantees above stop being true, quietly.

Voting members journal, because writeConcernMajorityJournalDefault is true. The in-memory storage engine, or that setting turned to false, reopens rollback of majority writes on a majority crash.

A majority of voters can talk to each other. Without that there is no primary, no writes, and linearizable reads time out. That is the CP posture from your cap-theorem and pacelc labs, chosen deliberately.

Clocks do not matter for safety. Terms, optimes and cluster time are all logical. Wall clocks affect election timing and the maxStalenessSeconds estimate, never which write wins.

The application sets its concerns. Every guarantee in 12.1 is conditional on a concern the client chose. The defaults get you majority writes on a Primary-Secondary-Secondary set, and local reads everywhere.

13.2 What shape should the deployment be?

Five decisions, and the reasons behind each.

Three data-bearing voting members across three failure domains, meaning availability zones. Use five if you want to survive a zone loss and a maintenance window at the same time. Keep the count odd, keep voters at seven or fewer, and make extra members non-voting.

No arbiters, unless you have read section 11.3 and accepted every word of it.

A hidden member, or one with secondaryDelaySecs, for analytics and for the day someone runs deleteMany({}). The delayed member is your last un-replicated copy, and it is the only thing between Thali and a very bad afternoon.

Shard only when a replica set genuinely cannot hold the working set or absorb the write rate. Shard the collections that need it and leave the rest unsharded on one shard; 8.0's moveCollection can move them later. Use a config shard for small clusters, and dedicated config servers when isolation matters.

13.3 What does Priya put in the Spring Boot service?

Spring Data MongoDB, for the learner's stack. Four settings matter, and they all live in one line of configuration.

spring:
  data:
    mongodb:
      uri: mongodb://m1,m2,m3/thali?replicaSet=rs0&w=majority&readConcernLevel=majority&retryWrites=true&readPreference=primary

w=majority is first because it is the one from section 4. Do not rely on the implicit default: a topology change, such as somebody adding an arbiter, must not be allowed to change your durability.

readConcernLevel=majority matters because the default is local everywhere, including 8.3. If you read from secondaries, add a causally consistent ClientSession for the read-after-write paths.

retryWrites=true is already the driver default. State it anyway, so that nobody "optimises" it away in a review.

For transactions, register a MongoTransactionManager and use @Transactional. Then set the TransactionOptions on the manager explicitly: read concern snapshot, write concern majority. A transaction does not inherit the database-level settings, and that is the exact trap Jepsen walked into in 2020. Keep the method short, make no HTTP calls inside it, and expect MongoTransactionException wrapping TransientTransactionError; the callback API's retry is yours to write in Spring.

For entities, @Document maps a class to a document and embedded objects to sub-documents. @DBRef maps to a reference — and @DBRef is the join you designed away. Prefer a plain id field with a second query, or a $lookup in an aggregation.

13.6 What is a change stream, and why is it the CDC you already have?

A change stream is a cursor over the oplog. You open it on a collection, a database, or the whole deployment, and it yields insert, update, replace and delete events, each carrying a resume token in its _id.

Two properties make it more than "tail the oplog". It emits only majority-committed changes. And it can be resumed after a disconnect, with resumeAfter, or after an invalidating event such as a collection drop, with startAfter — provided the oplog still holds the token's position.

Since 6.0 you can also ask for the document's pre-image and post-image.

This is your change-data-capture lab's log-based capture, with no external connector to run.

For a single-document write it is also your transactional-outbox. The order document is the event, atomically written and majority-durable, so the outbox row and the business row are the same row. The outbox pattern is still the answer when the event you must publish is not the document you changed.

13.7 When is MongoDB the wrong answer?

Four honest cases.

The data is genuinely relational: many-to-many, queried from every side, changed by transactions that touch five entities routinely. You will spend the document model's savings on transactions and $lookup.

You need serializable isolation, or cross-document constraints enforced by the database itself.

Your dominant access is analytical scans over columns. That is database-landscape's columnar answer, not this one.

You need cross-shard queries that are not on the shard key to be fast at scale. Nothing here makes that true.

14. Which ten common models feel right and are wrong?

In one breath: Ten sentences you will hear in a corridor. Every one of them is reasonable, and every one of them is contradicted by something you have already watched happen to Asha's order.

Each one below is the belief, why an intelligent person holds it, and the exact place it breaks. The replacement models are collected in one table at the end, so you can cover it and test yourself first.

14.1 "MongoDB acknowledged the write, so it is saved" — why not?

This feels right because every database's acknowledgement means that. It is the one assumption nobody thinks to state.

The counterexample is section 4, and it is ten lines long. The acknowledgement meant what the write concern asked for, and w: 1 asks for one machine.

14.2 "MongoDB loses data" — is the 2013 folklore still true?

This feels right because it was true, and the reports from that era are still linked in arguments today.

The counterexample is the free-play challenge in section 9. With majority writes on journaled voters you cannot produce a rollback, and the simulator's engine asserts it.

14.3 "w: "majority" means my read will see it" — why not?

Here the word does the damage. "Majority" sounds like "everyone", and "everyone has it" sounds like "anyone can show it to me".

Scenario 5 separates the two. The acknowledgement counted Meera's m1 and Ben's m2. Asha's refresh went to Arjun's m3.

14.4 Are read concern and read preference the same knob?

They both have "read" in the name and both mention secondaries, so people treat them as one dial with different labels.

Scenario 4 settles it: three read concerns, on the same member, with the same preference, gave three different answers.

14.5 ""majority" reads are the fresh ones" — are they?

The reasoning is that majority is stronger than local, and stronger ought to mean more current.

Look at scenario 4's second row again. The answer was durable and stale, which is not a contradiction — it is the definition.

14.6 "An arbiter is a free third vote" — what does it actually cost?

It costs nothing to run, which is exactly why people add one.

Scenario 6 is the bill. The implicit default silently becomes w: 1, and a double failure loses acknowledged writes.

14.7 Do transactions make MongoDB behave like PostgreSQL?

They are called transactions and they are labelled ACID, so the expectation is reasonable.

Then write skew commits cleanly. A transaction aborts if any outside writer touched its document first. It dies at 60 s. And its concerns must be set explicitly or the snapshot guarantee is void.

14.8 "Sharding scales every query" — which ones does it not?

More machines, more throughput. It is true of the queries that carry the shard key.

Section 11.8 is the rest: a query without the shard key runs on every shard, and gets slower as you add them.

14.9 Does "schemaless" mean there is no schema?

The server does not enforce one by default, so it is easy to believe there is none to enforce.

The first document that stores price as a string breaks the aggregation that sums it. The schema was always there; only the enforcement was missing.

14.10 Is the oplog a log like Kafka's?

Both are ordered, replicated, and consumed by tailing. The word "log" carries the wrong promises across.

The oplog is capped and idempotent. Its entries are the result of an operation, not the operation. And it is truncated from the head at the size limit regardless of whether anyone has read it.

What is this commonly confused with?

Five pairs. If you can say each difference in one sentence, the lab has landed.

  • Write concern vs read concern vs read preference — survival of a write; what a read may return; which member answers.
  • w: "majority" vs a quorum read — the first is a consensus majority, with an order and a commit point. The second, in quorums-and-replication, is a set overlap with no order at all. MongoDB has only the first.
  • Rollback (replica set) vs abort (transaction) — the first discards a former primary's unreplicated tail after a failover. The second discards a transaction's buffered writes before they were ever visible.
  • Chunk/range vs shard — a range is the unit of movement; a shard is a replica set that holds many ranges.
  • Cluster time vs oplog ts — one hybrid logical clock; the oplog timestamp is its physical component.

How faithful is the simulator?

It has one client and one session. It replicates in whole heartbeats. And its primary's step-down timer fires one heartbeat after the secondaries' timers, so that the stale-primary window is visible for a full tick.

Real timers are randomised. Real replication is continuous. And a real primary steps down as soon as its own electionTimeoutMillis passes without a majority.

So the ordering of events is the lesson. The exact tick counts are the model's own, and quoting them at a colleague as MongoDB's numbers would be wrong.

Where does this lab sit among your other labs?

  • R + W > N overlap versus a consensus majority — quorums-and-replication. MongoDB uses both words "majority", and you need the distinction in section 7.
  • The stale-primary window and why "at most one leader" is unachievable — split-brain. Scenario 4 of the simulator is that window, with the three MongoDB read concerns run against it. linearizability's read write-back becomes MongoDB's "linearizable" read concern.
  • Read-your-writes, monotonic reads, afterClusterTime, and the hybrid logical clock — causal-consistency and hybrid-logical-clocks. MongoDB's cluster time is an HLC; the SIGMOD 2019 paper that describes it uses those words. Section 10.5 shows the session mechanism.
  • B-trees, WAL-before-ack, checkpoints, MVCC snapshots — btree-and-lsm and mvcc-and-vacuum. WiredTiger is a B-tree engine with MVCC; section 10.1 states only what differs.
  • Snapshot isolation, write skew, first-committer-wins — isolation-levels. MongoDB transactions are snapshot isolated, not serializable, and section 10.6 says exactly what that buys and what it does not.
  • Partitions as the unit of ownership, handover protocols, MOVED/ASK, hot partitions from monotonic keys — partitioning-and-rebalancing and consistent-hashing. Sharding, section 10.7, is that lab with MongoDB's names: shard key, range, mongos, balancer, moveRange.

  • "Proceed and detect" versus "coordinate and prevent" — cockroachdb and optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking. WiredTiger is on the optimistic side, and that decides how MongoDB transactions fail.

MongoDB — Solutions

Worked answers. For design questions the reasoning is the answer; a different conclusion with the same reasoning is fine.

Level 1 — Explain

1.1 The database has three copies of the order book, and only one of them takes new orders. When we placed the order we asked that one copy "have you written it?" and it said yes — but a moment later it was cut off from the other two, they carried on without it, and when it came back it had to throw away everything the others never saw. We should have asked "have at least two of you written it?", which is a question that cannot later become false.

1.2 Atomicity (one document changes all-or-nothing; crossing documents needs a transaction that is priced to be rare). Storage locality (one contiguous value; the 16 MiB cap and the cost of rewriting a large document on every update). Replication (one oplog entry; a transaction becomes several entries, each still under 16 MB). Distribution (a shard key inside the document places the whole document; queries without that key scatter, and the key must be chosen for cardinality, frequency and non-monotonicity).

1.3 w: 1: acknowledged above the line (on one member only). w: "majority": acknowledged only once the entry is at or below the line. "local": may return entries above the line. "majority": returns only entries at or below the member's known line. "linearizable": returns only entries at or below the line and confirms, by a majority round trip, that the line it knows is the current one. Causal session: returns only after the member's applied position has reached the client's own last operation, which may be above or below the line (hence the majority/majority requirement for the guarantee).

1.4 Raft's Figure 8 rule: a leader may only advance the commit index over an entry from its own term; earlier-term entries are committed indirectly once a current-term entry above them is on a majority. Without the no-op, a new primary with no client writes could never advance the commit point past the old term's entries, so majority writes from the previous term would stay unacknowledged forever and "majority" reads would stay frozen even though a majority holds the data.

Level 2 — Identify

2.1 m2. Proof on screen: its term is higher (2 versus m1's 1), and its card shows it can see a majority (it and m3 voted); m1's narration says "no majority seen for N ticks". A third fact: m2's oplog has the no-op entry in term 2, which m1 does not.

2.2 The rule "the commit point … only advances over entries from its own term". The entry that lets it advance is m1's no-op ts …:1 t3; once m3 reports having pulled it, the term-3 commit point covers both the no-op and, indirectly, ord-2 from term 2.

2.3 The balancer on the config server primary decides; the source shard performs the copy (the destination pulls documents and the delta); the mongos routers learn last, when a shard tells them their cached routing table is stale. Config primary gone: metadata read-only, no migrations, reads and writes continue. Source shard gone mid-migration: the migration aborts; the metadata was not updated, so nothing is lost, and the destination's partial copy is discarded. mongos gone: the application's driver fails over to another mongos; a mongos holds no state.

2.4 w=majority. Without it the driver uses the server's implicit default, which becomes w: 1 the moment the set contains an arbiter and the number of data-bearing voters is not greater than the voting majority (P-S-A: 2 ≤ 2). With it, writes on a P-S-A set with one data member down would block (then time out) rather than silently downgrade — which is the behaviour you want to notice.

Level 3 — Predict

3.1 Acknowledged at tick 2: m2 pulls at tick 1, reports at tick 2, commit point covers the write, ack. At tick 2 m3 has nothing. (a) NOT FOUND — legal, m3 is a healthy secondary that is behind. (b) The read waits; it completes at tick 4 when m3 pulls the entry (written tick 0 + 3 ticks lag + 1 hop). Scenario 5 shows both.

3.2 A clean crash: the write is lost, not rolled back — m1 is down, so no one can pull from it, and the new primary (m2 or m3, same optime) does not have it. When m1 restarts it will roll the entry back into a file. If instead m1 is partitioned but up, the answer is still lost: catch-up (§10.3) would preserve the write only if the new primary could reach m1 during catch-up, and a partition prevents exactly that. Catch-up saves w: 1 writes when the old primary steps down voluntarily or is slow but remains reachable — a rolling restart, not a crash or partition.

3.3 The primary cannot confirm a majority; the read blocks and fails when maxTimeMS expires — about 10 s later, an error, no data. A "majority" read would have returned immediately from the primary's frozen commit point: durable data that may already be superseded on the new primary.

3.4 Targeted: find({customerId}), the range query on customerId (a contiguous key range maps to a known set of ranges, possibly on more than one shard but not all), and deleteOne({_id}) (allowed because _id is accepted for single-document deletes — but it is broadcast, since _id is not the shard key and mongos cannot know which shard holds it). Broadcast: find({courierId}), updateMany({status}). Rejected: updateOne({courierId}) — a single-document update must carry the shard key or _id.

3.5 The kitchen's non-transactional updateOne succeeds immediately (the transaction had only read the document; it held no write lock). The transaction's later update finds the document changed since its snapshot and aborts with WriteConflict, labelled TransientTransactionError. The callback API retries the whole transaction, which now reads the decremented value. Nothing was lost; the transaction simply lost the race.

Level 4 — Modify

4.1 Better: §11.12's duration — failover takes ~4 s instead of ~12 s, so the window with no writes shrinks. Worse: §11.12's frequency — a 2 s GC pause or disk stall now triggers an election, each of which rolls back any w: 1 tail and costs a term. Also worse: the stale-primary window becomes relatively larger for clients with slow server discovery. The right value is bounded below by your worst normal pause (GC, fsync stalls, cross-zone latency spikes) with margin, and above by the outage you can tolerate.

4.2 The guarantee that a majority-acknowledged write is never rolled back — but only under a majority crash-and-restart: the members acknowledged from memory, then lost power before the 100 ms journal sync. A partition or a single crash still cannot roll it back, because the surviving majority holds it. The "linearizable" page mentions it because a linearizable read's durability claim rests on the same journal: with the setting false, "documents returned by the read operation" are not durable across a majority restart.

4.3 Voting majority is 3, data-bearing voters 5, so the calculated majority is 3. Two members can be lost. Latency is governed by the third-fastest member's pull + journal sync + position report — the two slowest members never gate the ack, which is why a lagging fifth member is harmless to writes (and dangerous only to reads that land on it).

4.4 Monotonicity. Every insert lands in the range ending at MaxKey; one shard takes the whole write load; the balancer chases the hot range around. Fix that keeps the report targeted: a compound key {placedAt: 1, customerId: 1} still routes inserts to the tail — so no; the honest answer is that a ranged key on time cannot both spread writes and target time ranges. Fixes: (a) hashed on customerId (or {customerId: 1} ranged) for the OLTP path and feed the nightly report from a change stream into a separate reporting store (targeted report, via cqrs); (b) {placedAt: "hashed"} — spreads writes, but the report scatters to every shard, which for a nightly batch is acceptable.

4.5 Original {restaurantId, status, placedAt}: equality, equality, sort — the index walk yields placedAt order, no blocking sort. Modified {restaurantId, placedAt, status}: after the equality on restaurantId, the index is in placedAt order, so the sort is free, but status is now a filter applied while scanning every order of the restaurant, examining far more keys than needed; worse than the original. With status: {$ne: "delivered"} the predicate becomes a range, so ESR says it belongs after the sort field — and the modified index {restaurantId, placedAt, status} is now the correct shape, while the original would force either a blocking sort or a range scan on status that breaks the sort order.

Level 5 — Trace

5.1 Tick 8, partition heals: m1 = [ord-1 100:1 t1, ord-2 104:1 t1], SECONDARY t1; m2 = [ord-1, noop 114:1 t2], PRIMARY t2, commit point 100:1 t1 (the no-op is not yet on a majority as far as m2 knows); m3 = [ord-1, noop]. Tick 9: m1 syncs from m2; common point is index 0 (100:1 t1); m1's extra entry 104:1 t1 goes to the rollback file; m1 rebuilds to {ord-1}; state ROLLBACK. Same tick, m3's reported position now includes the no-op, so m2's commit point advances to 114:1 t2. Tick 10: m1 pulls the no-op; m1 = [ord-1, noop], SECONDARY t2, rollback file [ord-2]. Tick 11: m1 learns the commit point 114:1 t2 via heartbeat. Matches the simulator's narration.

5.2 Tick 4, m2 restarts as SECONDARY with an empty oplog and syncs from m1, which is still PRIMARY (it has the arbiter's vote). Tick 5: m2 pulls ord-1 and ord-2 (both written at tick 1). Tick 6: m2 reports; the calculated majority (both data members) now holds both entries; m1's commit point advances to 102:2 t1, and the pending w: "majority" write of ord-2 — if it had not already timed out at tick 6 (5 ticks after issue at tick 1) — is acknowledged; in fact wtimeout and the commit race at the same tick, and in the simulator the request loop runs after the commit-point step, so it is acknowledged. Tick 7: m1 crashes; m2 has both entries; after the election timeout m2 becomes primary with the arbiter's vote. Both writes survive. The lesson: the arbiter did nothing; the second data member being up did everything.

5.3 Scenario 7: the first attempt found no primary and was never applied anywhere. The new primary's transaction table has no entry for (s1, txn); it applies the write fresh and records the result. Scenario 3: the first attempt was applied on m2 and replicated to m1 and m3 along with the oplog entry carrying (s1, 2); when m1 becomes primary its table already has (s1, 2) → it returns the stored result and applies nothing. In both cases the client's second attempt returns success once, and the collection holds exactly one ord-2.

5.4 Steps: (1) balancer sends moveRange to B; (2) B starts, writes still go to B; (3) C builds missing indexes; (4) C copies documents in the range — ord-9 is inserted on B during the copy; (5) C pulls the delta, which includes ord-9; (6) B enters the critical section (brief write block on the range), updates the config metadata: the range now belongs to C; mongos routers learn on their next stale-version error; (7) B deletes its copy once no cursor is open, asynchronously. A "local" read on B stops returning ord-9 at step 6 — ownership moved, and "local" filters documents B no longer owns. An "available" read keeps returning it until step 7 actually deletes it: an orphan.

Level 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 Explicit w: "majority" on P-S-A is safer but has a different failure: with the one secondary down, every write blocks (or times out) because the calculated majority is both data members. The design has traded silent data loss for a full write outage on a single failure — which is not what "we saved a machine" promised. Violated: availability under one failure, the thing the arbiter was bought for.

6.2 "majority" on a secondary returns that secondary's known commit point, which lags the primary's by its replication delay plus a heartbeat. A client that writes with w: "majority" and immediately reads with secondaryPreferred + "majority" can land on a secondary whose known commit point predates the write → NOT FOUND. Rollback-safety is not read-your-writes. Violated: read-your-writes; fix: a causal session (with majority/majority) or primary for that path.

6.3 The transaction does not inherit the database-level default only in the sense that Jepsen found (pre-5.0 defaults); in 5.0+ the implicit default is majority, so this usually works — until the set gains an arbiter (P-S-A → implicit w: 1), or someone runs setDefaultRWConcern to w: 1 for a bulk load. Then "snapshot read concern provides no guarantee that read operations in the transaction used a snapshot of majority-committed data" and the transaction can be rolled back on failover. Violated: snapshot isolation and durability, silently, by a configuration change elsewhere. Set both on the transaction.

6.4 updateMany is a multi-document write and is not retryable, regardless of upsert. Scenario: the first attempt applies (inserts the order) and the ack is lost; the driver does not retry (no (lsid, txnNumber) dedup for multi writes); the application's own retry loop runs the upsert again — which, being an upsert keyed on ref, happens to be idempotent this time. The flaw is the reasoning, and it fails the moment the write is not an upsert on a unique key: use updateOne with upsert: true (retryable) and a unique index on ref.

6.5 The oplog window is the time between the oldest and newest entries at the current write rate, and the cap is by size: 50 GB at a "modest" rate may be days — or, during a bulk import or an index rebuild on the primary (which writes large oplog entries), hours. If Monday arrives with the secondary's last optime older than the primary's oldest entry, the secondary needs an initial sync. Violated: the assumption that size equals time; fix: oplogMinRetentionHours set to longer than the weekend, and monitor the window.

6.6 Two flaws. Duplicates: the consumer processes an event, then crashes before storing the token; on restart it resumes from the previous token and processes the event again — the store-after-process order makes at-least-once, not exactly-once; the consumer must be idempotent (idempotent-consumer). Loss: if the consumer is down longer than the oplog window, the resume token points at a position the oplog no longer holds and resumeAfter fails; the consumer must detect that and re-snapshot. Violated: exactly-once (never promised) and resumability (promised only within the window).

Level 7 — Handle failure

7.1 First classify by what happened on the winning side since the rollback: for each rolled-back document, look up its current state on the primary. (a) Absent on the primary and no later write refers to it (e.g. an order that no service ever saw): safe to re-insert, with w: "majority", as a new write with a note in an audit field. (b) Present on the primary with a newer state (the customer re-placed the order, or the status moved on): do not re-apply; reconcile by hand or discard. (c) Rolled-back deletes and drops are not in the files at all — list those from the oplog gap instead. Re-apply through the application's normal write path so validation and change streams see it, never by loading BSON directly. Then fix the cause: the write concern that let a rollback contain acknowledged data.

7.2 A w: "majority" writer waits ~40 s per write (or hits wtimeout). A "majority" reader on the primary sees data 40 s old — durable, stale. A "local" reader sees the latest, which is 40 s of unacknowledged, rollback-eligible writes. A transaction reads a majority snapshot 40 s old and, on commit with majority concern, waits like the writer; meanwhile its pinned snapshot adds cache pressure. Flow control is throttling the primary's write admission (tickets) to pull the lag back toward flowControlTargetLagSeconds, which shows up as rising write latency for everyone — the deliberate cure.

7.3 Works: reads and writes to data already placed (routers use their cached routing table). Does not: migrations and splits, any DDL (which in 8.3 goes through mongos to the config servers), adding or draining shards, creating sharded collections, and — after routers restart or their cache expires — routing at all. First symptom: DDL or sh.status() hangs, or a mongos restart fails to load the routing table.

7.4 Since 6.1, a retry that fails when "both the first and second attempt … fail without a single write being performed" carries NoWritesPerformed. May conclude: the write did not happen, so it is safe to retry at the application level or to report failure without reconciliation. May not conclude: anything about why (the primary may still be unavailable) or that a third attempt will succeed. Without the label, a retryable write's failure means "unknown", which forces reconciliation.

Level 8 — Design

8.1 Topology: five voters across three zones (2 + 2 + 1) so a zone loss keeps a majority and one more member can be lost; or three (1 + 1 + 1) if cost forbids. Writes: w: "majority", j implied — the acknowledgement must be a fact. Reads for the customer's page: primary with "majority", or secondaryPreferred + causal session with majority reads inside the same session as the write, which is the only combination with all four guarantees. Latency: the calculated majority is 3 of 5, so the ack waits for the second-fastest other member; with two members in the same zone as the primary that is intra-zone (< 1 ms) + journal sync, comfortably under 15 ms; with three zones and a 1+1+1 layout it is the 4 ms hop plus journal. If the target still fails: relax reads first (accept secondary + maxStalenessSeconds for non-payment pages), never the write concern; relaxing w reintroduces §4 on a ledger. Monitor majority-commit lag and journal sync latency as the two components of the ack.

8.2 Shard key {customerId: 1} (ranged, high cardinality, non-monotonic; targets the dominant history query and single-order updates by customerId+_id). Scatter: kitchen screen (restaurantId), courier queue (courierId), nightly report (placedAt). Read side: a kitchen_queue collection sharded on {restaurantId: 1} and a courier_queue sharded on {courierId: 1}, both fed by a change stream on orders (majority-committed events, resumable, with fullDocument) — the cqrs projection; the nightly report goes to the analytics store via the same stream. If wrong: reshardCollection (5.0) in the background, or refineCollectionShardKey if only a suffix is needed; both are online but heavy, so pick with analyzeShardKey on sampled queries first.

8.3 (a) Transaction across orders and inventory: atomic and snapshot isolated; fails under contention on the hot inventory document (write conflicts, 60 s cap, 5 ms lock wait); operator sees totalAborted rising; commit costs a majority round trip even for a w: 1 request. (b) Single-document: put the reservation inside the inventory document (reservations: [{orderId, qty}], bounded per SKU per hour by design) using one conditional findOneAndUpdate (stock >= qty), then create the order; the order creation is a separate write, so "reserved but no order" is possible and needs a TTL sweep — atomic per document, isolated by the storage engine, fails only as a clean "insufficient stock" verdict. (c) Saga: reserve, place, confirm, with compensations; no isolation between steps, eventual consistency, operator sees stuck sagas in a state table. Recommend (b) for Thali: the contention profile is "many orders, few SKUs", where (a) aborts constantly and (c) is machinery for a two-step problem; keep (a) for the rare multi-SKU basket where all-or-nothing matters more than throughput.

8.4 electionTimeoutMillis 10 000 → ~3 000 (elections start sooner) and consider catchUpTimeoutMillis from −1 to a small bound (a new primary stops waiting to preserve w: 1 tails). Changes: §11.12 elections become more frequent for the same pause profile; §11.1 rollbacks of w: 1 writes become more likely (shorter catch-up, more elections); §11.4's stale-primary window shrinks in absolute time; §11.2's blocked majority writes resolve sooner after a partition. Monitor before shipping: term-change rate, NotWritablePrimary error rate in the application, count of members entering ROLLBACK, GC and fsync pause distributions on every member (the timeout must clear their p99.9), and p99 write latency across the change. Ship with w: "majority" everywhere first — then the extra elections cost time, not data.
