Lab 61 · work-pressure-and-deadlines

Playbook — what to actually say

Paste-ready wording for the twelve situations this subject produces. Adjust the tone to your workplace; keep the structure, because the structure is what does the work: facts first, then the forecast with its probability, then a decision that belongs to them, never a problem without options.

The reasoning behind each one is in README.md §15.

1 · The date has just landed on you (same day)

Thanks — before I say yes to the 30th, let me put a proper forecast together so we are both working from the same numbers. I'll come back tomorrow with what fits by then, what doesn't, and a couple of options. If the 30th is fixed for a reason I should know about, tell me now and I'll build the options around it instead.

Why this works: you neither accepted nor refused. You bought a day, and you asked the §15.1 question — is this date real? — without making it a challenge.

2 · Coming back with the forecast (the next day)

Here's where we are on the 30th.

The work: 60 items. Our actual throughput over the last 10 weeks: 5 items a week on average, between 2 and 8.

The forecast: replaying our own history, 60 items lands in week 12 half the time, and week 14 is the date I'd hold 85% of the time. The 30th is week 10 — that comes true about 4 times in 100.

What closes the gap: 20 items out of the 60. At 40 items, week 10 goes to a 97% bet. I've marked the 20 I would cut and what each one costs us — happy to go through them, and happy to be overruled on which ones.

Three options:

  1. Full 60 items → week 14 with 85% confidence.
  2. 40 items on the 30th → week 10 with 97% confidence, the other 20 follow in November.
  3. All 60 by the 30th → possible, roughly a 1-in-25 chance, and everything that goes wrong lands in production.

My recommendation is 2. What would you like to do?

Why this works: every number is a fact about the past or a stated probability, and the decision is explicitly theirs. Option 3 is real, not a straw man — offering it is what makes 1 and 2 credible.

3 · The weekly forecast (send this every week, same day, always)

Payments release — week 6 update

  • Finished this week: 6 items (39 of 60 done).
  • Throughput: 5.1/week over the last 6 weeks.
  • Forecast: week 13, 85% confidence (was week 14 last week).
  • Changed this week: the reconciliation work came in smaller than expected; the two support escalations cost us about a day.

Four lines. Ten minutes. Send it whether or not it moved.

Why this works: it makes the week-3 conversation routine and the week-9 crisis impossible. By the time a number moves, it is the sixth in a series and nobody can be surprised by it.

4 · New work arrives mid-project

Happy to take this on. It's about 4 days, which moves the forecast from week 12 to week 13. Do you want it in at that cost, or is there something in the current list you'd rather drop for it?

Use it for small things too. Scope creep is entirely made of items that were individually too small to mention.

5 · Someone asks you to "just work weekends"

A hard push for the last two weeks before the date is something I'd support, and it would buy us real time. What it won't do is close a six-week gap — when we tried that on the last release we shipped a week later than the un-crunched estimate and spent the following month on the bugs we made. If the 30th genuinely can't move, the lever that works is scope, and I've got the list ready.

Why this works: you concede the true part first. Crunch really does work for short pushes, and denying that costs you the argument with anyone who has seen it work.

6 · You are told to do it anyway

Understood — we'll go for the 30th and I'll push for it.

So it's on the record and nobody is surprised later: my forecast is week 14 at 85%, the biggest risk is the third-party integration, and I'll keep sending the weekly update and flag it the moment it moves. If it does move, the fastest lever will still be dropping items, so it's worth knowing now which ones you'd sacrifice.

Say it once. Then execute properly and stop relitigating. The engineer who says "I told you so" in November was never going to be believed in September either.

7 · Confirming a verbal decision in writing

Summarising where we landed so we're all working from the same picture:

  • We're keeping the 30th and holding all 60 items.
  • Forecast is week 14 at 85% confidence; the 30th is week 10.
  • If it slips, first lever is dropping items from the "nice to have" list.
  • I'll send the forecast every Thursday.

Shout if I've got any of that wrong.

The test: the person you are summarising would be comfortable forwarding this. If it would embarrass them, rewrite it. You are building shared memory, not a case.

8 · Escalating past your manager (rare, and do it carefully)

Only when: the decision genuinely sits above them, you have already raised it with them, and you tell them you are doing it.

To your manager first:

I don't think we can close this at our level — the only lever left is scope and that needs Priya's sign-off. Can we take it to her together this week? If it's easier I'll write it up and you can send it.

Why "together" — an escalation that routes around someone converts the person best placed to help you into an obstacle.

9 · Asking for the scope decision from someone senior

We have a genuine conflict between the date and the list, and it needs your call because both sides of it are legitimate.

The date is week 10. Our own throughput puts all 60 items at week 14, 85% confidence. 40 items lands on week 10 at 97%.

I'm not asking to move the date. I'm asking which 20 items come out — I've drafted a proposal, but you own the trade-off between them.

Why this works: senior people say yes to decisions and no to problems. You have handed them a decision that only they can make, with the analysis already done.

10 · Your own load, to your manager

I want to flag something before it becomes a problem rather than after. The last two months have run at about 55 hours a week for me, and the last three weekends. That's not sustainable and my work is getting worse, not better — I made two mistakes last week I wouldn't normally make.

I'm not asking to drop anything today. I want us to agree what comes off my plate once the release lands, and to plan the next one at a rate I can hold.

Why this works: specific, measured, forward-looking, and it asks for a plan rather than sympathy. "I'm stressed" is easy to sympathise with and forget; "55 hours and two mistakes" is a fact that needs an answer.

11 · When it is chronic (the 1:1 that matters)

Something I want to name because it's now a pattern rather than one bad quarter: the last four releases have all been committed before we forecast them, and all four have run over. That costs us more than the dates — it's why we're carrying the bug backlog we are.

I'd like to try one thing for one quarter: no date goes out until we've put a forecast against it, and I send that forecast weekly. If it doesn't help, we drop it. Would you back that?

Why this works: it is a proposal with a scope, an owner, a duration and an exit, which is a completely different object from a complaint.

12 · After a crunch, protecting the recovery

Now the release is out, I want to flag what the next two weeks realistically look like. The bugs we made under pressure will surface roughly now — that's the normal pattern — so I've planned for reduced feature work while we absorb them. Committing the team at full rate straight away is how the next release starts behind.

Why this works: it prices the recovery as project work, which it is, rather than as time off, which is how it gets cut.

The four things never to do

  1. Silent slipping. Hoping through weeks 3 to 8 costs every cheap option you had. The whole cost of a slip is in when it is announced.
  2. Padding estimates secretly. It gets found out, it destroys the only currency you have, and it makes real forecasts unbelievable. Pad explicitly, as a confidence level: "week 14 at 85%".
  3. Martyrdom. Quietly absorbing an impossible plan teaches the organisation that the plan was fine. The next one will be worse, and you will have taught them that.
  4. "I told you so." Being right in November buys nothing. Being on record in September, once, calmly, buys everything.
