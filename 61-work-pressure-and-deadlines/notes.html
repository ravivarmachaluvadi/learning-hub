BEGINNER MODEL — sections 1 to 9

1. Why is a missed date so expensive?

In one breath: Somebody writes your honest guess on a slide, and from that moment it is a promise other people have made plans around. Almost none of the damage is the late date itself.

Asha leads five engineers: Meera, Arjun, Ben, Dev and herself. Priya is the director who owns the payments release. Priya asks Asha how long the work will take. Asha thinks about it carefully and says six weeks.

Priya writes "six weeks" on a slide. Marketing books a launch around it. Sales tells a customer. Nobody has lied and nobody has been careless. But six weeks has quietly stopped being a guess. It is now a fact that other people are standing on.

Then the work turns out to be harder than it looked. There is now a gap between a real project and a public number. That gap is where the damage happens, and this is the part worth being precise about.

Read the third column of this table first. It is the part everybody forgets.

What breaks How it looks from inside What it costs
the product the same features ship, with more defects in them a team on 60-hour weeks left 2.7× more bugs in the code for users to find than the same team on 40-hour weeks
the date, quietly nobody announces a slip in week 3 the announcement comes in week 9, after six weeks of hoping, when no cheap option is left

Here is the sentence this whole lab exists to earn. It is not a slogan. Every number in the eighteen sections after it is there to hold this sentence up.

In every simulation run in this lab, the project missed its date, and in none of them did anyone slack off. Missing the date was not a behaviour problem. It was the plan meeting the world.

You cannot fix a plan by trying harder inside it. You fix it by changing what is in it. To do that, you have to be able to show what the plan implies, rather than assert it. That is the whole job of this document.

Most writing on this subject is advice: communicate early, push back, set boundaries. The advice is correct. It is also useless in the room, because the person across the table has advice too, and theirs is just get it done. Advice loses to advice. Arithmetic does not.

So the middle of this lab builds a small, honest simulation of a team under a deadline. It then runs the moves people actually make: work weekends, add engineers, start everything at once, cut scope. For each move it reports what happens to the ship date. The last part covers the human half: what to say, to whom, in what order, and what to do when the answer is still "do it anyway".

If you are in the middle of a bad week right now and want exactly one thing, read section 6 and then open PLAYBOOK.md, item 2.

2. What does a doomed evening look like from inside the kitchen?

In one breath: Five cooks, forty tables, and a manager whose arithmetic was perfectly reasonable. By 8pm nobody is idle, everybody is faster than they were an hour ago, and the tickets keep getting older.

Forget software for a moment. Meera cooked in a restaurant kitchen for three years before she wrote any software, and this is the story she tells Asha's team whenever a date arrives.

It is Saturday night. The kitchen has five cooks. Tickets come in from the dining room. Each dish takes a cook a few minutes. The manager looks at the evening's bookings and does some arithmetic. Forty tables, five cooks, we can do this. He promises every table their food within twenty minutes.

By 8pm the pass is covered in half-finished plates. Nobody is idle. Nobody is slow. Everyone is moving faster than they were at 7pm. And the tickets are getting older.

What did the manager's arithmetic leave out? Four things, and Meera can name them without thinking, because she lived all four.

A cook does not only cook. She answers the phone, shows the new kid where the stock is, and steps away when a customer sends something back.

Some dishes take three times as long as expected, and nobody knows which ones until they start.

A plate that goes out wrong comes back forty minutes later. Worse, it comes back to the front of the queue, ahead of food nobody has started.

And when the manager shouts "faster", plates do go out faster — and more of them come back.

Every one of those four is a real mechanism in a software project. Every one of them is missing from the arithmetic that produced the promise. Hold on to this kitchen. Section 8 maps it onto Asha's project line by line, and section 10 puts a number on each of the four.

🔮 Predict: The manager sees the pass clogging up at 8pm and calls in two cooks from the other branch. They walk in at 8:30. Does the kitchen catch up faster?

Reveal

Not that night. The new cooks do not know where anything is, so for the first hour they are asking questions. Every question is asked to somebody who was cooking. The kitchen's output goes down first, then slowly up. By the time they are useful, the rush is over.

If you said yes, you counted two extra pairs of hands and stopped there. Two pairs of hands arrived; some of the existing hands stopped cooking to point at shelves. That trade is the whole result.

You already believe this about kitchens. Section 11 shows the identical result for engineers, with numbers: two engineers added in week 12 of this lab's project made it finish 0.9 weeks later. The same two people, hired in week 0, made it finish 3.0 weeks earlier. The hire was never the mistake. The timing was.

3. Where exactly does the promise come from?

In one breath: A capacity spreadsheet. It divides the work by the people and adds a bit for safety. It is the most reasonable thing in the world, and it is wrong by about a factor of two.

Watch the promise being made. This is the real shape of a real planning conversation, and you can run it yourself: java src/BrokenPlan.java.

  Scope        40 tasks x 3.2 days   = 128 engineer-days
  Capacity     5 engineers x 5 days  =  25 engineer-days/week
  Build        128 / 25              = 5.1 weeks
  Test & fix   fixed allowance       = 1.0 weeks
  Contingency  10%                   = 0.5 weeks
  ------------------------------------------------
  COMMITTED DATE                     = 7 weeks

One piece of vocabulary before we go on, because every argument about deadlines is conducted in this unit. When Asha says a task is "about three days", she means three days of one person's attention. One engineer's output for one working day is what engineers call an engineer-day. Five engineers for five days is therefore twenty-five engineer-days a week, and that is line 2 of the sheet.

Getting from "five engineers" to "twenty-five engineer-days a week" is the first place a plan goes wrong. Section 10, correction 2, takes that line apart.

Nobody in that room is stupid and nobody is lying. Asha's estimates were honest. Priya added a test phase and a contingency on top. Divide the work by the people, then add a bit for safety. Ask anybody outside software how to plan a project and you will get this sheet.

🔮 Predict: Asha's team runs this exact project. The sheet says seven weeks. Before you read section 4: how late are they, roughly? Ten per cent? Thirty? Double?

Reveal

They ship in week 15.7, which is 2.6× the six weeks the model treats as the promise. Most people guess twenty to thirty per cent, because that is what "a bit late" feels like, and because that is the size of the mistakes we can see. A factor of two feels like incompetence.

If you guessed 30%, you were pricing the surprises. The surprises are the small half of it. Four separate effects each cost between 10% and 30%, and they multiply rather than add. That is section 10's whole argument, and it is why nobody spots the error by looking harder at the sheet.

The sheet is not wrong because somebody was sloppy. It is wrong for reasons that have nothing to do with anybody's effort, and section 4 takes them one at a time.

5. If a single date is a bet you lose, what should you produce instead?

In one breath: Not a better date. Change nothing but luck and the same plan ships anywhere between week 12.6 and week 18.6. No single number can be right about that. A number can only be right about it with a probability attached.

Asha runs the identical plan twelve times. Same team, same forty tasks, same sheet. The only difference between runs is which tasks turn out nasty, and nobody can know that in advance.

Week 12.6 to week 18.6 — a six-week spread, with nothing changed but luck.

Sit with that for a moment. Nobody made a decision differently. Nobody worked harder in run 1000 than in run 1003. The spread is not a measure of the team. It is a property of the work.

So when Priya asks Asha for "the date", the honest answer is not a better single number. It is four things, and everything after this point in the lab exists to produce them.

The first is a range with probabilities on it, built from evidence rather than feelings. It sounds like this: "60 items left, this team's own last ten weeks, no new scope: week 12 is a coin flip, week 14 holds 85% of the time."

The second is a menu of decisions with their real costs, so that the date becomes somebody's choice rather than your failure. It sounds like this: "week 10 at 85% needs 20 of the 60 items gone. Here are the 20 I would cut."

The third is a way to keep the promise honest as the work reveals itself, updated weekly, so that nobody finds out in week 9.

The fourth is a sustainable version of you at the end of it, because you will be asked again next quarter, and the quarter after that.

6. What are the two sentences the whole lab reduces to?

In one breath: A date is a bet, not a fact, so say the odds out loud. And of the four things you can change, only one of them moves quickly and honestly.

Everything in this document collapses into two sentences. Learn these two and you have the lab, even if you never open the code.

1. A date is not a fact about the work; it is a bet about the work. Say the odds out loud, or somebody else will assume they are 100%.

2. There are only four levers — scope, time, people, quality — and under pressure exactly one of them moves quickly and honestly: scope.

The second sentence is the one that changes your Monday, so take the four levers one at a time.

Time is what Priya is telling Asha to change, and it is the one thing in the room that is already fixed. People arrive too slowly to help; section 11 gives the exact week after which a new hire makes the current date worse. Quality does not actually move. It only appears to move, and it gives the time back later with interest.

What is left is scope. That is why "what should we not build?" is the most senior question in any planning room, and the one engineers ask least often.

Both sentences are run, not asserted. Section 10 puts a number on each lever and section 11 shows each one failing in public.

What I can tell you instead is how often each week comes true, and what we would have to drop to make an earlier one likely."

7. Which words let you argue precisely?

In one breath: Vague words lose arguments. These are the load-bearing ones, earned in the story first and named afterwards, so that you can say exactly what you mean when it counts.

Every term below is defined again, in one line, in the glossary in section 18. Here it gets the story it came from.

What are the three different things people call "the date"?

Go back to the first page. Priya asked, Asha said six weeks, and Priya wrote it on a slide. Between those two moments the same six weeks changed category twice, and nobody noticed.

That silent upgrade is the most expensive confusion in the subject, so the three categories get three names.

Word What it really is Who it binds Where you met it
Estimate a guess about one piece of work, in isolation nobody Asha's "about three days" in section 3
Forecast a probability statement about a whole body of work, from evidence nobody, but it can be checked "week 14 holds 85%" in section 5
Commitment a promise to other people, who then act on it marketing, sales, the customer the slide in section 1

A forecast has an error bar by definition. A forecast without one is not a forecast; it is an estimate wearing a suit.

Naming the three lets Asha say the single most useful sentence in project work: "that was an estimate, not a commitment — do you want me to turn it into a forecast?" The journey is: "about three days" → "60 items left, P85 is week 14" → "we will ship the payments release on 12 October".

Why is one number never enough?

A point estimate is a single number: "six weeks". A distribution is the full set of outcomes with how often each one happens — the twelve runs in section 5, or the histogram in section 14. A percentile picks a point on that distribution. P50 is the coin flip, where half the outcomes land earlier. P85 is the date you make 85 times out of 100.

Why bother? Because a point estimate silently reports P50 at best, while everyone in the room hears P100. This lab's own run makes that concrete: a team's average velocity said week 12, and week 12 came true 53% of the time. P85 was week 14.

If you did the cap-theorem or distributed-rate-limiting labs, this is the same move as reporting p99 latency instead of an average. Nobody would accept "our average response time is fine" as a statement about the worst case. A date is a latency number.

What are the only four things that can change?

Scope is how much you build. Time is when it is due. People is how many work on it. Quality is how carefully.

Every deadline conversation you will ever have is about these four and nothing else. Fixing all four at once is not ambitious; it is arithmetic denial.

How do you talk about flow rather than effort?

Ben, on Asha's team, has forty branches open and cannot name one finished thing. Section 11 shows what that costs. To describe it you need four words, and one of them is a law.

Word Plain meaning Why it earns its own word
Throughput finished items per week it is counted, not guessed — a fact about the past
Cycle time how long one item takes start to finish, including all the waiting the waiting is usually most of it
WIP work in progress: how many items are open at once "started" and "finished" feel similar to a busy team and are worlds apart to a business
Little's Law cycle time equals WIP divided by throughput double what you have on the go and everything takes twice as long to come out

Little's Law is worth saying twice, because it is the least intuitive true thing in this lab. In story terms: if the pass has twice as many half-finished plates on it, each plate sits there twice as long, even though the cooks are working just as hard. In words: the time one item takes is the number of open items divided by how many finish per week. In symbols: cycle time = WIP / throughput.

Ben's forty open branches are not a personality flaw. They are a number in that formula. The same team with 40 tasks open at once had 1.4 features working by week 6; with 5 open it had 17.1.

Why does a fully allocated team move so slowly?

Utilization is the fraction of capacity that is committed. It is not a measure of how hard people work. It is a measure of how little slack the system has left.

Waiting time does not grow gently as utilization rises. It goes off a cliff, and the shape of that cliff is ρ / (1 − ρ), where ρ (rho) is just the utilization written as a fraction. Section 10 puts the numbers on it: at 80% busy an item takes 5× its own work time to come out, and at 95% busy it takes 19×.

This is the same mathematics as the queueing-theory lab, where one clerk at one desk produces exactly this curve. There the queue is made of people waiting for a passport. Here it is made of tasks waiting for an engineer. The arithmetic does not care which.

What is rework, and why does 5% of the budget hurt so much?

Rework is work you have to do again because the first version was wrong. A latent bug is a defect that exists but has not been found yet. An escaped defect is one you never found at all — production found it, which means a user found it.

Rework is the reason pressure does not remove work. It moves work. A bug Dev makes in week 4 is invisible until about week 7, and then it arrives as new work at the front of the queue. That is Meera's plate coming back to the pass, ahead of food nobody has started.

What is scope creep, and why is it never subtracted?

Scope creep is work added after the plan was made. Most of it is genuinely needed, so this is not a complaint about anybody's discipline. It is simply never subtracted from the date.

Section 14 prices it: 1.5 extra items a week moved a P50 from week 12 to week 18. In that forecast, creep was the single largest term — larger than everything else put together.

What does a new joiner actually cost?

Ramp-up time is how long a new person takes to reach full productivity, plus what they cost the people teaching them. In this model it is about six weeks to full speed, and a quarter of an experienced engineer's day, every day, while it happens.

That second half is the one that surprises people. Omar joining does not only mean Omar is slow for six weeks. It means Meera is slower too.

What is the engineer's picture? A queue that feeds itself

Said precisely, Asha's project is four things and one loop.

There is a queue of work items. There is a small number of servers, which is what the engineers are in this picture. Their capacity is reduced by interrupts, coordination and fatigue before any coding happens. Service times are drawn from a right-skewed distribution: a task can run very late but never very early, and nobody can see which is which in advance.

And then the part that makes it a machine rather than a pipe: a feedback loop, in which the rate of work determines the rate of future work.

10. What are the five things that eat a schedule?

In one breath: Start with the model in everybody's head, which is work divided by people, and let it break five times. Each break has its own number, and the five numbers multiply rather than add.

Model v1 — what if you just divide the work by the people?

40 tasks × 3.2 days = 128 engineer-days ÷ 5 engineers ÷ 5 days = 5.1 weeks

This is the model Priya is using, and it is not stupid. It is exactly right for work that is fully known, fully parallel, and done by people who do nothing else.

Almost no engineering work is any of those three things. Five corrections turn v1 into the real thing, and each correction is a separate mechanism with a separate price.

Correction 1 — why is an honest estimate still 28% short?

Task durations are right-skewed. A task cannot take less than zero time, but it can easily take four times as long as expected. Draw that shape and it has a long tail on the right and nothing on the left.

Now think about what happens in Asha's head when Priya asks. Asha pictures the typical case. The typical case is the median — the middle outcome, with half above and half below. But a schedule does not pay the median. A schedule pays the average, and for a right-skewed shape the average sits above the median, dragged up by the disasters.

The test suite checks this directly. Its tests are named after the claims they check, so summing_most_likely_estimates_understates_the_total is the claim that adding up everybody's most likely guesses gives a total that is too small.

java src/Tests.java
What the test reports Value What it means
median task duration 4.0d the length Asha pictures when she estimates
mean task duration 5.12d the length the schedule actually pays for
the same, from theory 5.11d the closed-form answer, agreeing with the run
the gap 28% undercount every honest estimate is short by this much on average

Every task is honestly estimated. Every task is 28% short, before anything goes wrong at all. Now add the ordinary human optimism on top. People picture the happy path, and this lab models that as a further 20%. Together the two effects leave the estimate covering roughly 63% of the real coding time.

A concrete case. Take one task Asha honestly estimated at 3.2 days. Eight times out of ten it lands between 2 and 6 days. One time in twenty it takes 14, because the API is undocumented, the staging environment is broken, and the "small refactor" reaches into nine files. Nobody estimates the 14-day version, and nobody should. But some task in the forty will be it, and the schedule has no room for it anywhere.

Correction 2 — why is capacity not people times hours?

The spreadsheet books 25 engineer-days a week from five engineers. Reality subtracts, in this order, before anybody writes a line of code.

Step Engineer-days left What is being taken out

The interrupt tax on the last row is the one people get wrong, and they get it wrong in one specific way.

🔮 Predict: Arjun's team hands 25% of its capacity to support and on-call. The project was going to take 12 weeks. How much later does it ship — 25% later?

Reveal

31% later, not 25%. Here is the run.

If you said 25%, you subtracted what was gone. The schedule divides by what is left. Giving a quarter of the team's time to support is not a 25% delay. It is a 33% delay. Twelve weeks of work done at 75% speed takes 12 ÷ 0.75 = 16 weeks, not 12 + 25%.

The bigger the tax, the wider the two answers get. At half your capacity the naive answer says 50% later, and the truth is double.

Under pressure a team goes faster by being less careful, and this genuinely works — for a while. That is the honest version, and the honest version is far more useful than the slogan, because anybody who has seen crunch work will stop listening the moment you deny it.

The catch is timing. A bug Dev makes in week 4 is invisible until about week 7. When it appears it is new work that jumps the queue ahead of features.

Correction 4 — why is starting not the same as finishing?

Here is the mechanism that is invisible in every capacity spreadsheet, because a spreadsheet has no idea how many things are open at once.

Correction 5 — why is the answer never a single line?

Even with the first four corrections applied, one thing remains, and it is the result from section 5. None of this is deterministic. The identical plan, run twelve times with nothing different but which tasks turned out hard, ships between week 12.6 and week 18.6.

So the output of all this arithmetic is not a date. It is a distribution. The only honest way to report a distribution is with a probability attached.

11. What are the seven ways this actually goes wrong?

In one breath: Seven named failures, each one a short story about somebody on Asha's team, each one with a command that shows it happening. Six of them are in the model. The seventh is the one you personally control.

F1 — Why is the heroic quarter always followed by a slow one?

Dev volunteers for 60-hour weeks. For two months it works, visibly. Then output falls below where it started.

Run it with java src/Experiments.java overtime, or open the visualization and set hours to 60. Escaped bugs go from 3 to 7. Total hours burnt rise by 57%.

Two loops are running at once. Tired hours are worth less than rested ones; this model floors an hour's value at 60% of a rested hour after sustained overtime. And rushed hours make more defects, which arrive as queue-jumping rework about three weeks later.

The work still gets done, and for short pushes crunch really is faster. What is lost is the sustainability of the pace, plus quality — permanently, because an escaped defect is by definition one that a user finds.

🔮 Predict: Asha's team crunches for eight weeks and ships. The next quarter's plan assumes the same rate. What has actually happened?

Reveal

Two things, and the second one is the career-relevant one.

Mechanically, a quarter's worth of rework and escaped defects now arrives during the quarter that was planned at full speed. The next project starts already behind.

Organisationally, and this is the part nobody warns you about, the crunch rate is now the baseline. Nobody who set the next plan saw the 60-hour weeks. They saw the output. This is the ratchet, and it is why "we did it last time" is the most dangerous sentence in the whole subject.

If you answered only "the team is tired", you named the smaller half. Tiredness recovers. A baseline does not, unless somebody moves it back on purpose. The only defence is to make the cost visible while it is being paid: hours worked reported alongside features delivered, in the same status update, every week.

F4 — Why is a team that carries the pager not the team on your plan?

This is the most under-priced line in every plan in the industry. If your team carries the pager, your usable capacity is not your headcount, and the fix is a number: "we are the on-call team; plan us at 3.5 engineers, not 5."

F5 — Why does the date move when nobody moved it?

Run java src/Experiments.java forecast. Sixty items are left and the team's own history says about five finish per week. With nothing added, the coin-flip date is week 12. With 1.5 items a week quietly added, the coin-flip date is week 18.

The arithmetic is unforgiving. Adding 1.5 against a throughput of 5 is a 30% tax, so the remaining time divides by 0.7. Creep does not add to the pile once; it lowers the rate at which the pile shrinks, permanently.

Usually the new items really are needed, so this is not a story about weak people saying yes. What is lost is the date, silently, because nobody logs an addition as a slip. This is the single largest term in most real forecasts and the easiest one to fix: every addition gets a date impact quoted at the moment it is added, in the same message. Not a refusal. A price tag.

"We're all fully allocated" is not a status report. It is a diagnosis. Fully allocated is precisely the state in which nothing moves.

12. What can you guarantee, and what can you not?

In one breath: You can guarantee a probability, a rate, a menu, a rhythm and your own conduct. You cannot guarantee a date, and you cannot guarantee that being right will win the argument.

This is the part to have straight before you walk into the room with Priya.

What can you actually promise?

  • A probability, from evidence. "Week 14 holds 85% of the time, based on this team's last ten weeks." That is a claim you can defend line by line.
  • A rate. Throughput is a fact about the past. Nobody can argue with it, which is exactly why it is the strongest thing you can bring into the room.
  • A menu. "Week 10 at 85% needs 20 of these 60 items gone. Here are the 20 I would cut, and what each one costs." That converts your problem into somebody else's decision, which is where it belonged all along.
  • A rhythm. The forecast updates every week, so nobody is ever surprised.
  • Your own conduct. You will say what you know when you know it, and then execute the decision that gets made.

What can you never promise?

A date at 100% is not available for work nobody has done before. Anybody promising it is promising the coin-flip date and hoping.

That effort closes an arithmetic gap is also not available. Sections 10 and 11 run that experiment repeatedly and it does not hold. Effort moves the cost, mostly into quality, and mostly into the future.

That being right will win the argument is the third. Sometimes the date is genuinely immovable and the answer is still "do it anyway". Section 15 covers that case, because it is common and it is survivable.

What did you trade away to get the guarantee?

Reporting a range instead of a date costs you something real, and it is worth naming so that it does not ambush you in the room.

A range sounds less confident. "Week 14" sounds like somebody in control. "Week 12 to 16, 85% by 14" sounds like hedging, to an audience not used to it.

The trade is worth making, but only if you make the range useful rather than merely honest. Two things do that. Always bring the decision that shortens it. And never report a range you would not defend to the day — a forecast that keeps sliding is worse than a date, because it teaches people that your numbers mean nothing.

Which four rules hold in every deadline conversation?

  1. Never let an estimate become a commitment silently. Name the upgrade out loud at the moment it happens.
  2. Never report a date without a probability, and never report a probability without the evidence under it.
  3. Never take work in without taking work out, or quoting the price. Both are fine. Silence is not.
  4. Never let a slip be discovered. It should be the fourth week in a row that the number has been in front of them.

Where exactly does the estimate go wrong?

One line, in Rand.java, and it is the whole of correction 1.

double lognormal(double median, double sigma) {
    return median * Math.exp(sigma * gauss());
}

In the story, this is "nobody knows which dish is nasty until they start it". In words: draw a duration whose typical value is the number Asha said, but which can run long and cannot run short. In symbols, the caller passes the median, which is the value a person pictures when estimating. What the schedule pays is the mean, median × exp(σ²/2). For a spread parameter σ of 0.7, that mean is 28% higher.

Nothing else in the model has to be pessimistic for the plan to be wrong.

Where does the capacity go?

In Project.java, the subtractions happen in this order, and the order is the argument.

double energy = ot > 0 ? Math.max(0.60, 1 - 0.03 * otWeeks * (ot / 20.0)) : 1.0;

Each line is one of section 10's corrections. Every line multiplies the line above it rather than subtracting from the original. That is why four "small" 10% effects are not a 40% problem.

Where does pressure turn into future work?

Also in Project.java. This is the feedback loop from section 8's diagram, in three lines.

// Pressure does not remove work, it moves work into the future.
double qualityMult = 1 + (ot / 20.0) * (1 + 0.08 * otWeeks);
defectAcc += spent * p.defectsPerEngineerDay * qualityMult;
...
int disc = day + (int) Math.round(p.discoveryDelayDays * (0.5 + qa.next()));

Two properties do all the work. Defects scale with both how much overtime there is and how long it has been going on — that second factor is the spiral. And defects are discovered weeks after they are made, which is why the burndown looks fine right up until it does not.

Why does rework hurt more than its size?

One argument to one method call, in Project.java.

// Rework preempts features: bugs jump the queue, which is exactly why a
// "code complete" project can sit at 95% done for a month.
backlog.add(0, new Task(nextTaskId++, d.reworkDays, true));

add(0, …) — the zero is the front of the queue. This is Meera's returned plate going ahead of food nobody has started. The 20.9 engineer-days of rework are only 5% of the budget, but they land on the critical path at the worst possible moment.

How does the lab compare two plans fairly?

Task durations are drawn from a stream seeded by the world seed alone. Two plans compared under the same seed therefore face an identical universe of work.

This is why the lab can say "the same project, with one decision changed" and mean it. Comparing two plans on different random draws would tell you about luck rather than about the decision. Engineers call this trick common random numbers.

What does the model deliberately not have?

A model's omissions are as load-bearing as its contents, so here they are by name.

There are no dependencies between tasks. Real critical paths are worse than this model, because variance compounds along a chain instead of averaging out. There is no people leaving, no morale, and no task that turns out to be impossible. There is no one senior engineer everything routes through. And there is no learning: a real team gets faster at its own codebase, and this one never does.

The overtime and ramp-up curves have the shape reported in published studies of sustained overtime. The exact constants are this model's own. Treat every number here as the direction and rough size of an effect, not as a prediction for your team.

  60 items left -> P85 = week 14, chance of week 10 =   4%
  50 items left -> P85 = week 12, chance of week 10 =  54%
  40 items left -> P85 = week  9, chance of week 10 =  97%

Twenty items out of sixty, and week 10 goes from a 4% bet to a 97% bet. That is the sentence Asha takes into the room. Not "we can't do it" — "here is the exact size of the gap, and here is what closes it."

15.1 — Which kind of deadline do you actually have?

Four completely different things get called "an unrealistic deadline". They need four different responses. Using the wrong response is how smart engineers lose these arguments while being entirely correct.

🔮 Predict: Priya's date is the last day of the quarter. Nobody can say what breaks if it moves. Which of the four kinds is that, and does bringing a forecast help?

Reveal

It is the anchor, and a forecast usually just moves it. This is the easy case, and it is far more common than engineers assume — which is why the habit of arguing rather than showing is so costly.

If you assumed it was manufactured urgency, you may be pattern-matching on a bad previous job. The test in the table below separates them in one question, and it takes about four seconds to ask.

Kind of deadline How to recognise it What actually works
The anchor The date came from a round number, a quarter boundary, or somebody's guess. Nobody can say what breaks if it moves. Evidence. A forecast usually just moves it. This is the easy case and it is more common than engineers assume.
The hard external date A regulation, a contract, a conference, a partner launch, a tax year. Real money or legal exposure attached. The date genuinely will not move. Do not argue about it. Go straight to scope: what is the smallest thing that is legally or contractually enough on that date?
Manufactured urgency Everything is urgent. There is always a new "critical" date. Last quarter's emergency shipped and nothing happened. Evidence will not work, because the date was never the point. This is a management-practice problem; see 15.7.
The real business constraint Runway, a funding round, a competitor, a customer who will leave. Costly but genuine. Full transparency in both directions. This is where a two-week push is legitimate, bounded and named as such.

One question separates all four: "What happens if this lands two weeks later?"

An anchor produces vagueness. A hard date produces a specific, checkable consequence. Manufactured urgency produces annoyance at the question — and that annoyance is itself the answer.

15.2 — What do you say on the day you are handed the date?

Do not accept, and do not refuse. Both are traps. Accepting makes the date yours; refusing makes you the obstacle. Convert it into a forecast instead, and buy a day or two to build one.

The move is one sentence: "Let me come back tomorrow with what fits and what doesn't."

Then bring three things. The forecast, with its probability. The gap, in units of work rather than in weeks. And a menu of options with dates attached. Never a problem without options; never options without dates.

Here is the framing that does the real work. You are not asking permission. You are handing back a decision that was always theirs. "I can't do it in six weeks" is a statement about you. "Six weeks fits 40 of these 60 items at 85% confidence; here are the 20 I would drop, or here is what the other dates look like" is a statement about the work.

15.3 — What is the one habit that prevents the crisis?

The single highest-value habit in this entire lab, and it is boring:

Send the same short forecast every week, whether or not it has changed.

Four lines, the same format, the same day, every week.

Finished this week:   6 items (60 -> 54 left)
Throughput, last 6w:  5.0 items/week
Forecast:             P50 week 12, P85 week 14  (promised: week 10)
Changed this week:    +2 items added for the fraud review

Why does it work? Because of the arithmetic in section 11, F5 and F7. A slip announced in week 3 is a conversation with cheap options: cutting scope in week 1 saves 5.1 weeks. A slip announced in week 9 is a crisis with no options left, because the same cut in week 12 saves 0.7 weeks.

The weekly note makes week 3 the natural moment to say it, because the number moving is routine rather than an alarm. You have been sending it since week 1.

It also quietly solves the credibility problem. When the number moves in week 6, it is the sixth in a series rather than the first thing anybody has heard. Nobody can say they were surprised, and nobody has to be blamed for surprising them.

15.4 — Which three sentences do most of the work?

When new work arrives mid-project, never refuse. Always price it.

"Happy to take it. It's about four days, which moves the forecast from week 12 to week 13. Do you want it in, or do you want something else out?"

That works because it is genuinely cooperative and completely non-negotiable at the same time. You did not say no. You made the arithmetic visible and handed the choice back. Do this every single time, including for small things. The whole damage of scope creep, which turned week 12 into week 18 in section 11's F5, is made of items that were each individually too small to mention.

When the answer is "just work harder", concede the true part first.

"We can push for two weeks before the date and that's real — it buys about X. As a way to close a six-week gap it doesn't work; here's what happened last time we tried it."

Crunch really is faster for short pushes, as section 10's correction 3 shows. Pretending otherwise costs you the argument with anybody who has watched it work.

When you are told to do it anyway, and you will be, say this once in writing. Then go and do the work properly.

"Understood — we'll go for the 30th and I'll push for it. So it's on the record: my forecast is week 14 at 85%, and the main risk is X. I'll send the forecast weekly and flag it the moment it moves."

That is not covering yourself. It is the disagree-and-commit that makes you somebody worth listening to next time. The engineer who says "I told you so" in November was never going to be believed in September either.

15.5 — Why write it down, and how do you do it without sounding adversarial?

Everything material goes in writing. But the reason matters, and it changes how you write it. You are not building a case. You are building a shared memory, because in week 9 nobody will remember week 3 the same way.

  • Write it as a summary for the group, not a note to file: "Summarising where we landed so we're all working from the same picture."
  • Give it four parts and no adjectives: facts, forecast, decision, owner. No "as I warned".
  • Send it to the person who made the decision. Copy the people affected by it.
  • If the reply comes verbally, summarise it in writing yourself: "Confirming what we agreed…".

Those four parts fit in a note this short:

Facts:     60 items left. We finished 5.0/week over the last six weeks.
Forecast:  P50 week 12, P85 week 14. Promised date is week 10.
Decision:  Week 10 at 85% needs 20 items out. My list is attached.
Owner:     Priya decides which 20; I'll re-run the forecast the same day.

There is one test for a good escalation note. The person you are escalating about would be comfortable forwarding it. If it would embarrass them, rewrite it.

15.6 — How do you handle the pressure itself?

Everything above is about the project. This part is about you, and it is the part engineers skip.

Separate the two things that feel identical. "This plan will not fit" is an arithmetic problem, and it is not yours to feel bad about. "I am not good enough" is what an arithmetic gap feels like from the inside. That is especially true in the WIP trap of section 11, F3, where Ben worked every day and finished nothing. Every simulation in this lab missed the date, and there was nobody in any of them to blame. When the feeling arrives, go and look at the forecast. It is usually the arithmetic, and the arithmetic is public.

Know the difference between a sprint and a lifestyle. Two weeks of long hours before a real date is a professional decision. A quarter of them is a system that has decided your time is free. The signal is not the hours. It is the recovery: a real sprint has a named end date and a slack week after it. If nobody has budgeted the recovery, it was never a sprint.

Watch the ratchet. Heroics become the baseline, as section 11, F1 shows. The defence is to make the cost visible while it is being paid rather than afterwards: hours reported next to features, every week, in the same note as the forecast. That is not complaining. It is the same principle as the rest of this lab — put the real number where people can see it.

Take the early symptoms seriously, because they show up before you notice them yourself. Sleep goes first. Work leaks into the last hour before bed. You dread Mondays specifically rather than generally. Cynicism arrives where you used to be curious. Small tasks start to feel enormous. Two or more of those for a month is data, not weakness.

Protect the recovery like a deliverable. After a real crunch, the next two weeks run at reduced load. Not because people deserve it, though they do, but because F1's rework is arriving in exactly those weeks and somebody has to absorb it. Plan it in, out loud.

15.7 — What if you do everything right and nothing changes?

Sometimes the forecasts are ignored, the scope conversation never happens, and every quarter is an emergency.

That is real, and it is worth naming honestly rather than concluding that you argued badly. Some organisations run on manufactured urgency and are not, in the short term, going to stop. Four things remain in your control.

Do the process anyway, at low cost. The weekly forecast takes ten minutes and builds the only currency that matters: a record of being right early, repeatedly.

Find the person who can actually decide scope, and talk to them directly. Very often the pressure is being relayed by somebody with no authority to relieve it.

Protect the floor, not the ceiling. If you cannot control the hours, control the quality floor: tests, review, no unreviewed merges. Those are the things that make the next quarter survivable, and they are much harder to argue away than a date.

Set an honest time limit for yourself. "If this is still the pattern in six months, I will move." Chronic deadline pressure with no lever is one of the few genuinely good reasons to change teams or companies. Treating that as a normal option, rather than as a failure, is what stops the slow slide into cynicism.

Which pairs get confused, and what separates them?

These two get mixed up The one sentence that separates them
velocity vs throughput velocity counts estimated points, a guess about a guess; throughput counts finished items, a fact. Forecast with throughput.

utilization vs productivity utilization is how committed capacity is; productivity is how much comes out. Past about 80% they move in opposite directions.
a deadline vs a target vs a milestone a deadline has a real consequence on the day; a target is a thing to aim at; a milestone is a checkpoint. Most "deadlines" are targets, and 15.1's question finds out which.

Rung 1 · Explain

1.1 One model answer:

The estimate covered the time spent typing the code, and only in the version where nothing goes wrong. It did not include the quarter of every week that goes to support, meetings and reviews; or the bugs the team made while working, which came back three weeks later as new work at the front of the queue; or the simple fact that when a task can only be early by a day but late by two weeks, the average is higher than the number anyone pictures. Add those up and the honest 5.1 weeks becomes 15.7. Nobody was slow — the plan measured one part of the job and paid for all of it.

1.2 The decomposition (§4) says 57.4% of paid time became features. Focus does not move that number, because the other 43% is not distraction — it is support, coordination, teaching, the team's own rework, and time spent waiting for something to pick up. The version to say out loud: "we are focused; the plan assumed 100% of our time and we have 57% — here is where the rest goes, and which parts of it you can decide to reduce."

1.3 Estimate: "that migration is about three days." Forecast: "the 60 remaining items land on week 14 with 85% confidence, based on our last ten weeks." Commitment: "we will ship on 12 October", said to someone who then books a marketing campaign. The failure mode is the silent upgrade from the first to the third with no forecast in between.

Rung 2 · Identify

2.1 In the default project the three largest non-feature slices are support and interrupts (25% of capacity, and by far the biggest), coordination between people (2% per engineer per other engineer — 8% for a team of five), and nobody had anything to pick up (idle capacity at the tail, when fewer tasks remain than there are engineers). The largest is a fact about this team, not a problem — until it is left out of the plan, at which point it becomes 3.7 weeks of overrun.

2.2 Fatigue appears in the first overtime week and grows every week (energy = 1 − 0.03 × weeksOfOvertime × (overtime/20), floored at 0.60). For a team of five on 60 hours it passes the coordination slice within the first few weeks and keeps climbing — the exact week is a good thing to read off the chart yourself rather than take from here.

2.3 The preemption line in Project.java:

backlog.add(0, new Task(nextTaskId++, d.reworkDays, true));   // index 0 = front

and the invisibility line:

int disc = day + (int) Math.round(p.discoveryDelayDays * (0.5 + qa.next()));

The delay matters more than the size because it decides when the work lands. 20.9 engineer-days of rework is only 5% of the budget, but it arrives near the end, at the front of the queue, after the plan has already committed. Work you know about early is a scheduling input; work that appears late is a slip.

Rung 3 · Predict

3.1 WIP 5 → 17.1 features by week 6. WIP 40 → 1.4. Ship week 15.7 vs 26.3.

3.2 Measured from week 4 (average of 40 runs):

  cut 40% of unstarted work    12.0     <- best, by a distance
  hold the plan                15.7

The real lesson is the size of the gap to the first row: the only intervention that removes work beats every intervention that moves it.

3.3 31% longer, not 25%. 1/(1−0.25) = 1.33, and the simulation gives 1.31.

Rung 4 · Modify

4.1 skewOn = false (every task exactly average, zero surprises):

  baseline (real variability)   15.7 weeks
  no variability at all         15.3 weeks

Four tenths of a week. Perfect estimation of every single task — a superpower no team has ever had — buys almost nothing here, because with 40 independent tasks the overruns and underruns largely cancel.

What it tells you: "we need to estimate better" is aimed at the wrong target. The gap is not noise around a correct number, it is a biased number (the median-vs-mean undercount, §10 Correction 1) meeting a capacity figure that was never real (§10 Correction 2). Precision does not fix bias.

The honest caveat: this model has no dependencies between tasks. On a real critical path variance does not cancel, it accumulates — every task waiting on a late one inherits the delay. So read this result as "variance is not the main term for independent work", not as "variance never matters".

4.2 interruptFraction = 0 → 12.0 weeks, recovering 3.7 of the 9.7 weeks of overrun — the single largest correctable term. It eliminates §11/F4 entirely and shrinks F6, because a team that is not carrying unplanned work has slack.

4.3 The ship date is 15.7 weeks whether you promise 3, 6 or 20.

One sentence: a deadline is a statement about what someone wants, and has no causal connection to how long the work takes.

The model is right about that, and it is also incomplete in two known directions. Deadlines do change behaviour: they focus decisions, they trigger scope cuts that would otherwise never happen, and — the effect this model deliberately omits — a distant deadline invites Parkinson's Law and Student syndrome, where work expands and starts late. So a deadline can genuinely change the outcome. What it cannot do is change the amount of work, and that is the mechanism people are relying on when they set an aggressive date and expect it to be met.

4.4 Crunching only the last two weeks:

  no crunch at all             15.7 weeks, 2.5 bugs escaped
  crunch the whole project     16.4 weeks, 6.7 bugs escaped
  crunch the last two weeks    15.1 weeks, 4.0 bugs escaped

The bounded end-of-project push works: 0.6 weeks earlier than not crunching, and 1.3 weeks better than crunching throughout. It costs 1.5 extra escaped defects.

This is the honest version of the crunch argument and worth carrying into a real conversation: a short, bounded push before a real date is a legitimate engineering decision with a known price. A quarter of 60-hour weeks is a way of paying more for less.

Rung 5 · Implement

5.1 Padding changes pointEstimateWeeks() from 5.1 to 10.2 weeks and changes the ship date by exactly nothing — the work is what it is.

The one-sentence lesson: secret padding changes the promise, not the project, and it costs you the only thing that makes a real forecast believable. Pad explicitly, as a confidence level ("week 14 at 85%"), which is the same number with its reasoning attached.

The sting is in Exercise 5.3: padding is not merely useless, it can be actively harmful.

5.2 Reference implementation:

/** The largest scope that still hits `week` with at least `confidence` probability. */
static int scopeForDate(int[] history, double week, double confidence) {
    int lo = 0, hi = 10_000;
    while (lo < hi) {
        int mid = (lo + hi + 1) / 2;
        double[] sim = simulate(history, mid, 0, 20_000, 42);
        if (chanceOfHitting(sim, week) >= confidence) lo = mid; else hi = mid - 1;
    }
    return lo;
}

Checked against the table in java src/Experiments.java forecast: with that history, 40 items gives 97% for week 10 and 50 items gives 54%, so scopeForDate(history, 10, 0.85) lands between them — around 43 items. The binary search works because the probability is monotonic in scope.

5.3 The patch that makes work expand into a per-task buffer:

// in Project.run, where task durations are drawn:
if (p.parkinson > 0)                       // an early finish is never reported
    d = Math.max(d, p.medianTaskDays * (1 - p.estimateOptimism) * (1 + p.parkinson));

Measured, 30% buffer both ways:

  pad every task by 30%, work expands to fill it  -> ships 18.6 weeks
  no padding, 30% buffer held at project level    -> ships 15.7 weeks
  against an honest week-16 promise:
      no padding   -> met 60% of the time
      padded tasks -> met 10% of the time

Per-task padding cost 2.9 weeks and 50 percentage points of reliability. The mechanism is asymmetry: a padded task can absorb its buffer but can never give it back, because early finishes are not reported. A project-level buffer is shared — one task's disaster is paid for by another task's good day — so the same nominal 30% covers far more risk. This is Goldratt's critical chain argument, and it is the strongest practical case for estimating honestly and buffering once, in public.

Rung 6 · Debug — the four planted mistakes

  Scope        40 tasks x 3.2 days   = 128 engineer-days
  Capacity     5 engineers x 5 days  =  25 engineer-days/week
  Build        128 / 25              = 5.1 weeks
  Test & fix   fixed allowance       = 1.0 weeks
  Contingency  10%                   = 0.5 weeks
  COMMITTED DATE                     = 7 weeks

Mistake 1 — capacity is booked at 100%. "5 engineers × 5 days" assumes every engineer spends every working day on this project. Measured by java src/Experiments.java interrupts: at 25% unplanned work the project takes 31% longer. Worth ~3.7 weeks. This is the biggest single error and the easiest to fix, because the number is measurable from last quarter.

Mistake 2 — the estimate is a sum of most-likely cases. 3.2 days is the happy-path median; the mean of that distribution is 5.1 days. summing_most_likely_estimates_ understates_the_total measures a 28% undercount from skew alone, before the 20% optimism. Worth ~4.5 weeks.

Mistake 3 — "test & fix" is a fixed block at the end. Defects are not a phase; they are made continuously and discovered about three weeks after they are made, arriving as queue-jumping rework. A one-week allowance is not a small buffer, it is a misunderstanding of when the work happens. Worth ~1.3 weeks, and more importantly it is the term that makes a project sit at "90% done" for a month.

Mistake 4 (in the recovery plan) — contractors booked at full capacity from day one. "2 contractors from week 8, adding 10 engineer-days/week from the day they start" ignores six weeks of ramp-up and the quarter of a mentor's day each one costs. Measured: the same project with those contractors ships at 15.9 weeks instead of 15.7 — the recovery plan makes it slightly worse.

Which to raise first, and which to leave alone.

Raise Mistake 1 first, always. It is a factual claim about how the team's time was actually spent last quarter, it is checkable, it is nobody's fault, and it is the largest term. Nobody has to admit they were optimistic to accept it.

Mistake 2 is the one to leave alone in the meeting, and this is the defensible answer the exercise asks for. Telling a room that their estimates are systematically biased is an argument about people's judgement, it is unfalsifiable in the moment, and it invites the useless response "then estimate better". You get the same correction for free — and without the fight — by forecasting from measured throughput instead of from the sum of estimates. Fix the bias by changing the method, not by winning the argument about it.

Rung 7 · Handle failure

7.1 · The bottleneck reviewer. Patch: a per-day cap on how many tasks can be marked done.

int reviewsLeft = p.reviewerCapacity > 0 ? p.reviewerCapacity : Integer.MAX_VALUE;
...
if (t.remaining <= 1e-9 && reviewsLeft <= 0) t.remaining = 1e-6;  // done, waiting
if (t.remaining <= 1e-9) { reviewsLeft--; /* ...mark done... */ }

Measured (WIP tracks team size, so people always have work):

  team       no cap      3/day      2/day      1/day
  5            15.7       15.7       15.8       17.1
  8            11.9       11.9       12.0       13.8
  12           10.1       10.0       10.3       13.0
  16            9.7        9.7        9.8       13.1

Read the last column. With a one-review-per-day bottleneck the date stops responding to team size — 12 engineers and 16 engineers both land at ~13 weeks. Read the top row too: at team size 5 the cap of 3/day costs nothing at all, because the team was never finishing three things a day.

The prediction about "we'll just add people": adding capacity past the constraint buys nothing. Before you accept more engineers, find out what the constraint is — review, QA, a shared environment, one person's approval. If the answer is "we'll just add people" and nobody can name the constraint, the money is being spent on the wrong thing. (This is Theory of Constraints, arrived at from the other end.)

7.2 · Bursty interrupts. Patch: lose whole days at random with the same 25% average.

if (p.burstyInterrupts && p.interruptsOn)
    interrupts = qa.next() < p.interruptFraction ? 1.0 : 0.0;

Measured: smooth 25% → 15.7 weeks; bursty whole days → 15.8 weeks. Essentially no difference — and if you predicted "much worse", you were reasoning correctly about the real world and hitting the edge of this model.

Why the model cannot see it: here an interrupt pauses the whole team together and the only output measured is the end date, over which the bursts average out. In a real team the damage from burstiness lands somewhere this model does not look — one engineer is yanked out while the others wait on their review, the half-finished task sits, and cycle time (§7) blows up while throughput barely moves. That is the mechanism of §11/F6, and it is why on-call teams feel far slower than their utilisation figure suggests.

To make the model show it you would need two changes: interrupts applied to individual engineers rather than the team, and a measurement of per-task cycle time rather than the project end date. That is a genuinely good extension, and the fact that the current model cannot show a real effect is worth more than a wrong number would be.

7.3 · Week 6, soft evidence, no weekly forecast so far. A defensible order:

  1. Today, before talking to anyone: build the forecast. Count what is actually finished, count what is left, take the last six weeks of throughput, run E8. You cannot have this conversation with a feeling, and you now have the tool.
  2. Same day, to your manager, privately. Not "we're going to miss it" — "I've put a forecast together and it says week 13, not week 10. I want to show you before I send anything wider." Private first is not politics; it stops them being surprised in public, which is the thing that makes people defensive.
  3. Bring the options with it (PLAYBOOK item 2): the gap in units of work, and the 20 items whose removal closes it.
  4. Start the weekly note this week (PLAYBOOK item 3), and say you are starting it. The point is that week 7's message is the second in a series rather than another alarm.
  5. If scope is not theirs to decide, ask to take it to whoever owns it, together (PLAYBOOK items 8 and 9).

What not to do: wait one more week for firmer evidence. Week 6 evidence with a stated probability beats week 9 certainty, because §10's scope experiment shows the lever you are trying to reach is worth 2.5 weeks now and 0.7 weeks then.

Rung 8 · Design

8.1 · The message. Model answer is PLAYBOOK item 2. Score yours on four things, not on wording:

  • Evidence, not assertion — your own throughput, stated as a range (2–8 items a week), not "the team feels stretched".
  • A probability on every date — "week 14 at 85%", never a bare number. If you wrote a single date you have reproduced the original mistake in the opposite direction.
  • The gap in units of work — "20 of the 60 items", not "we need more time". Work is a thing they can act on; time is a thing they must concede.
  • A decision that is theirs — three options, a recommendation, and a question at the end. If your message ends with a problem, rewrite it.

The one most people miss is the third. Also note what the data in the exercise contains: 1.5 items a week of new scope, which moves P50 from week 12 to week 18. If your message did not mention scope creep, you left the largest term out — and it is the one term that is nobody's fault and therefore the easiest to fix.

8.2 · The immovable regulatory date. What applies unchanged: capacity is not headcount (the two on-call teams should be planned at their measured availability, not their headcount); scope is the only fast lever; a hire in month 1 pays back, a hire in month 5 does not; forecasting from throughput beats forecasting from estimates.

What breaks: this model has no dependencies, and a three-team programme is mostly dependencies — the integration points between the teams are the schedule, and variance there accumulates instead of cancelling. It also has no notion of partial compliance, which is the central question here.

Month 1, and never in month 5:

  • Establish what "minimum legally sufficient" means and get it in writing from whoever owns the risk. On an immovable date, scope is the only lever, so the release must be ordered by regulatory necessity from day one. Everything else is optional by construction.
  • Build the integration path first, end to end, thin. The cross-team seams are the risk; discovering them in month 5 is the classic way these programmes fail.
  • Hire now if at all (§11/F2).
  • Set the on-call teams' planned capacity to their measured number, and get that written down before anyone commits to anything.

In month 5 you do the opposite of all of it: no new people, no new integrations, no architecture. You cut, you fix, and you protect the release.

The one weekly number for the executive: the probability of shipping the mandatory scope on the date (from the Monte Carlo, updated weekly). Not percent complete, which cannot fall and therefore tells them nothing. A number that can move in both directions is the only one that carries information.

8.3 · The weekly signal set.

Failure Early signal Where it comes from
F1 crunch spiral Hours worked per person, reported next to features delivered Anywhere honest; the pairing is the point
F2 late hire Time-to-first-meaningful-PR for each joiner Git history
F3 WIP trap Items in progress ÷ team size, and items finished per week Board
F4 interrupt tax % of time on unplanned work Ticket labels; a weekly 2-minute team poll works
F5 scope creep Items added after the plan, as a running total Board history — the number nobody plots
F6 utilization collapse Cycle time, and PR review latency Board / VCS metadata
F7 silent slip The forecast itself, weekly The Monte Carlo

The one with no good early signal is F7 — the silent slip — because the failure is a social one: the information exists in week 3 and does not travel. No metric fixes that, because the metric would have to be volunteered by the person not volunteering it.

The answer is structural, not analytical: make the forecast an automatic weekly artefact that goes out whether or not anything changed. Then not-reporting requires an action, rather than silence being the default. That is the whole reason §15.3 is a rhythm rather than a conversation — it converts a courage problem into a calendar problem, and calendar problems are the only kind that get solved reliably.
