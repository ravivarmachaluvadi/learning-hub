1. WHY does this exist?

Sometimes one action must change data in two different systems and it is catastrophic for only one change to happen: debit money at one bank and credit it at another; pop a message from one store and record it in another. Each system can promise all-or-nothing for its own changes, but nobody is in charge of the pair. Two-phase commit exists to make several independent systems commit or abort as one, by adding a referee and a strict two-round conversation.

2. THE PROBLEM, as a story

Think of a wedding. Two people must be married simultaneously — a state where one is married and the other isn't must never exist, not even for a moment, not even if someone faints mid-ceremony.

The officiant can't just declare "you're married!" and hope: maybe one of them was about to object. And the couple can't just each decide privately: maybe one says yes and the other walks out — now one person considers themselves married to someone who doesn't.

Notice what the real ceremony does. The officiant first asks EACH person, one at a time: "Do you take...?" — and waits for both *"I do"*s. An "I do" is not the marriage. It is a binding promise to accept the officiant's verdict: having said it, you cannot leave; you stand there and wait. Only after hearing yes from both does the officiant pronounce — and the pronouncement, that single utterance, is the moment both become married at once. Ask-then-pronounce. Two phases.

And the ceremony has a famous nightmare lurking in it: both have said "I do"… and the officiant faints before pronouncing. Are they married? The two of them cannot decide — the verdict existed only in the officiant's head. They stand frozen at the altar until the officiant wakes. Hold that image; it is the exact shape of 2PC's defining flaw.

3. THE NAIVE SOLUTION

Back to computers: move ₹500 from alice's account at Bank A to bob's at Bank B. The obvious code:

bankA.debit("alice", 500);    // commit #1
bankB.credit("bob", 500);     // commit #2  — and pray

Just do both, in some order. (Your outbox lab called this a dual write; this is the same beast wearing a banking uniform.)

4. WHY IT FAILS, concretely

Crash between the two commits: alice's ₹500 is gone, bob never got it — money destroyed. Reverse the order: bob is credited, then the debit fails (alice's balance too low, or the process dies) — money created from air. "Undo on failure" helps only if the undoer survives and the undo can't itself fail; "retry" helps only if you can tell what already happened. The two commits are independent decisions made at different times; any gap between them is a window where the world can end up half-changed. What's missing is a single moment of joint decision.

5. THE KEY IDEA

Split "commit" into a promise and a verdict. First, every system is asked to prepare: to durably guarantee "I can commit this, and I can also undo it — whichever you say, even if I crash in between." Only when every system has promised does a referee record one single, durable verdict — and that one recorded verdict is the moment the whole transaction commits, everywhere at once. After that, telling everyone is mere bookkeeping.

6. TERMINOLOGY

  • Two-phase commit (2PC) — the protocol above: a voting phase, then a decision phase. (No relation to "two-phase locking", a concurrency-control technique with a confusingly similar name.)
  • Coordinator — the referee/officiant: collects votes, records the decision, announces it. Often a library or transaction manager inside your application server, not a separate box.
  • Participant (or resource manager) — a system holding data: each bank, a database, a message broker.
  • Prepare (phase 1 request) — "could you commit transaction T if asked?" Answering YES requires durably storing everything needed to go either way (undo + redo information) before answering.
  • Vote — a participant's YES or NO. YES = the "I do": a binding, crash-surviving promise to await and obey the verdict. NO = veto; one NO aborts everyone. 2PC is unanimous consent, not majority.
  • Commit point — the single instant the transaction commits: the coordinator's decision record hitting its durable log. Not when participants apply it — when the referee's pen lifts off the paper.
  • In doubt / uncertain — the state of a participant that voted YES and hasn't heard the verdict. It may not commit, may not abort, may not release locks. This window is where all of 2PC's pain lives.
  • Blocking — what an in-doubt participant does when the coordinator is unreachable: nothing, indefinitely, holding locks.
  • Presumed abort — the standard optimization/rule: if the coordinator's log holds no decision for T, the answer is ABORT. Safe, because the verdict couldn't have reached anyone if it was never recorded.
  • XA — the industry-standard API for 2PC (X/Open XA): what Java's JTA (javax.transaction), XADataSource, and "distributed transactions" in app servers speak.
  • Write-ahead log (WAL) — each node's durable append-only journal; every promise and every verdict is a log record first, state change second. Recovery = re-reading your own log.

7. MENTAL MODEL

Carry this picture: 2PC narrows a multi-machine decision down to a single disk write. Phase 1 exists only to make that possible: it herds every participant into a state where both futures (commit and abort) are fully survivable, so the choice between the futures can be made unilaterally, by one node, in one atomic log append. Everything before that write is retractable; everything after it is inevitable. The cost of the narrowing: everyone stands frozen — promises made, locks held — staring at one machine's disk, until told which future they're in.

9. THE ACTUAL MECHANISM, step by step

The transfer: ₹500, alice@BANK-A → bob@BANK-B. alice has ₹1000, bob ₹200. Transaction id txn-1. Coordinator C (the travel… no — this time it's the transfer service's transaction manager).

Phase 1 — voting.

  1. C durably logs BEGIN txn-1 [A: alice −500, B: bob +500], then sends PREPARE txn-1 to A and B.
  2. BANK-A checks: alice has ₹1000 ≥ 500 ✓. It locks alice's row, writes ⟨txn-1, PREPARED, alice, −500⟩ to its durable log — undo and redo info both implied — flushes it to disk, and only then replies YES. From this instant, Bank A has lost the right to change its mind, even across a crash: the log entry is the promise.
  3. BANK-B does the same for bob's credit: lock, log, flush, YES.

The commit point.

  1. C has unanimous YES. It writes ⟨txn-1, COMMIT⟩ to its durable log and flushes. This disk write is the entire commit. Before it: a crash anywhere means abort (presumed abort — no decision recorded). After it: the transaction is committed even if nobody has been told yet (the lab's test_decision_log_write_is_the_commit_point proves exactly this).

Phase 2 — completion.

  1. C sends COMMIT txn-1 to both banks. Each applies its change (alice 1000→500; bob 200→700), logs ⟨txn-1, COMMITTED⟩, releases its locks, and ACKs.
  2. When all ACKs are in, C logs END txn-1 and can forget the transaction.

Two round trips; roughly four durable log flushes on the critical path (one per participant in phase 1, the decision, and participants' outcome records). Locks held from step 2 until step 5 — including across every network delay in between. That is the happy path.

10. FAILURE SCENARIOS — walk every crash point

The protocol's design rule: at every possible crash instant, the durable logs must contain enough truth to finish the job. Check it hop by hop.

(a) A participant votes NO (bob's account frozen by compliance, or insufficient funds). One NO → C logs ABORT → everyone aborts, locks released, nothing changed. Run: java TpcLab.java participant-votes-no.

(b) A participant crashes (or is unreachable) before voting. C waits, times out, counts silence as NO, aborts everyone reachable. This timeout is safe — no decision existed, so nothing can have committed anywhere. The crashed bank finds either nothing or no promise in its log on restart — also fine. Run: java TpcLab.java participant-crash-before-vote.

(c) A participant crashes just after voting YES. Its YES may or may not have arrived. Its log durably holds PREPARED. On restart it is in doubt: it re-acquires the lock (the promise survives restarts!) and must ask the coordinator for the verdict — it cannot infer it. Test: test_participant_recovers_in_doubt_and_asks_coordinator.

(d) Coordinator crashes before logging a decision (during voting, or after collecting votes but before the decision write). Its recovered log has BEGIN but no verdict → presumed abort: it aborts the transaction and tells everyone. Participants that were in doubt learn ABORT. Safe, because the verdict that never hit the log can never have been sent.

(e) Coordinator crashes AFTER logging COMMIT, before telling anyone. ⚠️ The wedding nightmare. Both banks hold PREPARED in their logs, locks on alice and bob, and wait. Consider Bank A's options:

  • Abort unilaterally? The verdict COMMIT exists on C's disk. B might ask a recovered C, learn COMMIT, and commit. A aborted, B committed — the exact half-transfer 2PC exists to prevent.
  • Commit unilaterally? In a different run, C died one instant earlier, before logging — verdict ABORT. Same local history at A, opposite correct answer.

The lab's experiment 6 (two-possible-worlds) makes this brutal and concrete: two runs in which Bank A's entire visible history is byte-identical — voted YES, then silence — end with alice at ₹500 in one world and ₹1000 in the other. No local rule can pick right. So a prepared participant blocks: locks held, every customer touching those rows waits behind it, for as long as the coordinator is down. Minutes. Hours. Run: java TpcLab.java coordinator-crash-blocks-everyone, then coordinator-recovery to watch the only cure — the coordinator's return:

    another customer reads alice at BANK-A -> BLOCKED (account 'alice' is
        locked by in-flight transaction txn-5 — come back later)
Coordinator restarts, reads its durable log, re-sends phase 2:
    BANK-A <- COMMIT: committed: alice now 500

(f) Phase 2 message lost / duplicated. C retries COMMIT until ACKed; participants treat a repeated COMMIT as a no-op (the lab's commit() is idempotent — same discipline as everywhere in your other two labs).

(g) Cooperative termination (partial mercy). An in-doubt participant can ask its peers: if any peer already received the verdict, or never got prepared at all (proving no unanimous YES existed), the answer spreads. But in scenario (e) — all peers prepared, none informed — everyone is equally in the dark, and everyone stays blocked. It shrinks the window; it cannot close it.

11. CORRECTNESS — what is and isn't guaranteed

Guaranteed (safety — and 2PC's safety is genuinely airtight):

  • Atomicity: no reachable history has one participant committed and another aborted. All-or-nothing, always, under any crash pattern.
  • Real isolation: locks from prepare to decision mean nobody observes the in-between. Compare your saga lab's dirty-read experiment — the anomaly sagas accept is precisely what these locks forbid.
  • Durability of the verdict: one log write, replayable forever.

Explicitly NOT guaranteed (liveness):

  • Progress while the coordinator is down after prepares. Participants block, indefinitely, holding locks. 2PC sacrifices availability for atomicity; it cannot have both (this is the two-generals/FLP shadow — no protocol on an unreliable network can, but 2PC concentrates the fragility in one node).
  • Non-blocking behavior under two simultaneous failures — even 3PC (a historical "fix") only handles single failures without partitions; real fixes replicate the coordinator's decision with consensus (Raft/Paxos), which is a different, heavier animal.

Traded away: availability (one machine's disk is everyone's fate), latency (2 round trips + synchronous log flushes), throughput (locks held across network hops), and universality (every participant must implement prepare/redo/undo — most SaaS APIs, and Kafka's XA-less broker, never will). This trade is exactly what outbox and saga refuse: they accept eventual consistency and visible intermediate states to keep availability and work with participants that only understand "do it".

12. THE CODE

TpcLab.java, ~280 lines of mechanism:

  • Bank.prepare — the vote: check, lock, durably log PREPARED before answering YES. Knobs: voteNo, crashBeforeVote, crashAfterVote (a crashed bank becomes unreachable — down).
  • Bank.readBalance — the outside world; raises Blocked on locked rows.
  • Bank.recover — restart from the log; PREPARED without an outcome → in doubt: relock and wait; askCoordinator resolves it.
  • Coordinator.runTransfer — phase 1, the decision write (crash knobs on both sides of it — the two sides are the two possible worlds), phase 2.
  • Coordinator.decisionFor — presumed abort in four lines: no decision in the log ⇒ ABORT.
  • Coordinator.recover — log-replay: any decided-but-not-ENDed transaction gets phase 2 re-sent.

What the same thing looks like in production Java (illustrative):

// JTA/XA — the app server's transaction manager is the coordinator
@Transactional  // with two XADataSources enlisted
public void transfer() {
    jdbcA.update("UPDATE accounts SET bal = bal - 500 WHERE id='alice'");
    jdbcB.update("UPDATE accounts SET bal = bal + 500 WHERE id='bob'");
}   // commit() → XAResource.prepare() on both, then commit() on both

The annotation hides an entire coordinator, two XA drivers, and every scenario in section 10 — which is exactly why XA incidents are so confusing to debug from the outside.

14. PRODUCTION — what the lab omits and how real systems do it

Lab simplification Production reality
"Durable log" = a Python list kept alive Real WALs with fsync — 2PC's latency cost is mostly these synchronous flushes; disk-flush tuning is XA tuning.
Coordinator is one object with one log The single point of failure. Real deployments: transaction managers with recovery logs (Narayana, Atomikos, Bitronix in JVM land), or — the modern fix — replicate the coordinator's decision via consensus: Spanner runs 2PC over Paxos groups; each "participant" is itself a replicated state machine, so no single disk is anyone's fate.
In-doubt = wait for our coordinator Real databases surface in-doubt transactions to operators: Oracle's DBA_2PC_PENDING + COMMIT FORCE / ROLLBACK FORCE, Postgres's pg_prepared_xacts after PREPARE TRANSACTION — the on-call human is the cooperative-termination protocol of last resort, and "heuristic commit/abort" (guessing) is the documented, atomicity-breaking emergency exit.
Locks = a dict Row locks held across two network round trips — the throughput killer. This, not the crash stories, is why XA quietly disappeared from high-scale stacks: even when nothing fails, everything is slower.
Two participants, one transaction Real coordinators interleave thousands; logs carry per-txn state machines; presumed-abort keeps the log small (only COMMIT decisions and prepares must be flushed; aborts can be lazy).
Everyone speaks the protocol The adoption wall: participants must implement prepare/undo/redo + the XA API. Databases mostly do (Postgres, MySQL, Oracle, MQ brokers via JMS XA). Kafka, most REST/SaaS APIs, DynamoDB, S3 — do not, and never will. One non-XA participant in your flow and 2PC is off the table entirely — which is how most teams actually end up at outbox + saga.
— Where 2PC is alive and well: inside systems, invisibly — Spanner and CockroachDB commit multi-shard transactions with (consensus-hardened) 2PC; XA between two databases you fully control in one datacenter remains legitimate and simple. The pattern died at the microservice boundary, not in the database engine room.

16. RELATED CONCEPTS — and when to choose which

  • Transactional outbox (sibling lab) — when the "second system" only needs to hear about the change, eventually. No veto power, no isolation needed → don't pay for 2PC; commit locally + relay. This covers the vast majority of "I almost need a distributed transaction" cases.
  • Saga (sibling lab) — when several services must each change state and business-level undo is possible. Trades 2PC's isolation for availability: compensations instead of locks, visible intermediate states instead of blocking. Choose sagas at service boundaries; choose 2PC only when a half-visible intermediate state is unacceptable and every participant speaks XA.
  • Three-phase commit (3PC) — adds a pre-commit round so participants can decide without the coordinator after single clean failures. Broken by network partitions (two sides reach opposite verdicts), costs an extra round trip always; historically important, deployed almost nowhere. Know it as an interview answer, not a tool.
  • Consensus (Raft / Paxos) — solves a different problem (many replicas agreeing on one value/log with majorities, no unanimity, no veto), but is 2PC's modern rescue: replicate the coordinator's decision so no single crash blocks anyone (Spanner = 2PC over Paxos). Rule of thumb: consensus = same data on many machines; 2PC = different data on many machines, changing together.

Solutions

Tier 1 — Predict

P1. At the instant the NO arrives: alice's row is locked at Bank A (it prepared first); bob's row is NOT locked (B refused — a NO acquires nothing durable). Bank A's log: ⟨txn, PREPARED, alice, −500⟩. B's log: ⟨txn, VOTED-NO⟩. Next appends: the coordinator logs ⟨txn, ABORT⟩ (the decision — an abort gets recorded at the same commit-point location, it's just the other verdict), then A logs ⟨txn, ABORTED⟩ as its lock is released. Note the asymmetry the run shows: YES is expensive (lock + flush

  • obligation); NO is cheap and final.

P2. alice reads 500 (A was told, applied, unlocked); bob reads BLOCKED (B is prepared, in doubt, lock held). This is not an atomicity violation — it is the isolation doing its job during an unusually long window. The transaction IS committed (the verdict is on C's disk); B just hasn't heard. Nobody can observe "debit happened, credit didn't" — the credit's absence is hidden behind the lock. The state is ugly (half-announced), but never wrong. Atomicity would be violated only if bob's row were readable at 200 after alice's showed 500 — exactly what the lock forbids.

P3. The rule destroys money in WORLD 1 (coordinator logged COMMIT before dying): A times out and aborts — alice keeps ₹1000; the recovered coordinator's log says COMMIT, so B (which waited) commits — bob gains ₹500. ₹500 created from nothing; global state: alice 1000, bob 700. The symmetric rule ("timeout ⇒ COMMIT") fails identically in WORLD 2 (alice 500, bob 200 — money destroyed). No timeout rule at a prepared participant can be safe, because — as the experiment proves — both worlds present identical local evidence. The only safe unilateral timeout in all of 2PC is the coordinator's timeout before the decision is logged, because at that point no verdict exists to contradict.

P4. Count: C's BEGIN (1) + A's PREPARED (2) + B's PREPARED (3) + C's VOTED-NO is B's log entry — careful, with three participants A and B log PREPARED (2 writes), C logs VOTED-NO (1 write), coordinator logs BEGIN and then ABORT (2 writes) = 5 total. The wasted work: A's and B's PREPARED flushes — two synchronous disk writes (plus locks held) for a transaction that was doomed the moment C looked at its data. Unavoidable because votes are collected in parallel ignorance: nobody knows the outcome until the last vote is in — unanimity means the cost of a veto is paid by everyone who said yes. (This "pay even to lose" property is part of 2PC's latency reputation.)

Tier 2 — Modify

M1. test_participant_recovers_in_doubt_and_asks_coordinator fails: after the crash, A's log contains no PREPARED, so Bank.recover finds nothing in doubt — no lock, no pending entry. The killer timing: A says YES (message sent), crashes before logging; coordinator has unanimous YES, logs COMMIT; recovered A has no memory of any promise — its vote evaporated. When the commit arrives (or A asks), A has no undo/redo info and no lock; meanwhile it may have already accepted other transactions touching alice. The verdict says debit; A can't honor it or may apply it over changed state — money wrong either way. The rule broken: promise durably, then promise out loud — the log write must precede the message that makes it binding. Same law everywhere in your labs: the outbox commits the message with the order before anything is sent (outbox lab §5); the saga coordinator acts and then logs but leans on idempotency precisely because act-and-tell isn't atomic (saga lab D1: record-then-act vs act-then-record). In 2PC's phase 1 the participant has no idempotency to lean on — the log flush before the YES is the whole promise.

M2. Decision table for in-doubt asker:

Peer's state Asker may safely… Why
never heard of txn ABORT peer never voted ⇒ unanimity was impossible ⇒ no COMMIT verdict can exist
VOTED-NO ABORT a NO exists ⇒ verdict is ABORT
COMMITTED COMMIT the verdict has been seen: it was COMMIT
ABORTED ABORT verdict seen: ABORT
PREPARED (in doubt too) nothing — keep asking / keep blocking both are inside the uncertainty window; the verdict may be either

The last row is scenario (e): all peers prepared, coordinator gone — cooperative termination spreads knowledge but cannot create it.

M3. Run: coordinator logs COMMIT after A's YES; B then votes NO (bob's account frozen). The decision is already durable — by the commit-point rule it is committed — but B refused and will abort locally. A commits, B aborts: atomicity gone. The broken property: the decision write may happen only after unanimity is proven — the commit point's authority comes entirely from the fact that every participant has already made both futures possible. Logging early is writing a verdict about promises not yet given.

M4. Sequence: scenario (e) (coordinator logged COMMIT, died); operator, tired of nine-hour locks, runs heuristicAbort on Bank B — bob's lock released, B logs ABORTED. Coordinator recovers, re-sends COMMIT; A commits (alice 500). Auditor finds: C's log ⟨COMMIT⟩, A's log ⟨PREPARED, COMMITTED⟩, B's log ⟨PREPARED, ABORTED⟩ — a written confession of a half-committed transaction (₹500 vanished). Real databases ship it anyway because indefinite blocking can be the worse business outcome (an entire batch pipeline stopped for hours); heuristic decisions convert an availability catastrophe into a bounded, documented consistency debt that reconciliation can repay. The XA spec even standardizes the resulting states (HEURRB, HEURCOM) precisely so the damage is at least visible.

Tier 3 — Debug

D1. test_prepared_account_blocks_other_customers (and its sibling assertion inside the blocking test) fails — readBalance no longer throws. The anomaly: transfer T1 (alice→bob) prepares at both banks, locks dropped after YES. Reader R now sees alice 1000 and bob 200 — fine so far. T1's verdict COMMIT arrives: alice 500, bob 700. But suppose instead a second transfer T2 (alice→carol, ₹800) starts in the gap: alice is unlocked, balance still 1000, so A votes YES on T2 as well. Both verdicts COMMIT: alice = 1000 − 500 − 800 = −300 — a state the prepare-time check explicitly forbade, reached with every individual step "validated." The locks were carrying isolation — and, as this shows, the validity of every YES vote: a vote is a promise about a world that must be frozen until the verdict, or the promise silently rots.

D2. Options: (1) wait for a coordinator that will never return — blocks forever; (2) ROLLBACK FORCE (heuristic abort) — unblocks now; wrong if the lost verdict was COMMIT, and with the recovery log gone, nobody will ever know — reconcile with the other bank's books by hand; (3) COMMIT FORCE — mirror-image risk; (4) reconstruct the verdict from surviving participants' logs (cooperative termination by grep): if ANY participant shows COMMITTED/ABORTED for the txn, follow it — this is the only risk-free option and should be tried first; if all show PREPARED, you're back to guessing. The architectural decision that prevents it all: the coordinator's decision log must be replicated / survivable infrastructure (HA transaction-manager storage — or 2PC over a consensus group, Spanner-style), and decommissioning anything holding recovery logs must be gated on no in-doubt transactions reference it. A transaction manager's log is data, not config — the migration treated it as config.

D3. Run: A and B both prepare; in-memory coordinator decides COMMIT, tells A (A commits, unlocks); coordinator restarts (memory gone); B, in doubt, re-asks; coordinator finds no decision for the txn and — by presumed abort, which is only sound when decisions are durable — answers ABORT. B aborts. A committed, B aborted, both banks behaved perfectly. The missing property: the commit point must be a single durable, crash-surviving fact — "one write, one truth, forever". In-memory, the verdict is Schrödinger's: it can be observed (by A) and then cease to have ever existed (for B). Presumed abort turns that amnesia into an actively wrong answer, because its whole logic is "no durable record ⇒ no one can have been told" — an invariant the restart just broke.
