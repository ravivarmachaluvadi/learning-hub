Lab 67 · Whole Systems

CockroachDB — the database that refused to buy a clock

Tell a friend: Asha pays a bill in Mumbai and is told it went through. She telephones Ben in Ohio, whose clerk looks in his own copy of the same ledger and says no such payment exists. Nobody lied and no page was missing: the two wristwatches that dated the entries disagree, and one of them called the payment "the future". Google's Spanner cured this by buying clocks good enough that a writer could sit out their error before answering. CockroachDB cannot buy those clocks, so instead it declares how far apart the watches may be, acknowledges every write immediately, and hands the leftover doubt to whoever reads next.

The trainer for this lab is visualization.html, also published as The Reader Pays. Nothing to install, no code to run. What the lab is made of, where its numbers come from and where it sits among your other labs are in section 17 at the end.

1. Why can't a database survive, hold money and speak SQL all at once?

In one breath: You want a database that keeps working when a machine dies, never loses track of money, and understands ordinary SQL. Any two of those have been easy for decades. All three, on more data than one machine holds, was an open problem until about 2012.

You are running a business on a database. Three things have to be true at once. They fight each other.

The first is survival. Not graceful degradation. Not a failover in four minutes with a page to a human. The database keeps answering while a machine is on fire.

The second is money held correctly. When two things must both happen or neither, that is what happens — even when the two things live on different machines.

The third is SQL. Your application speaks it. Your reporting stack speaks it. Every engineer you will hire already speaks it.

Pick any two of the three and the answer has existed for decades. Here is what each pair costs you.

Pick these two What you give up The shipped product that does it
Money and SQL survival one PostgreSQL box, backed up nightly
Survival and SQL money across machines shard it yourself, reconcile later
Survival and money SQL a key-value store with no joins

Now pick all three, at more data and more traffic than one machine can hold. Until about 2012 the honest answer was: you can't. Shard it yourself and give up transactions across the shards.

Then Google's Spanner proved you could have all three. The price of admission was hardware you cannot buy: GPS antennas and atomic clocks in every datacentre. They feed a time service that tells you not what time it is, but how wrong it might be. That design is the google-spanner lab, and this lab assumes you have read it.

CockroachDB answers a narrower question. Can you have all three on ordinary machines with ordinary clocks, in somebody else's cloud, where you are not allowed to install an antenna on the roof?

Suppose nobody solves it. The consequence is the one every large system ends up living with. A payments ledger sharded by customer ID. An application-level "distributed transaction" made of retries and reconciliation jobs. And a monthly meeting about the seventeen accounts that do not balance.

Asha's bill is one line in that ledger, and Ben is waiting to hear about it. The next section is what goes wrong for the two of them.

2. What goes wrong when one ledger book becomes three?

In one breath: One clerk with one book gets order and time for free, because one wristwatch dated everything. Split the book across three towns and both gifts vanish. A payment can be real, acknowledged, and invisible.

A town has one ledger book and one clerk. Every purchase, payment and transfer is a line in the book. Every line is dated and timed by the clerk as it is written.

Because there is one book and one clerk, two things are automatically true. Nobody ever thinks about them.

Everything is written in one order — the order the clerk wrote it. And every line's time is comparable to every other line's, because one wristwatch produced all of them.

Then the clerk goes home with influenza. The town stops.

So the town hires three clerks in three towns. Meera works in Mumbai. Arjun works in Frankfurt. Dev works in Ohio. Each gets a copy of the book, so no single illness can stop anything.

And now both of the free gifts are gone.

Three clerks write in three books, so there is no single order. And this next fact generates everything else in the lab. Each clerk dates entries by their own wristwatch, and no two watches agree.

They are not broken watches. They are ordinary ones, checked against the radio now and then, drifting a little between checks. Meera's runs a fifth of a second fast. Dev's runs a tenth of a second slow. Nobody can tell. To know your watch is fast you would need a watch you already trusted.

Now the damage, in milliseconds, exactly as the simulator runs it. Asha pays a bill in Mumbai. Ben is her supplier in Ohio, waiting to hear. Call the true instant of Asha's payment 1000.

Step through the last row slowly. Asha's entry is dated 1180. Dev is reading the book as of 960. 1180 is later than 960. So as far as Dev can tell, the payment has not happened yet. He tells Ben: "no payment here."

Nobody lied. Nobody's watch is broken. No message was lost. Ben has been told something false about money, twenty milliseconds after Asha was told it was true.

That is the failure this lab is about. Not lost data, not late data, but data that is present, acknowledged, and invisible.

3. What does a reasonable person try first?

In one breath: Four fixes, in the order a sensible engineer reaches for them. Three of them you have already killed in earlier labs. The fourth is not wrong at all — it is simply unavailable to you.

Here are the four, with the three quick ones first.

# The fix Where it dies Already covered in
1 One bigger machine. No second clock, no second order. Works beautifully until the machine dies or the data outgrows it. This is not a distributed-systems failure; it is the absence of the property you paid for. cap-theorem
2 One writer, several read replicas. One clock stamps everything, so order is safe. The single writer is the ceiling and the outage. Promoting a replica loses whatever had not shipped. quorums-and-replication, split-brain
3 Shard by customer, transactions within a shard only. Any invariant spanning two shards — a transfer, a uniqueness constraint, a ledger that must balance — has no home. You counted what happens when you bolt two-phase commit on anyway: 16 fractured audits in 40 runs, with no crashes at all. distributed-transactions
4 Do what Spanner does: buy a clock that admits its own error, and make every writer wait out that error before answering. This one is correct. It closes the problem completely. It is also the one you cannot have. google-spanner

Fix 4 deserves more than a row, because it is not wrong. It is unavailable.

Why is fix 4 correct and still out of reach?

Start with the thing in the story. Imagine Meera's wall clock has a band painted on its face rather than a single hand. The band is a promise: the true moment is somewhere inside it. Half the width of that band is how wrong the clock might be, right now, at worst.

That is the whole of Spanner's clock. Three names, in the order you should meet them.

The thing in the story Said in words The engineer's name
the band painted on the clock face an earliest and a latest, with the truth guaranteed to be between them TT.now(), returning [earliest, latest]
half the width of that band how wrong this clock might be, at worst, right now ε, said "epsilon"
"has that moment definitely gone past?" true only when the whole band has moved past the stamp TT.after(s)

Now the protocol, in one sentence. A writer stamps its transaction with the latest edge of the band. Then it refuses to acknowledge the client until the band has moved entirely past its own stamp. By the time anybody hears the write is done, its stamp is provably in the past for every machine everywhere.

The Spanner lab put the bill at about 5 ms added to every write. It also listed the hardware in every datacentre that keeps ε down to single-digit milliseconds.

The reason you cannot copy this is not intellectual. It is a loading dock. ε is small only because there is a GPS receiver on the roof and an atomic clock in the rack. You cannot install either in a cloud region you rent by the hour.

Without them, ε on ordinary machines disciplined by network time is hundreds of milliseconds. And a design that sleeps for twice ε on every write becomes a design that sleeps for most of a second on every write.

4. Where do all four fixes break?

In one breath: Take Spanner's design, change nothing, and run it on rented machines. The protocol still works perfectly. Only the number moves — and the number is the product.

This is the most instructive failure in the lab. Nothing about the protocol breaks. One constant does.

Set ε to a realistic commodity value of 250 ms. The writer's wait is twice ε, so every acknowledgement is held for roughly 500 ms.

What you changed ε The wait on every write What a payments API answers in
nothing — Google's hardware ~4 ms ~8 ms about 15 ms
the same code, on rented machines 250 ms ≈ 500 ms 515 ms

You have bought a perfectly correct database that nobody can use. Asha's payment is now correct and half a second late, every single time, whether or not Ben was ever going to ask about it.

And you cannot simply declare ε small and carry on. ε is not a preference. Claim 5 ms while your clocks actually disagree by 200 ms, and the writer's wait is too short. External consistency then dies with commit wait switched on, silently. That is exactly the fourth scenario in the Spanner lab.

So the four fixes leave us here. Fixes 1 to 3 give up one of the three requirements outright. Fix 4 keeps all three and charges the writer an amount proportional to how bad your clocks are. On Google's hardware that is a few milliseconds. On rented hardware it ends the business.

What the failure reveals is not "clocks are hard". It is sharper than that.

The cost of clock uncertainty has to be paid by somebody. Spanner charges the writer, on every single write, whether or not anyone was ever going to read it. That is a fixed premium on all traffic, and on commodity hardware the premium is unaffordable.

Which raises the question this whole lab turns on. Is the writer the right payer?

🔮 Predict: Suppose you move the bill to the reader instead. Reads are usually the majority of traffic — often 95% of it. Does that make the total bill bigger or smaller?

Reveal

Smaller, by a lot, and the reason is not the ratio. It is that the reader's bill is conditional. A writer's wait is charged on every write, always, whether or not a reader was ever going to look.

A reader only pays when it happens to meet a value stamped inside its own window of doubt. On most workloads that is rare. Ninety-five reads that touch nothing recent cost nothing at all.

If you guessed "bigger, because there are more readers", you were pricing a premium per operation. This is not a premium. It is an excess, paid only on claims.

5. What exactly must the ledger promise?

In one breath: Three separate promises hide inside "the database is correct". CockroachDB keeps two of them and declines the third on purpose, and knowing which is which is most of what a senior engineer knows about it.

Strip away the mechanism. There are three promises, and they are worth separating.

Promise A — no stale reads. Once a write has been acknowledged to its client, every transaction that begins after that acknowledgement sees it. This is the promise Ben's telephone call broke in section 2. It is the one users notice, because it is the one that makes a database feel like a single machine.

Promise B — serializable. However many transactions run at once, the outcome matches some one-at-a-time order of them. No fractured reads, no write skew, no lost updates. You have the full anomaly taxonomy already from isolation-levels.

Promise C — that order matches real time, for everybody. Not merely "some serial order exists". The stronger claim is that the serial order never contradicts the wall clock — even for two transactions that share no data and never met.

Promise C has three names in this curriculum. They are the same property.

Where you met it What it was called there Applied to
linearizability-vs-serializability strict serializability whole transactions
google-spanner external consistency whole transactions
this lab, from section 12 onward strict serializability the one thing declined

Here is the honest statement of what this lab builds. It is made now, rather than sprung on you in section 12.

CockroachDB guarantees A and B. It does not guarantee C, and does not claim to. Cockroach Labs' own writing puts it plainly: "CockroachDB doesn't quite offer strict serializability, but we're fairly close to it."

Three places pay that off. Section 12 shows you exactly what falls through the gap between B and C. Scenario 8 of the simulator lets you watch it happen with every clock inside its budget. And section 13 shows you the one table type where CockroachDB buys C back — by doing exactly what Spanner does.

6. What is the key idea, in two sentences?

In one breath: Stop trying to know the time. Declare how wrong your clock is allowed to be, and make the reader carry that doubt. Then apply the same refusal to be sure everywhere else: never prevent a conflict, just detect it afterwards and redo the work.

Two sentences. Read them slowly. The whole lab is inside them.

Sentence one: do not try to know the time. Know instead how wrong your clock is allowed to be. That number is one you declare, not one you measure. Then put the cost of that doubt on the reader.

Sentence two: never coordinate to prevent a conflict. Proceed optimistically, detect the conflict afterwards, and retry.

The first sentence inverts Spanner exactly. Cockroach Labs state the inversion in one line: "While Spanner always waits after writes, CockroachDB sometimes retries reads."

Look at what that trade actually is.

Spanner CockroachDB
Shape of the cost certain, small, universal uncertain, occasionally large, rare
Who is billed the writer the reader
When every write, always only when a read meets a recent value
What a quiet read costs nothing nothing
What an unlucky read costs nothing its work, done twice

Writes are acknowledged immediately. Reads that touch nothing recent cost nothing. The bill only arrives when a reader and a recent writer nearly collide in time — which, on most workloads, is seldom.

The second sentence is why CockroachDB looks so unlike a lock-based database from the outside. Almost nothing waits for anything. Writes do not wait for a commit record. Readers do not wait for writers. Writers do not wait for readers.

Instead the system detects, afterwards, that something incompatible happened. Then it throws work away.

Meet Omar. He writes the application that talks to this cluster, and he is the third person the bill can be sent to. His code must wrap every transaction in a retry loop, because the database is entitled to refuse a perfectly reasonable transaction and ask for it again.

The refusal has a name he will see in his logs. In words it is "I could not commit this without breaking serializability, so please run it again." The SQL standard calls that a serialization failure, and the code for it is SQLSTATE 40001.

That code is part of the API contract, not an error path. An ordinary, healthy CockroachDB application retries on it several times an hour and nothing is wrong.

Say it back. Without using the words timestamp, uncertainty or commit wait, explain to Omar why Spanner's writes are slow and CockroachDB's reads are sometimes done twice.

A good answer sounds like

"Both systems know their clocks might disagree, and somebody has to absorb that. Spanner makes the person filing the document sit still for a moment before anyone is told, so by the time a reader turns up there is nothing left to absorb. CockroachDB cannot afford that pause, because its clocks disagree by too much. So it hands the document over straight away, and occasionally a reader finds an entry it cannot place in time and has to start its work again."

7. What are these five things called, now that you have earned the words?

In one breath: Five terms, and every one of them names something you have already watched happen in the ledger office. The rest of the vocabulary arrives in section 10, at the moment it is needed.

What is a chapter of the book really called?

The book is too big for one town, so cut it up. Not into random handfuls — into chapters, each holding all the entries whose names fall between two bookmarks. /accounts/a–m is one chapter. /accounts/n–z is the next. Asha's row is in the first, Raj's in the second.

Engineers call a chapter a range.

Here is the precise version. CockroachDB stores the entire cluster as one sorted key-value map. That means every table, every index and every internal record, all in one map. It cuts that map into contiguous chunks. Each chunk is a range, and each range is replicated three times by default. Each range's copies form their own independent Raft group (raft).

A range is not a fixed size. It grows and shrinks with the data, and two numbers govern that. Say them in words first: split when a chapter gets too fat, merge when it gets too thin.

The rule in words The setting Its default
split this chapter in two when it grows past this size range_max_bytes 512 MiB
merge this chapter into its right-hand neighbour below this size range_min_bytes 128 MiB

Why does the word range exist at all, when you already have partitions? To distinguish this from the hash partitioning of consistent-hashing. A hash bucket holds keys that have nothing to do with each other. A range holds keys that are adjacent in sort order.

That is what makes WHERE id BETWEEN 100 AND 200 a single seek plus a scan, rather than a question asked of every machine in the cluster. The trade buys you ordered scans and costs you hot spots on sequential keys. Section 11 makes the cost concrete.

This is range partitioning with automatic splits, the shape you studied in partitioning-and-rebalancing. One consequence to hold onto: a transfer from Asha to Raj crosses two chapters, so it is a two-range transaction.

Who is allowed to answer, and why is it only one of them?

For each chapter, one of the three clerks is on duty. Only the duty clerk may answer questions about that chapter without consulting the others. The other two hold copies and stay quiet.

Engineers call that duty the range lease, and the clerk holding it the leaseholder.

One replica of each range holds the exclusive right, for a bounded time, to serve reads from its local copy and to sequence writes. Reads at the leaseholder need no consensus round at all. That is the difference between a read costing 1 ms and a read costing a cross-region round trip.

In the simulator, Arjun in Frankfurt holds the lease for /accounts/a–m. A client connected to Frankfurt reads Asha's balance in 1 ms. The same client reads Raj's balance in 60 ms, because Dev in Ohio holds that lease.

Why does the word need to exist next to "Raft leader", which sounds like the same job? Because it is not the same job, and the difference cost CockroachDB real production incidents.

Raft leadership is about who appends to the log. The lease is about who may answer without appending. Historically these could drift apart onto different replicas — a leader-leaseholder split. A leaseholder whose Raft log had fallen behind could render a range unavailable during a partition.

Since v25.2 the default is leader leases, which structurally fuse the two. The Raft leader is always the leaseholder, except briefly during a transfer.

A lease is also a fencing token, in exactly the sense of split-brain. Its entire job is to make it impossible for two machines to believe they are in charge at once.

What is a pencil mark in the ledger?

Asha's transfer touches two chapters. The clerks do not lock them. They write the new value into the book in pencil, with a note beside it saying which transaction it belongs to. Anyone who finds it then knows where to go and ask whether it counts.

Engineers call that pencil mark a write intent.

The documentation describes it precisely: a write intent is "a combination of a replicated lock and a replicated provisional value". It is an ordinary MVCC record carrying a pointer to a transaction record. It travels through Raft like any other write, so it survives machine death.

And it blocks nobody outright, because a reader that meets one can go and resolve it.

Why keep this idea separate from a lock? Because a lock fuses two things that are better apart. A lock says "you may not proceed." An intent says "here is the value, and here is where to find out whether it is real."

The second is strictly more useful. A reader holding an intent can finish the writer's transaction on its behalf. You met the ancestor of this idea as Percolator's staged lock in distributed-transactions; section 10.3 makes one change to it that matters enormously.

In scenario 1 of the simulator, /accounts/asha briefly carries an intent reading ₹1,500 @ ts 1000, pointing at transaction T1's record, before anything anywhere says COMMITTED.

Where is it written down whether a transaction counts?

Every pencil mark says "see master card #714." Card #714 lives in one place, and it is the only thing that decides whether the whole transaction counts. Fill it in and every pencil mark in the book counts at once — even before anyone has gone back to ink them in.

Engineers call the card a transaction record.

It is a single key-value pair. It lives on the range that contains the transaction's first written key, and that key has its own name: the anchor. The record's value is the transaction's status.

Read this table as the five states of card #714. The fifth is the one people forget.

The card says In words How it is decided
PENDING started, not yet finished the coordinator is still working
STAGING finished, if everything it listed got written section 10.3 — this is the clever one
COMMITTED it counts, permanently written down, or inferred from STAGING
ABORTED it does not count, permanently the coordinator gave up, or recovery killed it
no card at all nobody knows yet compare the intent's timestamp with a liveness threshold

Every intent in the transaction, on every range, points at this one record.

Why does the word need to exist? Because the record is what makes a multi-range commit atomic by construction rather than by protocol. There is no vote. There is no agreement about the outcome. There is one shard-local write that a hundred intents all defer to.

This is the "relocated commit point" from the distributed-transactions lab, and it is what deletes two-phase commit's blocking problem. Compare it with two-phase-commit, where the decision lived in a coordinator process's memory. Here it is replicated data, and Percolator's primary key is its direct ancestor.

In scenario 2, Frankfurt dies with the record stuck in STAGING. The record outlives its author, on a different machine, and a stranger reads the outcome off it.

What is a reader's window of doubt?

Everyone knows the watches disagree. So everyone agrees on one number in advance: no watch is more than half a second away from any other.

That number is a promise, not a measurement. And it hands the reader a rule. A clerk reading the book "as of 960" must treat every entry dated between 960 and 1460 as "I cannot tell whether this is in my past", and go and check.

Engineers call that band the uncertainty interval.

Said as a rule: a transaction reading at some timestamp treats every committed value stamped in the next half-second as ambiguous. Not visible. Not invisible. Unresolvable.

Written as notation, and only now that you have the picture:

The band in words The band written down The number it uses
from just after my read time, up to and including half a second later (t, t + max_offset] max_offset, default 500 ms
the cluster-wide promise about how far two clocks may differ the --max-offset startup flag 500 ms by default

Meeting an ambiguous value raises an error inside the database. In words it says "I read something I cannot place in time." Its name is ReadWithinUncertaintyIntervalError, and the transaction restarts at a higher timestamp.

Why does this word have to exist, when Spanner already has ε? Because it names the same quantity, relocated.

Spanner's ε CockroachDB's max_offset
Where the number comes from measured by the hardware declared by you
Which end of the transaction pays the writer, before answering the reader, on collision
What kind of statement it is a fact an assumption
What happens when it is wrong the wait is too short the window is too narrow

Same quantity, opposite ends, and one is a fact while the other is an assumption. Section 12 is largely about that difference.

Watch it work in scenario 3 at the default settings. Ben's read starts at 960 with a window ending at 1460. Asha's payment is stamped 1180, which is inside the window. So the read restarts at 1180⁺ and returns SETTLED.

Now drag the max-offset slider to 0. The same read returns PENDING — a wrong answer about money, with no error raised.

This is the "uncertainty restart" that hybrid-logical-clocks named and did not build. It is the same ε as google-spanner, with the opposite payer.

8. Which two pictures should you carry, and where does the nice one lie?

In one breath: Carry a simple picture and a precise one. The simple picture is three clerks, a book in chapters, pencil marks and master cards. It is accurate enough to reason with, and it lies to you in exactly four places.

The simple picture: three clerks and a book in chapters

The book is cut into chapters. Every chapter is photocopied to all three towns, so no town's fire destroys anything. For each chapter one clerk is on duty and may answer questions about it alone. The others hold copies and stay quiet.

To write a transaction you go round and put pencil marks in every chapter you are changing. Each mark says "see master card #714." Then you fill in card #714. Once card #714 says the transaction counts, every pencil mark counts, everywhere, at once — even before anyone has inked them in.

And every entry is dated by the duty clerk's own watch. Everyone knows the watches disagree, and everyone has agreed on one number: no watch is more than half a second from any other. So a clerk reading the book as of 960 treats every entry dated between 960 and 1460 as "I cannot tell whether this is in my past", and goes back to check.

Three small tables rather than one long one, so you can find the row you want. The right-hand column says where in this lab the thing does its work.

Where the story lies to you

Four places, and each one is load-bearing.

Lie one: that the duty clerk is elected by a show of hands about the data. A lease is not consensus about content. It is a bounded, fenced grant of authority.

Why that matters is split-brain's whole argument. A machine partitioned away from the others can still believe it is the duty clerk, and the design has to make that belief harmless. Leader leases do it by refusing to let leadership and duty ever be held by different machines.

Lie two: that the ink is what makes it real. "Pencil first, then ink" implies so. It is false.

In the real design, filling in the master card is not even the commit. The transaction is committed the instant the card says STAGING and every mark it lists has been agreed by a majority. The inking is bookkeeping done afterwards, by whoever happens to pass. Section 10.3 builds this, and it is the least intuitive idea in the lab.

Lie three: that chapters are fixed. Ranges split and merge continuously as data grows and shrinks, and the boundaries move under load. There is no moment at which the shape of the book is settled.

Lie four: that somebody knows the true time. The story tempts you to imagine a narrator who knows it is "really" 1000 when Asha pays.

The simulator has exactly such a narrator, drawn as the top lane of its timeline. No component of the real system does. Every mechanism in this lab is built out of relative comparisons between numbers that are all individually wrong.

10. How is the mechanism built, one failure at a time?

In one breath: Five parts. Each one starts with the version a sensible engineer would build, breaks it, and repairs it. Every odd detail of the real design is the scar of one of these breakages.

10.1 Where does a row actually live?

Asha (a row in the ledger) has to live on some machine. Which one?

The first design anybody writes hashes the primary key and sends the row to whichever machine the hash points at. That is consistent-hashing. It spreads load perfectly, and it is what Cassandra and DynamoDB do.

It breaks on the second query you will ever write. SELECT … WHERE id BETWEEN 100 AND 200 has to be sent to every machine, because a hash scatters adjacent keys deliberately. So does ORDER BY id LIMIT 10. So does any range scan on any index. You have made single-key lookups perfect and everything else a fan-out.

The repair is to keep the keys in order. CockroachDB stores the whole cluster as one sorted key-value map and cuts it into contiguous ranges. Adjacent keys are adjacent bytes, in the same range, on the same machine, in the same LSM tree (btree-and-lsm).

A SQL row becomes key-value pairs by an encoding worth seeing once, because two production problems fall straight out of it. In words: the table's number, then the index's number, then the indexed values — and the rest of the row is what that points at.

   /<table_id>/<index_id>/<indexed column values>  →  <the remaining column values>

The primary key always uses index number 1, written in the encoding as index_id = 1. Take a row of accounts, which is table 106 here. Its primary key is 'asha' and its columns are (balance, tier). It comes out roughly like this:

   /106/1/"asha"   →   (2000, "gold")

A secondary index on tier, which is index number 2, is another entry in the same map:

   /106/2/"gold"/"asha"   →   —

Three consequences follow immediately. You should be able to derive each one from that single line of encoding.

What follows from the encoding Why Where it bites
a range scan is a sequential read every row of accounts between 'a' and 'm' is contiguous bytes makes ordered SQL cheap
a secondary index is just more rows in the same map, usually in a different range it is a separate entry with its own sort position one insert with two indexes is a multi-range transaction, so 10.3's saved round trip matters
a monotonically increasing primary key is a design fault, not a preference every new row sorts to the end of the map every insert hits the same range, one leaseholder, one machine — section 11.6

How does a client find the range it wants? With a two-level index, held inside the map itself. The first level, meta1, tells you which second-level record to consult. The second, meta2, addresses every user range.

The location of meta1 is spread to every node by gossip (gossip-protocols), and every node caches the range descriptors it has used. Two levels address up to 4 EiB, so a lookup is at most two hops and usually zero.

🔮 Predict: Ranges split when they grow past 512 MiB and merge when they shrink below 128 MiB. Why are those two numbers so far apart, rather than both being, say, 256 MiB?

Reveal

Because a single threshold would make the system oscillate. A range sitting right on the line would split, and each half would immediately be under the line, so they would merge, and the merged range would be over the line again.

The gap between 128 MiB and 512 MiB is deliberate slack — engineers call it hysteresis. A range has to lose three quarters of its data before a merge is even considered.

If you guessed "512 is for performance and 128 is for storage", you are thinking about the absolute values rather than the distance between them. The distance is the point.

10.2 Who is allowed to answer a read?

The obvious first design is to read through Raft. Every read is proposed to the range's Raft group and answered once a majority has agreed. This is obviously correct, and it is what a first implementation does.

It breaks on cost. Every read now costs a consensus round — including the 95% of your traffic that is reads. Across regions that is tens of milliseconds. You have built a correct database with the read latency of a write.

The repair is the range lease. One replica holds the exclusive right to serve reads locally for a bounded period. Reads become a local lookup. Arjun in Frankfurt answers about /accounts/a–m without telephoning anybody.

The leaseholder also sequences writes, which is what makes the timestamp cache in 10.5 possible at all.

And that repair broke, in production, for years. The lease and the Raft leadership were separate grants and could land on different replicas — the leader-leaseholder split from section 7. A leaseholder whose Raft log had fallen behind the others would keep answering reads while being unable to make progress on writes. Under a network partition this could leave a range unavailable indefinitely.

The second repair is leader leases, the default since v25.2. The Raft leader is always the leaseholder, except briefly during a deliberate transfer.

That change also removed a structural single point of failure. The older epoch-based leases depended on one cluster-wide node liveness range that every node heartbeated. Leader leases replaced it with store-wide failure detection. Heartbeating still exists for cluster membership, death detection and replica placement — it is simply no longer on the critical path of holding a lease.

A third kind, expiration-based leases, still covers system ranges. They expire after a few seconds and are renewed by continued Raft activity.

Three kinds of lease, then, and it is worth keeping them apart.

Kind of lease How it stays alive Where it is used now
leader lease held by whoever is Raft leader, by construction the default since v25.2, for user ranges
epoch-based lease a cluster-wide node liveness range, heartbeated legacy; the previous default
expiration-based lease a few seconds, renewed by Raft activity system ranges

🔮 Predict: The leaseholder answers reads from its own copy without asking anybody. A stale or partitioned leaseholder could therefore serve an old value forever. What stops it?

Reveal

The lease is time-bounded, and this is exactly the fencing argument from split-brain. A leaseholder may only answer reads while its lease is valid; a partitioned one runs out and stops. A new leaseholder cannot be granted until the old lease has provably expired, so two machines can never both believe they are on duty at once.

The safety property does not come from the leaseholder being well-behaved. It comes from time bounding what a misbehaving one can do. Which is the same trick as the uncertainty interval, applied to authority instead of to data.

If you guessed "the other replicas would correct it", you have imagined a conversation that never happens. The whole point of a lease is that no conversation is needed.

10.3 How does a transaction write — and why is the commit not an event?

Take the transfer from scenario 1. Move ₹500 from Asha to Raj. Asha's row is in /accounts/a–m, whose leaseholder is Arjun in Frankfurt. Raj's row is in /accounts/n–z, whose leaseholder is Dev in Ohio. Two ranges, two leaseholders, 60 ms apart.

The design you already know is Percolator, from distributed-transactions. Stage the writes as intents. Then flip one designated record from pending to committed. Let secondaries clean up lazily.

CockroachDB's intents and transaction record are exactly Percolator's lock and primary, generalised — the record is its own key rather than one of the data keys.

Here is the trace, from the simulator with parallel commits off. Read the last line first.

t=1000   BEGIN on Frankfurt · read_ts = HLC(Frankfurt) = 1000
t=1001   GET /accounts/asha  @ Frankfurt (local, 1 ms)  → ₹2,000 @ ts 200
t=1061   GET /accounts/raj   @ Ohio (60 ms)             → ₹500   @ ts 300
t=1061   PUT intent /accounts/asha = ₹1,500 @ 1000
         PUT intent /accounts/raj  = ₹1,000 @ 1000       ← round 1 of consensus
t=1121   both intents agreed by majorities
t=1181   PUT txn record PENDING → COMMITTED              ← round 2 of consensus
         client acknowledged.  Elapsed: 181 ms

It breaks on the second round. Look at what was already true at t=1121. Both values are durable on majorities. Nothing can undo them. No participant can change its mind.

The outcome was already forced. And yet the client waits another full cross-region round trip while the coordinator writes down a conclusion nobody could have contradicted.

It gets worse with schema. Every secondary index entry is another key, often on another range. A wide table's insert pays this on more ranges.

The repair is parallel commits, and it works by redefining what "committed" means. The documentation puts it exactly:

"A transaction is considered committed if its record is in the STAGING state and an observer can prove that all of the writes listed in its transaction record have successfully achieved consensus."

Read that twice, because it is the whole trick. Committed stops being an event someone performs and becomes a condition anyone can check.

So the coordinator writes the record at the same time as the intents, not after them. The record goes in as STAGING, and it lists the keys it is waiting on:

   TransactionRecord{ Status: STAGING, Writes: []Key{"/accounts/asha", "/accounts/raj"} }

The same trace with parallel commits on:

t=1000   BEGIN on Frankfurt · read_ts = 1000
t=1001   GET /accounts/asha  → ₹2,000 @ ts 200
t=1061   GET /accounts/raj   → ₹500   @ ts 300
t=1061   PUT intent /accounts/asha, PUT intent /accounts/raj,
         PUT txn record {STAGING, in-flight:[asha, raj]}   ← all three, in parallel
t=1121   every one of them agreed → the commit condition holds → transaction IS committed
         client acknowledged.  Elapsed: 121 ms
t=1126   record STAGING → COMMITTED; intents resolved to values   ← off the critical path

121 ms instead of 181 — exactly one cross-region round trip saved. The simulator asserts that identity across its configurations.

Cockroach Labs' own claim is the qualitative version of the same thing. Without parallel commits, client-perceived latency grows at twice the rate of the round-trip time. With them, at exactly the rate of the round-trip time.

Parallel commits Rounds of consensus on the critical path The client's wait How latency grows with round-trip time
off 2 181 ms at twice the rate
on 1 121 ms at exactly the rate

🔮 Predict: Pick the moment t=1122. The client has been acknowledged. Nothing anywhere says COMMITTED. Neither pencil mark has been inked. At exactly that moment the coordinator machine catches fire. Is the money moved?

Reveal

Yes, definitively. Not "probably", not "a human must decide". The commit condition was satisfied at t=1121, and it is a property of data replicated on majorities. Nothing about the coordinator's continued existence was ever part of it.

The next reader to touch either key finds an intent and follows it to a STAGING record. It checks the keys that record lists, finds them all present, and concludes the transaction committed. Then it does the tidying itself. Run scenario 2 to watch a stranger finish someone else's transaction.

Compare this with two-phase-commit, where a coordinator dying between prepare and commit leaves participants holding locks with no way to learn the outcome. And with XA in the JVM, where the resolution is a human being inventing a heuristic outcome for an in-doubt transaction.

The blocking problem is not solved here. It is deleted, by making the outcome a fact in the data rather than a message from a process. If you guessed "it depends whether the coordinator logged anything", you are still imagining the decision living in a process.

And what if one of the listed writes is missing? Then somebody has to decide, and the decision must be permanent. Two readers running recovery a moment apart must not reach different conclusions.

So when recovery finds a listed key without an agreed intent, it does not simply abort. It first makes that write impossible forever. It does that by raising the barrier on that key, which is the timestamp cache of 10.5, above the transaction's timestamp. Only then does it mark the record ABORTED.

Any straggling attempt to complete that write now fails. The same procedure produces both outcomes and both are atomic. Scenario 2's final step walks the abort branch.

10.4 How does a reader avoid a stale answer?

This is the part that is unique to CockroachDB, and it is stage 3 of the simulator. It is also Asha and Ben's problem from section 2, finally solved.

Every version in the store is stamped with a hybrid logical clock value. That is the timestamp you built in hybrid-logical-clocks: a wall-clock reading plus a logical counter. In CockroachDB the pair is an int64 of nanoseconds plus an int32. A transaction takes its read timestamp from the clock of the node its client connected to.

The obvious first design is to read at your own now. Show every version stamped at or below your read timestamp. This is textbook MVCC, and on one machine it is exactly right.

It breaks on the story in section 2. Here is the trace, from the simulator, at the defaults, with the doubt set to zero. Meera is Mumbai, Dev is Ohio.

t=1000  Mumbai (clock +180) commits Asha's payment.  commit_ts = 1180
t=1060  Asha is acknowledged
t=1070  Asha telephones Ben
t=1080  Ohio (clock −120) begins Ben's read.  read_ts = 960
t=1141  value ts 1180 > read_ts 960  →  "in my future"  →  invisible
        Ben is told: PENDING                                  ← STALE READ

The repair is to refuse to answer when you cannot tell. Give the reader an uncertainty interval: from just after its read timestamp, up to half a second later. A value inside it is neither visible nor invisible. It is unresolvable, and returning either answer might be a lie.

So the read raises ReadWithinUncertaintyIntervalError and restarts at a timestamp just above the offending value. Same trace, with the doubt at its default of 500 ms:

t=1080  read_ts = 960 · uncertainty interval = (960, 1460]
t=1141  value ts 1180 ∈ (960, 1460]  →  ambiguous  →  ReadWithinUncertaintyIntervalError
        restart at 1180⁺ · ceiling stays 1460 · window is now 280 ms wide, not 500
t=1142  value ts 1180 ≤ read_ts 1180⁺  →  visible
        Ben is told: SETTLED                    ← correct, and it cost one round trip

And that repair looks like it might never terminate. A busy cluster is writing all the time. If every value in the window costs a restart, a long report could restart forever.

Two brakes stop it, and they are independent.

Brake one: the ceiling never rises. On restart the read timestamp moves up. The ceiling does not. It was computed once, when the transaction began. So every restart strictly shrinks the remaining doubt. The documentation states it directly: "the upper bound of the uncertainty interval does not change on restart, so the window of uncertainty shrinks."

Watch it in the trace above. The window went from 500 ms wide to 280 ms wide, because its left edge moved and its right edge did not.

Brake two: observed timestamps. When a transaction first contacts a node, it writes down what that node's clock read at that moment. Any value on that node stamped above that reading must have been written after the transaction spoke to it. So it is provably in the transaction's future, not merely possibly. No restart.

The consequence is a hard bound. A transaction can be forced to restart at most once per node, and never by more than the width of its uncertainty interval.

Scenario 4 is that bound, made visible. Same report, same four rows, one switch.

Observed timestamps Restarts What the report saw for /payments/p2
on, the default 1 the older version, PENDING @ 580
off 2 the newer version, REFUNDED @ 1285

Read the second column carefully, because it is a subtlety people miss. The optimisation does not only make things faster. It changes which snapshot you get.

Both answers are serializable. They are different, equally valid points in the transaction order. Nothing about correctness depends on which one you get — and that is precisely why the optimisation is allowed to exist.

🔮 Predict: Dev's clock in Ohio is slow, so writes made in Ohio carry small timestamps and look older than they are. Does that hurt Ohio's own readers?

Reveal

No — and working out why is the best test of whether the mechanism has landed. A slow clock backdates its writes, so they fall below other readers' read timestamps and are plainly visible. Doubt only arises when a value is stamped above the reader's timestamp, which needs the writer's clock to be ahead of the reader's.

Scenario 4 has a row exactly like this. It is Raj's, written on Ohio at true time 900 and stamped 780, and it causes no drama at all.

The direction matters enormously for a different reason. A fast clock is the dangerous one: it invents timestamps in the future, and future timestamps are what hide from readers. This is why the failure in 11.3 is asymmetric, and why the condition derived there involves the difference between the writer's and the reader's offsets, not the absolute error of either.

If you guessed "yes, a slow clock hides its own writes", you have the direction reversed. A slow clock over-shares; a fast clock hides.

10.5 How is it serializable without ever blocking a reader?

Snapshot isolation comes free with MVCC, and it is not enough. isolation-levels counted exactly what it lets through. distributed-transactions showed that write skew survives distribution completely untouched. So something has to close it.

The textbook answer is to lock what you read — distributed strict two-phase locking. It is correct. It also means readers queue behind writers and writers behind readers, across regions, and it inherits two-phase commit's blocking.

Rejected for the same reason as everything else in this lab: it makes an ordinary operation wait for a remote machine.

The real repair has three moving parts.

Part one — reads leave a mark. Each leaseholder keeps a timestamp cache in memory. For each span of keys it records the highest timestamp at which anybody has read it.

When a write arrives, its timestamp is checked against that mark. A write is never allowed to slip in underneath a read that already happened. If it would, the write's whole transaction is pushed: its commit timestamp moves up above the mark. Nobody waits. The write simply gets a later position in time than it asked for.

Part two — being pushed creates a debt. A transaction that read at 890 and now claims to commit at 1000⁺ is claiming its reads were valid at 1000⁺ too.

Under SERIALIZABLE that has to be proved, by a read refresh: re-check every span the transaction read for changes in the gap it was pushed across.

The gap in words The gap written down
from the timestamp the transaction read at, up to and including the one it was pushed to (old_ts, new_ts]

Clean means the push was free. Dirty means the transaction cannot be salvaged, and the client gets RETRY_SERIALIZABLE, which is Omar's SQLSTATE 40001 from section 6.

Part three — Omar's code retries. This is not an error path. It is the contract.

Scenario 5 is the canonical case, traced by the simulator. Two doctors, and a rule that at least one must stay on call.

t=1000  T_A on Frankfurt reads both doctors at 1000 · both ON CALL
        T_B on Ohio      reads both doctors at  890 · both ON CALL
        timestamp cache now marks both rows as read at 1000
t=1200  T_A writes dr_mehta = OFF @ 1000 · nobody else read it higher · COMMITTED
t=1260  T_B writes dr_qureshi = OFF @ 890
        timestamp cache[dr_qureshi] = 1000 ≥ 890  →  push T_B's commit_ts to 1000⁺
t=1265  T_B was pushed → refresh its reads over (890, 1000⁺]
        dr_mehta changed at 1000, inside the gap  →  refresh FAILED
        RETRY_SERIALIZABLE (40001)
t=1330  T_B retried at 1000⁺ → reads dr_mehta = OFF → the application refuses

One doctor stays on call, and not one lock was taken on a reader.

Turn off the re-check reads when pushed switch and the same trace ends with both doctors off call. That is snapshot isolation. It is also why READ COMMITTED transactions in CockroachDB explicitly do not perform read refreshing. That isolation level has been generally available since v24.1, but it is not the default.

Two more pieces complete the concurrency picture. They are named here because you will meet them in error messages.

The piece What it does How long it is held
latch manager sequences requests inside one replica so two operations on the same key do not interleave; many read latches coexist, a write latch excludes one request — these are not locks
lock table the per-node, in-memory structure holding locks in-progress transactions actually acquired: SELECT … FOR UPDATE, and the locks discovered behind write intents one transaction, each with a wait queue
TxnWaitQueue kept on the range's Raft leader; tracks transactions that tried to push another and could not until the push resolves — and it is where distributed deadlocks are broken

Notice that the timestamp cache is doing two unrelated jobs with one structure. It enforces serializability by pushing writes above reads. And it is the barrier that parallel-commit recovery raises to kill a missing write for good.

That economy is worth remembering, because it also explains a failure in section 11. The structure is in memory only, and never replicated.

🔮 Predict: Both T_A and T_B did their work honestly, and each wrote a row the other never wrote. Why is refusing one of them necessary, rather than an over-cautious database being unhelpful?

Reveal

Because the conflict is not between two writes. It is between one transaction's write and another transaction's read.

T_B decided it was safe to take Dr Qureshi off call because it had read that Dr Mehta was on call. T_A then changed exactly that fact. So T_B's decision rests on a reading that is no longer true at the moment T_B wants to commit. Serializability says a transaction's whole set of reads must be valid at one single point in the order — and T_B's are not.

This is write skew, and it is the anomaly snapshot isolation cannot see. The two rows never collide; the reasoning does.

If you guessed "the database should merge them, since they wrote different rows", you are treating serializability as a property of writes. It is a property of whole transactions, reads included.

11. What are the nine ways it fails, and what survives each one?

In one breath: Nine ways this design gets hurt. Eight of them are loud — something errors, something is slow, a dashboard turns red. One of them is silent, and that is the one to be frightened of.

Meet Priya. She is the on-call engineer for this cluster, and she is the one who gets telephoned. For each failure below, the last thing said is what Priya actually sees.

11.1 What happens when the leaseholder dies?

Create it with scenario 6, or by killing a node in a real cluster with kill -9.

The range itself is barely inconvenienced. Three replicas, two survivors, still a majority. What dies with the machine is the timestamp cache — the record of who read what, which lives only in the leaseholder's memory and is never replicated.

The surviving replicas elect a new leader, which under leader leases immediately becomes the new leaseholder. And that new leaseholder has no idea what anybody read before its lease began.

It cannot afford to assume nothing was read. So it assumes everything was. It installs a low-water mark at the moment its lease started, and refuses any write from a transaction older than that.

t=1000  T_X reads /oncall/dr_mehta at 1000 from Frankfurt (mark recorded in RAM only)
t=1050  Frankfurt dies
t=1410  Mumbai wins the election, takes the lease, sets low-water mark = 1590
t=1470  T_X tries to write → 1590 > 1000 → ABORT_REASON_NEW_LEASE_PREVENTS_TXN
t=1550  T_X retried from BEGIN → COMMITTED

Serializability holds throughout, and no incorrect answer was ever returned. Nothing bends — but liveness for that range pauses for the length of the lease handover, and in-flight transactions touching it are destroyed.

Priya sees a brief spike in 40001s correlated with a node restart, plus a range-unavailability blip that resolves itself.

This is the single best argument for why Omar's retry loop is mandatory. A rolling restart is the most ordinary event in cluster operation, and it throws away healthy transactions.

11.2 What happens when the coordinator dies mid-commit?

Create it with scenario 2. The mechanism is covered fully in 10.3.

The property worth naming here is that the recovery procedure is idempotent and convergent. Two different readers running it at the same moment on the same STAGING record reach the same conclusion. Whichever gets there first makes its conclusion permanent before anyone can contradict it.

Atomicity and durability both survive, on both branches. Nothing bends.

Priya sees nothing at all. There is nothing to page about.

Contrast that with XA and JTA in the JVM tier. The same event there produces an in-doubt transaction and a human being inventing a heuristic outcome.

11.3 What happens when a clock drifts past the budget?

Create it with scenario 7. Selecting it moves Meera's clock in Mumbai to +600 ms and Dev's in Ohio to −150 ms. Then toggle machines shut themselves down.

This is the failure that matters most, because it is the one the whole design is conditional on.

With the self-check on, Meera's node finds it disagrees with its peers by more than 80% of the 500 ms budget, and terminates itself. Its leases move. The payment is stamped by an honest clock. Ben's read restarts and returns SETTLED.

With the self-check off:

t=1000  Mumbai (clock +600) commits Asha's payment.  commit_ts = 1600
t=1060  Asha acknowledged
t=1080  Ohio (clock −150) reads.  read_ts = 930 · uncertainty limit = 1430
        value ts 1600 > 1430  →  "in my future"  →  invisible
        Ben is told: PENDING     ← wrong, with no error, no warning, no log line

The condition for this is worth stating in words before you meet it as symbols. A stale read is possible exactly when the writer's clock is ahead of the reader's by more than the declared budget plus however much real time passed between them.

The simulator verifies that exhaustively over 1,344 combinations of the two clock offsets, the budget and the network latency.

The condition in words Written down
the writer's clock error, minus the reader's clock error, exceeds the declared budget plus the real time between the two stampings off_writer − off_reader > max_offset + Δ
the two clock errors, which are the only terms that matter off_writer and off_reader
the real time between the writer taking its timestamp and the reader taking its own Δ

Three things fall out of that inequality, and they are worth more than the mechanism itself.

One: only the difference between two clocks matters. Every clock in the cluster being an hour fast is completely harmless.

This is the same shape as the condition derived in the Spanner lab, which also depended on the difference of two admissions rather than on any clock being right.

The Spanner condition in words Written down
the gap between the two events is smaller than the first clock's admitted error, minus the second's, minus the pause g < ε₁ − ε₂ − P

Two: Δ helps you, so a faster system is a more exposed one. Slow networks and slow readers give the inequality more slack. Improve your latency and you shrink your own safety margin.

This is the third time you have met that inversion. The Spanner lab's version was "making Paxos faster makes the bug more likely."

Three: lowering --max-offset to reduce restart latency is buying performance with correctness margin. Cockroach Labs recommend 250 ms for clusters using the multi-region SQL abstractions. That recommendation is a statement about how good you believe your clocks are, and nothing in the database will check it for you.

Serializability survives — the history is still equivalent to some serial order. Promise A, no stale reads, is lost.

And what Priya sees is the point: nothing. There is no error, no metric that moves, no log line. The only thing that moved was the clock-offset gauge.

That is why the gauge must be treated as a correctness alarm rather than a performance one. A latency incident with flat CPU, flat disk, flat network and a raised clock-offset gauge is a time incident.

This is the third sighting of the same conditional bound in this curriculum. hybrid-logical-clocks clocked one +60 ms node dragging a ±8 ms cluster's bound from 16 to 67. The google-spanner lab watched external consistency die with commit wait switched on.

🔮 Predict: The self-check evicts a node at 80% of the budget rather than at 100%. Why leave 20% on the table, when the guarantee is only broken at 100%?

Reveal

Because eviction is not instantaneous, and drift does not stop while it happens. The node has to notice, decide, shut down, and have its leases moved elsewhere. Every one of those steps takes time, and the clock keeps running away during all of them.

The 20% margin is the budget for that removal. It exists so that removal happens before the promise breaks rather than after. A check at 100% would fire at the exact moment the first wrong answer became possible, which is already too late.

If you guessed "to reduce false positives", you have it backwards — a tighter threshold means more evictions, not fewer. The margin costs availability to buy correctness.

11.4 What happens during a network partition?

Create it by cutting one region off from the others.

A range makes progress only on the side holding a majority of its replicas. The minority side cannot elect a leader, cannot acquire a lease, and therefore cannot serve reads or writes for ranges whose majority is elsewhere.

It does not serve stale data. It serves nothing.

Clients on that side see requests hang. Then a per-replica circuit breaker trips and returns ReplicaUnavailableError instead of hanging indefinitely.

The setting in words The setting Its default
how long a replica may be stuck before it starts refusing instead of hanging kv.replica_circuit_breaker.slow_replication_threshold 1m0s

Every guarantee survives. What is lost is availability on the minority side.

Priya sees unavailable ranges on the minority and normal service on the majority. This is textbook CP by cap-theorem and PC/EC by pacelc — no surprises, and the good news is that the failure is loud.

11.5 What happens to a contended row?

Create it with many transactions writing one row: a counter, a merchant's balance, a sequence.

Follow the chain. Every writer's read leaves a mark. Every writer's write is pushed above someone else's mark. Every push forces a refresh. Refreshes that meet a change fail with 40001.

Throughput does not degrade gracefully. It collapses, because the work is being done and then thrown away.

Correctness survives entirely. What is lost is throughput — and it is exactly the optimistic-versus-pessimistic trade you priced in optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking and counted in distributed-transactions.

Approach Aborts, in that lab's run What the aborts were
Percolator, optimistic 255 conflict aborts
two-phase locking, pessimistic 632 timeout aborts

Priya sees a rising RETRY_SERIALIZABLE rate, and crdb_internal.cluster_contention_events pointing at one index.

Four fixes, in order of preference.

  1. Send the statements in one batch, so the whole transaction is one round trip.
  2. Take the conflict pessimistically up front with SELECT … FOR UPDATE, which converts a late refusal into an early wait.
  3. Split the hot row into per-shard rows and sum them.
  4. Stop writing it that often.

11.6 What happens with a sequential primary key?

Create it with id SERIAL PRIMARY KEY, or with a primary key beginning with created_at.

This falls straight out of the encoding in 10.1. Every new row sorts to the end of the map. So every insert in the cluster lands in the last range, which has one leaseholder, on one machine. You have bought a distributed database and are using one node of it.

Load-based splitting will split the range. But the new last range immediately becomes the new hot spot. The problem regenerates itself.

Every guarantee survives. What is lost is the entire point of the cluster.

Priya sees one node at 90% CPU while the others idle, and the Hot Ranges page naming a single range.

11.7 What happens when a commit is ambiguous?

Create it by killing the client's connection at the exact moment of acknowledgement. Or have a lease transfer interrupt a write in flight, which surfaces as RETRY_ASYNC_WRITE_FAILURE.

The transaction may or may not have committed, and the client cannot tell. This is not a CockroachDB defect. It is a property of every network.

Every guarantee survives — the database knows the answer perfectly well. Omar's client does not.

Priya sees duplicate charges, if the application is careless.

The fix is Omar's. His application must be able to ask "did this already happen?" and get a definitive answer. That is exactly idempotency's idempotency key, and exactly the discipline the retry loop already demands.

Notice how the two requirements coincide. A transaction body that is safe to retry after a 40001 is very nearly a transaction body that is safe to retry after an ambiguous result.

11.8 What happens to a long report versus garbage collection?

Create it with an analytical query, or an AS OF SYSTEM TIME read, older than the retention window.

Old MVCC versions are garbage-collected after a configured age. A read below that horizon fails, because the versions it needs are gone.

The setting in words The setting Its default
how long an overwritten version is kept before collection gc.ttlseconds 14400 seconds — four hours

Every guarantee survives. What is lost is the ability to read the past that far back.

Priya sees batch timestamp … must be after replica GC threshold.

Two fixes. Raise gc.ttlseconds for the tables in question and pay for the storage. Or use protected timestamps, which is what backups and changefeeds (change-data-capture) use to pin the horizon while they work.

Note the related ceiling from the other end. RETRY_COMMIT_DEADLINE_EXCEEDED is what a transaction gets when it has been pushed forward by other transactions for so long that it would outlive the closed-timestamp horizon.

12. What is guaranteed, what is not, and who paid?

In one breath: Three promises kept, one declined on purpose, and a bill. Everything on the list is conditional on one number that you declared and nobody checks.

What do you get?

Serializability, and only serializability, by default. Every history is equivalent to some serial order of its transactions.

READ COMMITTED exists, and must be asked for explicitly. It has been generally available since v24.1, gated by sql.txn.read_committed_isolation.enabled. SERIALIZABLE remains the default, which is unusual among SQL databases and is a deliberate position.

No stale reads. Once a write transaction has committed, every read transaction starting afterwards sees it. This is the promise the uncertainty interval exists to buy, and it is the one that makes the cluster feel like one machine. It is Asha's payment being visible to Ben.

Atomicity across any number of ranges, at one round of consensus, with no blocking window in which a coordinator's death leaves anybody stuck.

Durability against the loss of a minority of replicas, from Raft, with no special casing anywhere in the layers above.

What do you not get?

Strict serializability. Cockroach Labs say so plainly: "CockroachDB doesn't quite offer strict serializability, but we're fairly close to it". They also say this: "even though CRDB guarantees 'no stale reads', it still can produce transaction histories that are not linearizable."

The specific hole is the causal reverse, and it is scenario 8. Here is the trace, at default clock settings, with nothing faulty. Ben is running the audit.

t=600   an audit begins on Frankfurt.  read_ts = 600 · ceiling = 1100  (fixed, for life)
t=670   audit reads invoice 88 → UNPAID @ ts 100
t=1000  Mumbai (clock +180) marks invoice 88 PAID.  commit_ts = 1180
t=1060  ...and acknowledges the client
t=1080  a person sees that confirmation and, because of it,
        Ohio (clock −120) raises Raj's credit limit.  commit_ts = 960
t=1140  ...and acknowledges
t=1220  audit reads Raj's row → ts 960 is inside its window → restart at 960⁺ → sees ₹5,000
t=1260  audit re-reads invoice 88 → ts 1180 > ceiling 1100 → "in my future" → skipped
        The audit reports: invoice UNPAID, credit limit ₹5,000

Ben's audit has observed an effect without its cause. Marking the invoice paid was acknowledged at real time 1060. Raising the limit did not begin until 1080. Real time says one strictly preceded the other. The database's order says the opposite, and the audit reports it.

Three things about this deserve emphasis, because it is easy to over-react and easy to under-react.

Nothing is broken. Every clock is inside its budget. There is a perfectly valid serial order for these three: raise the limit, run the audit, mark the invoice paid. Every transaction's reads are consistent with that order. Serializability holds exactly.

It takes three transactions, and two of them must share no data. Had the Ohio transaction so much as read the invoice row, its own uncertainty interval would have caught the PAID write and pushed it above. The anomaly needs a pair of transactions with disjoint read and write sets, plus a third that overlaps both. It is impossible when the transactions touch common data, or when they run on the same node.

No stale read occurred. The audit started at real time 600, long before the invoice write was acknowledged at 1060. Promise A only ever covered transactions that begin after an acknowledgement, and this one did not.

Everything above is conditional on --max-offset being true. This is the honest boundary, and it is worth stating as bluntly as possible.

The correctness argument contains no term for how accurate your clocks actually are. It contains a term for how accurate you declared them to be. Exceed the declaration and the guarantee ends silently, with a wrong answer and no error — scenario 7 with the self-check off.

The 80%-of-budget self-termination rule is what makes the assumption self-enforcing rather than merely hopeful. The 20% margin exists so that removal happens before the promise breaks rather than after.

What was traded away, and to whom did the bill go?

Cost Who pays it When
Uncertainty restarts the reader only when it meets a value in its window
Pushes and read refreshes the writer only under contention
SQLSTATE 40001 retry loops Omar, the application developer always, as a design constraint
Cross-region consensus latency on every write to a range whose majority is remote
Strict serializability given up except for GLOBAL tables, in 13.3

That third row is the one people underestimate.

Spanner's read-write transactions are pessimistic and use wound-wait, so aborts are normal there too. But in CockroachDB the retry loop is load-bearing in a wider set of ordinary situations, including a rolling restart from 11.1.

The transaction body must be re-runnable. That is a constraint the database imposes on Omar's code, and it is the real price of the optimistic posture in section 6.

Safety and liveness, separated

Two different kinds of promise, and it pays to know which one you are relying on.

What it means What it rests on here
Safety nothing wrong is ever observed Raft majorities for durability; the lease as a fencing token; the timestamp cache plus read refreshes for serializability; and the uncertainty interval for no-stale-reads
Liveness progress eventually happens a majority of each range being reachable; clocks staying inside the budget so nodes do not evict themselves; contention low enough that retries converge

Only one item in that whole table has a hardware assumption inside it: the uncertainty interval, which is conditional on the clock budget. Everything else is a property of software.

Under a partition, liveness is what is sacrificed — loudly, on the minority side.

13.1 How far is the toy from the real thing?

13.2 What shape is a real deployment?

Three nodes minimum, because three is the smallest number that can form a majority of three.

The replication factor is 3 by default, written num_replicas = 3, and system ranges get 5. Tolerated failures are one less than the replication factor, halved and rounded down: (replication factor − 1) / 2. So 3 replicas survive 1 loss and 5 survive 2.

Here is the default zone configuration. Three of its four numbers explain a great deal of behaviour, and it is worth memorising.

ALTER RANGE default CONFIGURE ZONE USING
    range_min_bytes = 134217728,   -- 128 MiB: merge below this
    range_max_bytes = 536870912,   -- 512 MiB: split above this
    gc.ttlseconds   = 14400,       --   4 hours of MVCC history
    num_replicas    = 3;

Read those four names as four questions Priya is answering.

The setting The question it answers

range_max_bytes when is a chapter too fat to leave whole?
gc.ttlseconds how far into the past may anyone read?
num_replicas how many copies of each chapter?

Non-voting replicas are a fifth idea, available since v21.1 and configured by num_voters alongside num_replicas. They follow the Raft log without participating in the majority.

That gives a distant region local read capacity without enlarging the write quorum. It is the same idea as Spanner's read-only replicas, under a different name.

13.3 Where does the bill flip back to the writer?

CockroachDB exposes three table localities. They are worth understanding as three different answers to one question: where should the leaseholder be?

Locality Where the leaseholders live Best for
REGIONAL BY TABLE one named region, for the whole table the default; fast there, a round trip away everywhere else
REGIONAL BY ROW wherever each row's hidden region column says a customer's rows living where the customer is — the main tool against 11.9
GLOBAL everywhere, effectively read-mostly reference data — and it inverts the entire lab

That third row needs spelling out, because it is CockroachDB doing precisely what Spanner does, in one table.

The leaseholder closes timestamps a few hundred milliseconds in the future. A writer arriving at a GLOBAL table is told the present is already set in stone, and its only option is to write above the present. Having done so, it must commit-wait — hold its acknowledgement until real time has caught up with the timestamp it used. So once the client is told the write succeeded, any subsequent read is guaranteed to have a high enough timestamp to see it. Because timestamps are closed far enough ahead, readers never block on a contending writer at all.

That is commit-wait, in CockroachDB, on commodity clocks.

Now look back at the four-row table from the google-spanner lab — writer pays, reader pays, network pays, client pays. It turns out not to be a fixed property of a database at all. It is a per-table dial.

CockroachDB's default is reader-pays. GLOBAL is writer-pays. You choose per table, according to your read/write ratio. That is the single most useful idea to carry out of this lab.

One tuning knob to know. For clusters whose round-trip times exceed 150 ms, the setting that controls how far ahead timestamps are closed should be raised.

The knob in words The setting What to do
how far in the future a GLOBAL table closes its timestamps kv.closed_timestamp.lead_for_global_reads_override raise above 800 ms, in 100 ms steps
the point at which you stop tuning and ask for help the same setting 1500 ms — talk to support instead

Survival goals are the orthogonal axis. SURVIVE ZONE FAILURE is the default and the cheaper one. SURVIVE REGION FAILURE requires at least three regions, and places voting replicas so that an entire region can vanish.

13.4 How do you read the past on purpose?

The same closed-timestamp machinery gives you cheap historical reads.

Each range tracks a closed timestamp: a moment below which no new write can ever appear. Once a follower replica has applied everything up to it, that follower can serve reads at or below it without holding the lease.

The setting in words The setting Its default
how far behind the present the closed timestamp aims to sit kv.closed_timestamp.target_duration 3s
how often an idle range publishes a fresh closed timestamp with no traffic kv.closed_timestamp.side_transport_interval 200ms

Three ways to ask for the past.

How you ask What it means The catch
AS OF SYSTEM TIME follower_read_timestamp() far enough back to be very likely served by a local follower the docs recommend at least 4.2 seconds in the past, to reduce the chance of blocking on a conflicting write
AS OF SYSTEM TIME '-1h' exact staleness — you name the moment subject to the four-hour garbage-collection horizon
with_max_staleness(INTERVAL) or with_min_timestamp(TIMESTAMPTZ) bounded staleness — the database picks the freshest timestamp it can serve locally within your bound you get a moment, not the moment

This is the same job as Spanner's t_safe and MinNextTS, under different names. And it is the same trade: reads that cost nothing and cannot block, in exchange for being a few seconds behind.

16. Glossary: what does every word mean?

In one breath: Every term introduced above, in order of first appearance, with one line each. Use it as a lookup, not as reading.

Term One line
hybrid logical clock (HLC) A timestamp combining a wall-clock reading with a logical counter, so it both orders events correctly and roughly means what a clock means.
--max-offset The cluster-wide declared bound on how far any two clocks may differ; default 500 ms. Not measured — promised.
range A contiguous span of the cluster's single sorted key-value map; splits above 512 MiB, merges below 128 MiB.
replica One copy of a range. Three by default, forming that range's own Raft group.
leaseholder The one replica currently entitled to serve a range's reads locally and sequence its writes.
leader lease The default since v25.2: the lease is held by the Raft leader by construction, so the two can never separate.
epoch-based lease The previous default, tied to a cluster-wide node liveness range; now legacy.
expiration-based lease A short lease renewed by Raft activity, still used for system ranges.
leader-leaseholder split The old failure mode where the lease and Raft leadership sat on different replicas, and a lagging leaseholder could hang a range.
write intent A provisional MVCC value carrying a pointer to its transaction record — a replicated lock and a replicated value in one.
transaction record The single key holding one transaction's status: PENDING, STAGING, COMMITTED, ABORTED, or absent.
anchor key The transaction's first written key, whose range hosts the transaction record.
parallel commits Writing the STAGING record alongside the intents instead of after them, cutting commit from two rounds of consensus to one.
implicitly committed The state of a transaction whose record says STAGING and all of whose listed writes have achieved consensus — committed as a fact about data, with nothing yet written down.
transaction status recovery The procedure a reader runs on a STAGING record: prove the listed writes exist and finish the commit, or block the missing one forever and abort.
uncertainty interval (read_ts, read_ts + max_offset] — the range of timestamps a reader cannot classify as past or future.
ReadWithinUncertaintyIntervalError The error raised inside the database when a read meets a value in that range; it causes a restart, not a client-visible failure.
uncertainty restart Re-running the read at a higher timestamp; the ceiling does not move, so the doubt strictly shrinks.
observed timestamp What a node's clock read when this transaction first contacted it; anything that node stamps above it is provably in the transaction's future.
timestamp cache The in-memory, per-leaseholder record of the highest timestamp at which each key span has been read. Never replicated.
push Moving a write's transaction timestamp up above a read that already happened, instead of making it wait.
read refresh Re-verifying a pushed transaction's earlier reads over the gap it was pushed across; required under SERIALIZABLE, not performed under READ COMMITTED.
RETRY_SERIALIZABLE / SQLSTATE 40001 The client-visible refusal when a read refresh fails; the reason a retry loop is part of the contract.
RETRY_WRITE_TOO_OLD A write met a committed write at a higher timestamp on the same row.
ABORT_REASON_NEW_LEASE_PREVENTS_TXN A new leaseholder cannot vouch for reads recorded by the old one, so it refuses older transactions.
RETRY_ASYNC_WRITE_FAILURE A write could not be replicated because of a lease transfer or partition.
RETRY_COMMIT_DEADLINE_EXCEEDED A transaction was pushed forward for so long that it ran out of horizon.
latch manager Per-replica sequencing of concurrent requests on the same keys; held per request, not per transaction.
lock table The per-node in-memory structure holding locks that in-progress transactions have acquired, each with a wait queue.
TxnWaitQueue The structure on a range's Raft leader tracking transactions that failed to push another, and where distributed deadlocks are broken.
meta1 / meta2 The two-level index that maps a key to the range holding it; meta1's location is gossiped to every node.
range descriptor A range's identity: its ID, its key boundaries, and where its replicas live.
hash-sharded index An index whose key is prefixed with a hash bucket, so a sequential column spreads across ranges instead of piling into the last one.
closed timestamp A per-range promise that no new write will ever appear at or below it — what makes follower reads safe.
follower read A read served by a non-leaseholder replica at or below the closed timestamp; local, cheap, and slightly stale.
bounded staleness Asking for the freshest timestamp servable locally within a stated staleness bound, rather than naming a timestamp yourself.
non-blocking transaction The GLOBAL-table protocol: close timestamps in the future, force writers above the present, and make them commit-wait.
survival goal Whether a database is configured to survive the loss of a zone or of an entire region.
gc.ttlseconds How long overwritten MVCC versions are retained; default 14400 (four hours), and the floor under historical reads.
causal reverse The anomaly in which a transaction's effect is ordered before its cause, possible with three transactions two of which share no data — the gap between serializable and strictly serializable.
strict serializability Serializability plus the requirement that the serial order never contradicts real time; Spanner's "external consistency"; what CockroachDB declines to guarantee.

Rung 1 — Explain

E1.1

On one machine, two things are true for free and nobody thinks about them: everything happens in one order, and every event's timestamp comes from the same clock, so any two timestamps are meaningfully comparable.

Spread the same database over three cities and both facts disappear. There is no single order any more, and — the part that causes the trouble — every write is stamped by whichever machine handled it, using that machine's own clock. Those clocks disagree by tens or hundreds of milliseconds, and no machine can detect its own error, because to know your clock is fast you would need a clock you already trusted. So a write that genuinely happened first can carry a larger timestamp than one that happened after it, and a reader who filters by timestamp will get the order backwards.

CockroachDB's idea is to stop trying to know the time. Instead you declare how far apart any two clocks are allowed to be — 500 ms by default — and you make the reader carry that doubt: any value stamped within that window above a reader's own timestamp is treated as "this might secretly be in my past", and the reader does its work again at a higher timestamp rather than risk a wrong answer.

The trap to avoid: answering in terms of network partitions or message loss. Assume the network is perfect and instant and every problem in this lab still happens. It is a problem of interpretation, not delivery.

E1.2

Both give you serializable transactions across machines, and both have to pay for the fact that clocks disagree. The difference is who pays and when.

Spanner charges the writer, always. Its clocks report an interval rather than an instant, and a writer must wait out the width of that interval before telling the client the write succeeded. The cost is small — a few milliseconds — but it is charged on every write, and it is small only because there are GPS receivers and atomic clocks in every datacentre. In exchange, reads are lock-free, local, never retry, and the resulting order never contradicts real time.

CockroachDB charges the reader, occasionally. Writes are acknowledged immediately. Nothing waits. But a read that happens to encounter a value stamped in an awkward window has to run again at a higher timestamp. The cost is zero on most reads and one extra round trip on the unlucky ones. In exchange it runs on rented hardware anywhere — and it stops one step short of guaranteeing that the order matches real time (see E4.3 and stage 12).

Choose Spanner when you need the ordering guarantee itself — an audit trail whose order will be litigated, or a system where an external observer's account of "what happened first" must match the database's — and you can live inside Google Cloud.

Choose CockroachDB when you need PostgreSQL-compatible serializable transactions on infrastructure you rent, especially write-heavy workloads where a fixed per-write latency premium would hurt: payments, order capture, anything transactional and latency-sensitive.

E1.3

They have mistaken the contract for a failure.

SQLSTATE 40001 is CockroachDB telling the application: "I cannot commit this transaction without breaking serializability; run it again." It is the visible edge of the design in E1.1 — the database never blocks a transaction to prevent a conflict, it lets both proceed and refuses one at the end. A retry loop around every transaction is part of using this database, in the same way that handling a 409 Conflict is part of using an optimistic HTTP API. There is no bug to file. There is a missing loop.

There are also ordinary, non-contention causes: a rolling restart produces 40001s, because a new leaseholder cannot vouch for reads the dead one recorded and refuses transactions older than its lease (stage 11.1).

What I would actually look at, in order:

  1. The rate, and its trend. A steady low background rate is normal. A step change is not.
  2. The breakdown by reason. RETRY_SERIALIZABLE means contention. ReadWithinUncertaintyIntervalError means clock skew has become visible to the workload — a different problem with a different fix. ABORT_REASON_NEW_LEASE_PREVENTS_TXN means leases have been moving, so look for restarts and rebalancing.
  3. crdb_internal.cluster_contention_events, which names the index and key range where transactions are colliding — usually one hot row.
  4. Whether transactions are succeeding on retry. If they are, the system is working as designed and the question is throughput, not correctness.

The one thing that would worry me is the second reason above, because a rising uncertainty-restart rate is the workload noticing clock skew — and the clock-offset gauge is the only warning you get before the guarantee itself is at risk (stage 11.3).

Rung 4 — Modify

E4.1 (simulator, scenario 5, read refresh off)

Both rows end at OFF: dr_mehta = OFF @ 1000 and dr_qureshi = OFF @ 1000⁺. Nobody is on call.

The anomaly is write skew. The isolation level CockroachDB has been reduced to is snapshot isolation — which is exactly why READ COMMITTED transactions in CockroachDB explicitly do not perform read refreshing (docs). Safety was lost: an outcome that no serial order could produce is now observable. Liveness in fact improved — turning the check off removed an abort.

The harder question. There is no write-write conflict, and looking for one is why write skew is hard to catch. The conflict is between T_B's write and T_A's read. T_A read dr_qureshi at timestamp 1000 and made a decision based on the value it saw. T_B then wants to change dr_qureshi at a timestamp below 1000 — which would retroactively falsify a read that has already been acted upon. The timestamp cache is what makes that read visible to a later writer at all; the push is what stops the write sliding in underneath it; and the refresh is what stops the pushed transaction from keeping the benefit of a read it made at the old timestamp. Remove any one of the three and the anomaly returns.

E4.2 (simulator, scenario 4)

restarts value read for /payments/p2

How both are serializable. A transaction's read timestamp is not a fact about the world; it is the position in the transaction order that this transaction has been assigned. With the optimisation on, the report ends up at position 1160⁺ and sees the world as of then. With it off, it is pushed to 1285⁺ and sees the world as of then. Both are legitimate positions and both give an internally consistent view. Neither is "more correct"; they are different points in a valid serial order.

What it tells you about optimisations. The observed-timestamp rule is allowed to exist precisely because it only ever moves a transaction to a different valid position, never to an invalid one. It converts "possibly in my past" into "provably in my future" using information the transaction already had. An optimisation that changed the answer to one no serial order permits would be a bug, not an optimisation — and the way to check any proposed optimisation in this design is to ask which serial order its outcome corresponds to.

No value of max_offset addresses that, because the uncertainty interval only ever protects a reader against writes in its own past. The only fix is to make one of the two transactions wait until real time has caught up with its timestamp — which is commit-wait, which is Spanner, and which CockroachDB does offer, for GLOBAL tables only (stage 13.3).

t=1     read the local key   (1 ms)
t=61    read the remote key  (60 ms)
t=61    send, in parallel: intent on key A, intent on key B,
        txn record {STAGING, in-flight:[A,B]}
t=121   all three agreed → commit condition holds → client acknowledged
        client waited 121 ms
t=126   record → COMMITTED, intents resolved            (client already gone)

Parallel commits off:

t=61    send intent on key A and intent on key B
t=121   both agreed
t=121   send txn record PENDING → COMMITTED
t=181   agreed → client acknowledged
        client waited 181 ms

121 ms versus 181 ms (simulator). And the saving is exactly one cross-region round trip at every latency — the simulator confirms it across the range:

round trip parallel commits on off difference
10 ms 21 ms 31 ms 10 ms
60 ms 121 ms 181 ms 60 ms
120 ms 241 ms 361 ms 120 ms
180 ms 361 ms 541 ms 180 ms

The generalisation. The intents for all n ranges are sent in parallel, so they cost one round trip, not n. So:

  • with parallel commits, the client waits ≈ 1 × RTT for the commit;
  • without, ≈ 2 × RTT.

n does not appear in either expression. What n actually changes is (a) the probability that at least one of the ranges is slow, since you wait for the slowest, not the average — so the tail latency degrades with n even though the mean does not; and (b) the number of ranges whose leases must be reachable at once, so availability degrades with n too. This is worth stating because it corrects a natural but wrong mental model: distributed commit is expensive in round trips (a constant), and expensive in exposure (linear in n).

E5.2 — swapped clocks (simulator: Frankfurt −120, Ohio +180)

tsA = HLC(Frankfurt) at true 1000 = 880      T_A, Dr Mehta's transaction
tsB = HLC(Ohio)      at true 1010 = 1190     T_B, Dr Qureshi's transaction

both read both rows; timestamp cache on each row now records the higher read, 1190 (T_B)

t=1200  T_A writes dr_mehta at 880.
        timestamp cache[dr_mehta] = 1190 (recorded by T_B, a different transaction)
        1190 ≥ 880  →  PUSH T_A's commit_ts to 1190⁺
        T_A was pushed → refresh its reads over (880, 1190⁺]
        nothing has changed yet → refresh CLEAN → T_A commits at 1190⁺
        (a successful refresh also re-records T_A's reads in the cache at 1190⁺)

t=1260  T_B writes dr_qureshi at 1190.
        timestamp cache[dr_qureshi] = 1190⁺ (T_A's refreshed read)
        1190⁺ ≥ 1190  →  PUSH T_B's commit_ts to 1190⁺⁺
        T_B was pushed → refresh its reads over (1190, 1190⁺⁺]
        dr_mehta changed at 1190⁺, inside the gap  →  refresh FAILED
        RETRY_SERIALIZABLE (40001)

Simulator confirms: TA=COMMITTED commit@1190⁺, TB=ABORTED read@1190 commit@1190⁺, refreshFail=1, and dr_qureshi still ON CALL.

The general rule this demonstrates. In the default trace, T_A had the higher read timestamp and survived; here T_A has the lower one and survives. So the rule is not about timestamps at all:

The transaction that commits second in real time is the one refused — because the conflict does not exist until the first one's write does, and a refresh can only detect what already happened.

That has a practical consequence worth carrying: under contention, the slower transaction loses, so long-running transactions on hot data starve. It is the same starvation shape you priced in optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking, and it is why SELECT … FOR UPDATE exists — it converts a late refusal into an early wait, which is a trade a long transaction should usually take.

E5.3

The bound is one restart per node, so with three nodes, at most three.

Proof from the two brakes.

Brake one — the ceiling is fixed. The uncertainty ceiling read_ts + max_offset is computed once, when the transaction begins, and never recomputed. Every restart raises read_ts strictly, so the window strictly shrinks, and no restart can ever be caused by a value above the original ceiling. This alone bounds the total spread of restarts to max_offset, but not their count.

Brake two — the observed timestamp is monotone per node. The first time the transaction contacts node N, it records N's clock reading obs[N]. Suppose a value on N stamped v forces a restart. To be ambiguous it must satisfy v ≤ obs[N], and after the restart read_ts = v⁺ > v. Now consider any later value w on the same node N. Either w ≤ read_ts, in which case it is visible and causes no restart; or w > read_ts > v. For w to be ambiguous it must also satisfy w ≤ obs[N] — but the transaction can only see a value that was already written when it read, and any value written after the first contact has w > obs[N], so it is classified provably-future. The only remaining case is a value with v < w ≤ obs[N] that already existed at first contact and was not returned by the first read — which cannot happen, because a read returns the newest version below the relevant timestamp. Hence at most one restart per node. This matches the documented statement that a transaction restarts "never for longer than the uncertainty interval, nor more than once per node."

The worst case. To actually achieve three restarts you need three separate nodes each holding a value that is (i) stamped above the transaction's current read timestamp, (ii) below that node's observed timestamp, and (iii) read after the transaction has already been pushed by the previous one. Concretely: three fast-clocked leaseholders, each written to shortly before the transaction contacts it, read in increasing order of their timestamps. The practical version is a report that scans several ranges on several fast-clocked nodes while they are all taking writes — and the mitigation is not to make the clocks better but to read in the past on purpose (AS OF SYSTEM TIME), where nothing can be ambiguous at all.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

E6.1 — the moving ceiling

The flaw: restarts would no longer terminate.

Concrete run. max_offset = 500. A report begins on Frankfurt at read timestamp 1000, so its window is (1000, 1500]. A fast-clocked node is committing one write every 50 ms, stamped 1200, 1250, 1300, and so on.

  • Reads a value at 1200 → ambiguous → restart. Real rule: window becomes (1200⁺, 1500]. Proposed rule: window becomes (1200⁺, 1700].
  • Reads a value at 1250 → ambiguous under both → restart. Real: (1250⁺, 1500]. Proposed: (1250⁺, 1750].
  • …and so on. Under the real rule the window is shrinking by 50 ms each time and the process must stop within 500 ms of where it started. Under the proposed rule the ceiling runs away ahead of the writes forever, and the transaction can never reach a timestamp at which the node's next write is not ambiguous.

Guarantee violated: liveness. Safety is untouched — every answer the transaction would give is correct; it simply never gives one. This is the most instructive kind of flaw, because the proposed rule is more cautious than the real one and cautious-in-the-wrong-place is a real failure mode.

The deeper point: the fixed ceiling is not a conservative approximation of the truth, it is the exact statement of what the transaction needs. It began at real time t, and max_offset bounds how far any clock can be from any other at t. Nothing that happens later can widen that bound retrospectively, so nothing should widen the ceiling.

E6.2 — recovery without the barrier

The dropped step: raising the timestamp cache above the transaction's timestamp on the missing key, before marking the record ABORTED.

Concrete run. Transaction T writes intents on keys A and B and a record {STAGING, in-flight:[A,B]}. The intent on A is agreed; the message carrying the intent on B is delayed in the network — not lost, just slow. The coordinator dies.

t=100   reader R1 finds the intent on A, follows it to the STAGING record
t=110   R1 checks B: no intent present
        [proposed rule]  R1 marks the record ABORTED and discards A's intent
t=115   the delayed message finally arrives: B's intent is agreed by a majority
t=200   reader R2 finds the intent on B, follows it to the record
        the record says ABORTED... but under the proposed rule nothing stops the
        write to B having succeeded after the abort decision was taken

Depending on the order in which the two readers and the delayed message interleave, one reader can conclude the transaction aborted while the other finds every listed write present and concludes it committed. The result is a torn transaction: A discarded, B durable, and two observers disagreeing about whether it happened.

The real procedure closes this by making the abort self-enforcing: it raises the barrier on B above T's timestamp first, so the delayed write can no longer succeed at that timestamp, and only then records ABORTED. The decision creates the condition that makes it true.

Guarantee violated: atomicity. And the general principle worth extracting: in a system with no coordinator, a decision is only safe if the act of making it prevents the alternative. Writing down "aborted" is a claim; making the write impossible is a fact.

E6.3 — the empty timestamp cache

Concrete run.

t=1000  T_A on Frankfurt reads /oncall/dr_qureshi at 1000 → ON CALL
        Frankfurt's timestamp cache records: dr_qureshi read at 1000
t=1050  Frankfurt (the leaseholder) dies
t=1100  Mumbai takes the lease with an empty cache
t=1200  T_B writes dr_qureshi = OFF at commit_ts 900
        the new leaseholder sees no read mark → no push → T_B commits at 900
t=1300  T_A commits its own write, having read dr_qureshi = ON CALL at 1000

T_A's decision rests on reading dr_qureshi = ON CALL as of timestamp 1000, and a write at timestamp 900 — below T_A's read — has now been inserted underneath it. There is no serial order containing both: T_A must come after T_B by timestamp, but T_A did not see T_B's write. Serializability is lost.

The real design refuses instead: the new leaseholder installs a low-water mark at its lease start covering the whole range, so any write from a transaction older than the lease is refused with ABORT_REASON_NEW_LEASE_PREVENTS_TXN (simulator, scenario 6). The cost is that healthy transactions are thrown away by an ordinary node restart — which is exactly why the retry loop is not optional.

Note what this reveals about the structure: the timestamp cache is described as a performance structure, and it is not replicated — but it is load-bearing for safety. The design's answer is not to replicate it (expensive) but to make its absence conservative (cheap, and occasionally rude to clients).

Rung 7 — Handle failure

E7.1 — the ambiguous commit

The application change: an idempotency key, and a record of it inside the same transaction.

  BEGIN
    INSERT INTO payment_attempts (idempotency_key, ...)   -- unique constraint on the key
    UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance - 500 WHERE id = 'asha'
    UPDATE accounts SET balance = balance + 500 WHERE id = 'raj'
  COMMIT

The client generates the key before its first attempt and reuses the same key on every retry. On a retry, one of two things happens: either the insert succeeds, meaning the first attempt did not commit and this one should proceed; or it violates the unique constraint, meaning the first attempt did commit and the client can safely report success.

What the database must provide for this to work: the uniqueness check and the money movement must be in the same transaction, so that they commit or fail together. That is precisely the thing a sharded setup cannot give you when the key table and the accounts are on different shards — and it is the single most concrete reason to want a distributed transaction at all. It is also why payment_attempts must not be a separate service.

Why a 40001 retry loop is not enough on its own. A 40001 is a definite answer: the transaction did not commit, and retrying is unconditionally safe. An ambiguous result (RETRY_ASYNC_WRITE_FAILURE, or simply a dropped connection at the moment of acknowledgement) is an absence of an answer: the transaction may have committed. A loop that retries both identically will double-charge on the second. The idempotency key is what converts the ambiguous case into the definite one — and note the pleasing coincidence from stage 11.7: a transaction body written to be re-runnable after a 40001 is already 90% of the way to being re-runnable after an ambiguous result. The remaining 10% is the key.

See idempotency for the full treatment.

E7.2 — the sequential primary key

The cause is the encoding in stage 10.1: /<table>/<index>/<pk> in a sorted map, so a monotonically increasing primary key means every insert sorts to the end and lands in the last range, which has one leaseholder, on one machine. Load-based splitting will split that range, and the new last range immediately becomes the new hot spot — the problem regenerates.

Fix What it costs
UUID primary key (random) Inserts spread perfectly. You lose ordering by id: ORDER BY id is meaningless and "the last 100 rows" needs an index on a timestamp column. Keys are 16 bytes rather than 8, in the primary key and in every secondary index.
Hash-sharded index (USING HASH) Keeps the sequential column and its ordering semantics, and spreads writes across n buckets. A range scan must now visit all n buckets and merge, so scans cost more; and choosing n is a tuning decision.
Reorder a composite key so a high-cardinality column leads Free, if such a column exists and your access patterns tolerate it. Changes which queries are a seek and which are a scan — this is the fix that most often breaks something else.

(a) Only ever looked up by id → UUID. You are not using ordering, so give it up entirely and get perfect distribution for nothing. This is the default answer and should be the default schema choice for new tables.

(b) Frequently scanned by created_at ranges → hash-sharded index on created_at. Here ordering is the workload, so you cannot randomise it away. Accept the merge cost on scans in exchange for writes that spread. Do measure it: the docs specifically recommend performance testing with and without, because for some scan-heavy workloads the merge cost exceeds the write benefit.

E7.3 — the climbing clock offset

Check, in this order:

  1. Which node, and is it one or several? One node is a local problem — a failing NTP client, a noisy neighbour on the hypervisor, a VM that has been live-migrated. Several nodes drifting together points at a shared upstream time source, which is worse.
  2. The uncertainty-restart rate. If it is climbing alongside the offset, the skew has already become visible to your workload.
  3. The NTP or PTP daemon on the affected node — is it running, is it synchronised, what stratum, when did it last step the clock?
  4. Recent infrastructure events — instance migration, host maintenance, a kernel or hypervisor upgrade.

Do first: fix time synchronisation on that node, or drain and remove the node from the cluster. Removing a node is cheap and loud; a bad clock is expensive and silent.

Do not: raise --max-offset to make the number look better. That is the exact wrong move. Raising the budget does not make the clocks better; it widens every reader's window of doubt, so it increases restart latency across the whole cluster while reducing the margin that triggers the self-check. You would be paying more, for less safety, to hide a symptom. Also do not lower it in a panic — lowering it while a node is genuinely 380 ms out is how you turn a warning into a stale read.

At 400 ms, the node finds it disagrees with at least half its peers by more than 80% of the budget and terminates itself (docs). You lose a node, availability drops for the ranges it led until leases move, and everyone notices immediately.

Why 400 and not 500. Because at 500 the guarantee is already broken. The uncertainty interval is exactly 500 ms wide; a clock 500 ms out can produce a write that hides from a reader. The 20% margin exists so that removal happens before the promise can be violated, not at the moment it is. It is the difference between a smoke alarm and a fire report — and it is the same design instinct as the fixed uncertainty ceiling in E6.1: bound the damage a misbehaving component can do before it misbehaves, rather than detecting it afterwards.

Rung 8 — Design

These are discussions, not answers. What follows is one defensible position with its reasoning exposed; a different set of choices, defended as well, is equally good.

E8.1 — a three-region payments ledger

Localities.

  • accounts and payments → REGIONAL BY ROW, partitioned by the account's home region. This is the whole ballgame: 90% of traffic touches one region's accounts, and REGIONAL BY ROW puts those rows' leaseholders in that region, so the common transaction is entirely local — no 120 ms round trip, no cross-region consensus. The 10% that crosses regions pays the full price, which is correct: it is a more expensive operation.
  • Reference data — currencies, fee schedules, merchant categories, country rules → GLOBAL. Read on every payment from every region, written a handful of times a week. This is exactly the read-mostly shape GLOBAL exists for, and it removes a cross-region read from the hot path of every single payment. The write cost (a few hundred milliseconds of commit-wait) is irrelevant at that write rate.
  • An idempotency_keys table, in the same locality as payments, written in the same transaction as the money movement (E7.1).

Survival goal: SURVIVE REGION FAILURE. For a payments ledger with a regulator, losing a region must not lose the ledger. It costs write latency — a quorum now spans regions even for a regional transaction — so measure it, and be honest that SURVIVE ZONE FAILURE is a real option if the regulator's actual requirement is weaker than the instinct to survive everything.

--max-offset: 250 ms, the documented recommendation for clusters using the multi-region abstractions. Lower than the 500 ms default means narrower windows of doubt and fewer restarts — and a thinner margin, so it must be paired with monitoring that treats the offset gauge as a correctness alarm (E7.3).

Primary keys: UUID everywhere, with the region column leading the index where the locality requires it. Payments are a strictly append-shaped workload, which is the worst case for sequential keys (stage 11.6). Do not use SERIAL.

The negative-balance invariant. Read the balance and write it in the same transaction; serializable isolation makes the check-then-act safe with no locks and no application logic. Under contention on a popular merchant's account, expect 40001s — the fix is SELECT … FOR UPDATE on that row to take the conflict pessimistically, or splitting the merchant's balance into per-region sub-balances that are summed. Pick the second only when the first stops being enough, because it changes what the invariant means.

The nightly reconciliation: AS OF SYSTEM TIME follower_read_timestamp(). It reads a few seconds in the past, is served by local followers in every region, blocks nothing, is blocked by nothing, and adds no contention to live traffic. Raise gc.ttlseconds above its four-hour default for the tables it scans if the job can run long.

Monitor: clock offset per node (alert well below 400 ms — page at 200); transaction retries broken down by reason; hot ranges; contention events; unavailable ranges; p99 write latency per region. Alert on: clock offset, unavailable ranges, and a step change in RETRY_SERIALIZABLE.

If the regulator upgrades to "the order of all payments must match real time." This is the requirement CockroachDB does not meet by default, and it must be said plainly rather than argued around. The available responses, in order of honesty:

  1. Make the affected tables GLOBAL, which turns on commit-wait and buys back real-time ordering — at hundreds of milliseconds of write latency per payment. For a payments ledger that is very likely unacceptable, and you should say so with the number attached.
  2. Move to Spanner for the ledger tables. This is what the requirement is actually asking for, and it is worth naming rather than engineering around.
  3. Interrogate the requirement. The causal reverse needs three transactions, two of which share no data. Payments against the same account or the same merchant can never exhibit it, because they overlap. If the regulator's real concern is "two payments on one account must be correctly ordered", you already meet it, and the honest move is to demonstrate that rather than to buy a guarantee you do not need.

E8.2 — migrating from sixteen sharded PostgreSQL instances

What gets simpler, and this is most of the value:

  • The saga disappears. Every cross-shard case it existed to handle becomes an ordinary BEGIN … COMMIT. You delete the compensating transactions, the state machine, the orchestrator, and the tests for all of it. From saga: a saga is chosen fractured visibility with compensations — you were choosing it because you had no alternative, and now you do.
  • The nightly reconciliation job disappears too, or shrinks to an assertion. It existed to repair what the saga missed. Nothing is missed now. Keep a much cheaper version as a canary; delete the repair logic.
  • Resharding disappears. Ranges split and rebalance automatically; there is no shard map in application configuration and no migration project when a shard gets hot.
  • Cross-shard reads become ordinary queries instead of scatter-gather in the application.

What gets harder:

  • Every transaction needs a retry loop. This is new, it is not optional, and it is not only about contention — a rolling restart produces 40001s (stage 11.1). It touches every data-access path in the codebase.
  • Transaction bodies must be re-runnable, which means no side effects inside the transaction that cannot be repeated: no sending an email, no calling a payment gateway, no incrementing an in-memory counter. This is a real refactor and it is easy to underestimate.
  • Latency for cross-region transactions is now visible in your own system rather than hidden in the saga's asynchrony. The saga was slow too — it just was not slow inside a request. Expect a conversation about p99s that is partly a conversation about honesty.
  • Schema design decides physical distribution. PostgreSQL forgives a BIGSERIAL primary key; CockroachDB pins your whole insert workload to one node for it (stage 11.6).
  • Not every PostgreSQL feature is present, and the ones that are may perform differently. Audit before committing to a date.

The one new failure mode the on-call rotation must learn: clock skew. Nothing in sixteen-shard PostgreSQL had a correctness property that depended on clocks. Now one does, its failure is silent, and the only warning is a gauge (stage 11.3). Put it in the runbook, put it on the dashboard, and rehearse it — because it is the only failure in the list that does not announce itself.

Migration order: move a low-risk, single-shard table first to shake out the retry loops and the schema idioms; then a table involved in the saga, so you can delete a compensation path and prove the value; then the core ledger. Run dual-write with reconciliation during the transition — which is the one time the reconciliation job you are about to delete earns its keep.

E8.3 — the global product catalogue

Choice: a CockroachDB GLOBAL table.

Apply the who-pays framing from stage 6. The workload is roughly a million reads per write. The question is therefore: who should carry the cost of clock uncertainty and cross-region coordination? The answer is obvious as soon as it is asked that way — charge the writer, because there are almost no writers.

  • A default CockroachDB table charges the reader: reads outside the leaseholder's region cross an ocean, on every page load. Wrong payer, at the worst possible ratio.
  • A GLOBAL table closes timestamps in the future, forces the hundred daily writes to commit-wait a few hundred milliseconds, and lets every read be served from a local replica without blocking on anything. A merchandiser waiting 400 ms to save a product is a non-event; a customer waiting 120 ms extra on every page load is a business problem.
  • Spanner would serve this well too and is architecturally the same answer — its writers always commit-wait. Choose it only if you are already there; it is not worth a cloud migration for one table.
  • A cache in front of single-region PostgreSQL is the cheapest thing that works, and for a product catalogue it is a completely reasonable answer. What you give up is a bound on staleness: cache invalidation across regions is best-effort, and a stale price is a support ticket. GLOBAL gives you the same read latency with a transactional guarantee instead of a hope.

What would change the answer:

  • Writes become frequent — say, algorithmic repricing every minute. Commit-wait now sits on the hot path, the ratio flips, and REGIONAL BY ROW partitioned by the merchandising team's region becomes the better shape.
  • Reads become tolerant of staleness. If pages can happily show data five seconds old, AS OF SYSTEM TIME follower_read_timestamp() on an ordinary regional table gives you local reads for free, with no write penalty at all. This is the cheapest option in the whole list and is the one most often overlooked — always ask how fresh the reads actually need to be before paying for freshness.
  • The catalogue is only read in one region. Then none of this matters and a plain regional table is correct. Do not buy multi-region machinery for a single-region problem.
