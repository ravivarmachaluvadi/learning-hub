Lab 39 · Copies and Agreement

Paxos — how a school that never holds a meeting picks one trip, forever

Tell a friend: Five teachers must agree on one school trip, but they are never in the building together, so everything goes by mailbox notes that get lost or arrive late, while two class reps campaign for different places at once. Paxos is the set of rules that still gets the school to exactly one destination, never changed, however badly the mail behaves. The trick: before asking for anything, ask a majority of teachers what is already written down, and if they tell you something, campaign for that instead of your own idea. Paxos promises the school will never book two trips. It does not promise it will ever book one, and that missing half is why every real system puts a leader on top.

This lab covers single-decree Paxos, also called basic Paxos or the Synod algorithm: agreeing on one value. Agreeing on a whole sequence of values is Multi-Paxos, and section 15 gets there. What the lab is made of, and where its numbers come from, is in section 21 at the end.

1. Why does this problem exist at all?

In one breath: One computer can lose your data. Several computers can disagree about your data, which is worse. Paxos is the smallest set of rules that stops them disagreeing.

Put important data on one computer, and one power supply can lose it. So you put it on several. Now you have a new problem, and it is far nastier than it sounds: the machines must agree on what the data is.

Why can't they simply vote? Because some of them will be switched off at the moment of the vote. Some messages will vanish. Some will arrive an hour late, looking perfectly fresh.

Between 1978 and 1998, people kept writing "obviously correct" agreement protocols. Each one turned out to decide two different things under some unlucky ordering of messages. Losing a bank transfer, or booking a seat twice, is the kind of bug you cannot patch after the fact.

Leslie Lamport's answer was to find the smallest set of rules that is provably immune to every possible ordering. Paxos is that set.

2. The school trip: how do you decide when nobody can meet?

In one breath: Five teachers, one trip, one coach company that needs a single answer. Nobody is ever in the same room. All you have is mailboxes.

Five teachers must agree on one destination for the school trip. It has to be exactly one. The coach company needs a single answer, and once they have been paid, the answer can never change.

The awkward part: the teachers are never all in school at once, and they never talk to each other. You cannot get them in a room. All you can do is leave a note in a teacher's mailbox and hope for a reply.

Notes sometimes get lost. Notes sometimes sit in a bag for two days and then get read. Teachers sometimes go off sick for a week.

You are a class rep and you want the Zoo. Priya is another class rep, doing exactly the same thing at exactly the same time, and she wants the Museum. Neither of you knows the other is doing it.

Get the school to commit to one destination, forever.

3. Why not just tell everyone?

In one breath: Five notes saying "Zoo", one in each mailbox. Done? Not even close.

Write "Zoo" on five notes, put one in each mailbox, done.

🔮 Predict: Your five "Zoo" notes and Priya's five "Museum" notes are all in the mail at the same time. What do the teachers end up believing?

Reveal There is no single answer, and that is the problem. Depending on delivery order, some teachers believe "Zoo", some "Museum", and some nothing at all. No rule exists to say which belief is *the* decision. No moment exists where anyone could safely tell the coach company an answer. If you said "whoever's notes land first wins", ask yourself: who is watching the mailboxes to declare it? Section 4 walks three concrete versions of this disaster.

4. Three ways "tell everyone" falls apart. Which one is subtle?

In one breath: First-note-wins has no finish line. All-five-must-agree stops the moment one teacher is off sick. Majority-wins is nearly right, and "nearly" is where the interesting bug lives.

Attempt 1: tell everyone; first note wins. Your Zoo notes reach teachers A and B first. Priya's Museum notes reach D and E first. C is off sick. Now A and B believe "Zoo", D and E believe "Museum", and nobody believes anything in particular. Worse, there is no moment where anyone can say the decision is made. You cannot tell "everyone agrees" from "everyone I have heard from agrees."

Attempt 2: require all five to agree. Now there is a clear rule. But C has been off sick for a week, so nothing can ever be decided. One absent teacher out of five stops the school. That is not fault tolerance. That is the opposite.

Attempt 3: majority wins. Get 3 of 5 and call it decided. This is much better, and it is nearly right.

🔮 Predict: You get three teachers to write down "Zoo" and go home happy. Can Priya still get the school to book the Museum a week later, with every teacher following the majority rule exactly?

Reveal Yes. Watch it break below. The clue is that "3 of 5" and "3 of 5" can be two different threes, and the teacher they share has nothing stopping her from changing her mind.

Watch it break:

  1. You get A, B and E to write down "Zoo". That is 3 of 5. Decided: Zoo. You go home happy.
  2. A week later Priya, who never heard about any of this, gets C, D and E to write down "Museum". E already had "Zoo" written down. Under these naive rules E just crosses it out and writes the new one. That is also 3 of 5. Decided: Museum.

Two different decisions, both by a legitimate majority. The coach company has been told two different things. Notice that nobody misbehaved. Every teacher followed the rule as written. The rule itself is broken.

5. What two facts fix everything?

In one breath: Any two groups of three teachers share at least one teacher. So ask three teachers what they already wrote before you propose anything, and if you hear an answer, campaign for that.

Two facts, and Paxos is just their consequence:

  1. Any two majorities of the same group always share at least one member. With 5 teachers, any 3 and any other 3 must overlap. There is no way to pick two groups of 3 out of 5 that miss each other.
  2. So make every new attempt ask a majority what has already happened, before it proposes anything. If it learns of an existing entry, it must abandon its own idea and re-propose that one.

Rule 1 guarantees the asker cannot miss an existing decision. Rule 2 turns that knowledge into safety. Priya, in the story above, would have heard "Zoo" from E. She would then have campaigned for the Zoo, not the Museum.

That is the whole algorithm. Everything below is detail.

7. What picture should you carry in your head?

In one breath: A class rep is not an author. She is a courier. She asks permission to speak, and the price of permission is repeating what she was told.

Carry this: Paxos is a protocol for asking permission to speak, where the price of permission is having to repeat what you were told.

A proposer does not get to be the author of the value. It is more like a courier. It asks a majority "may I go next, and what did I miss?". If the answer contains any news at all, it is obliged to carry that news forward instead of its own message. Only when a majority tells it "nothing has ever happened here" is it allowed to introduce its own value.

That is why Paxos can never change its mind. After a value is chosen, every majority contains someone who will report it. So every future proposer becomes a courier for that same value.

The teacher's notebook. Every teacher keeps one notebook page with three lines on it.

Line What she writes there Engineers call it
1 the highest ticket number she has promised to honour promised_n
2 the ticket number of the last note she actually wrote down accepted_n
3 what that note said (Zoo or Museum) accepted_value

That page is the whole of Paxos's memory. Everything the protocol does is either a question about this page or a change to it.

The engineering model. The same machine, stated precisely. The entire system is three kinds of state plus one rule:

  • each acceptor (teacher) holds exactly three durable numbers: the page above, promised_n, accepted_n and accepted_value;
  • each proposer (class rep) holds its current ticket number n, which phase it is in, and the set of promises and acceptances it has collected;
  • the network (the mailboxes) is a bag of messages that can be delayed, dropped, or duplicated. Never trusted, never ordered.

There is one rule, and it is the invariant everything defends. In words: once a majority has written a value under some ticket, every later ticket that gets as far as asking teachers to write must ask for that same value. In symbols: if any majority has accepted (n, v), then every proposal numbered above n that ever reaches phase 2 carries value v. Phase 1 exists to make that rule enforceable.

Where the story lies to you. Carry these corrections with it:

  1. Teachers sound sequential; acceptors are concurrent. Notes feel like they arrive over days, one at a time. Real messages interleave at microsecond scale in adversarial orders. The reason the rules must be airtight is precisely that no human is watching the mailboxes.
  2. A teacher's memory is exactly the bug. In the story, remembering is effortless. In the machine, "remembering" means writing to disk before replying. An acceptor that answers first and persists later is the safety violation of experiment 5, not a minor optimization.
  3. Notes are unique; messages are not. A physical note exists once. A network message can arrive twice, late, or out of order. That is why every operation is built to be idempotent: accepting (n, v) twice equals accepting it once.
  4. Nobody announces the result. The story tempts you to imagine a moment where someone declares "it's the Zoo!". In the machine, chosen is a global fact that can be true while no single participant knows it. That is why learners exist as a separate role.

9. What does one round actually look like, note by note?

In one breath: Ask three teachers for a ticket and for their old entry. If anyone reports an entry, propose that. Otherwise propose your own. When three teachers have written the same thing under the same ticket, it is decided.

First, the round as a conversation. Then the same round as rules.

Phase 1, in the story. You pick a ticket number nobody has used, say 1. You mail three teachers: "May I have ticket 1? And what have you already written down?" Each teacher looks at her page. If ticket 1 is higher than the number on line 1, she writes 1 on line 1 and replies: "Ticket 1 is yours. My page is blank." If not, she replies "No, I already promised a higher ticket."

Phase 2, in the story. You have three replies. If any of them reported an old entry, you must propose that entry, whichever one carries the highest ticket number. If all three said "blank", you may propose the Zoo. You mail the three teachers: "Write Zoo under ticket 1." Each one checks line 1 again. If 1 is still at least as high as what she promised, she writes it down and replies "Written." Three "Written" replies under the same ticket, and the Zoo is decided.

Now the same thing as the engineer writes it.

Phase 1 (Prepare / Promise).

  1. The proposer picks a proposal number n that is unique and higher than any it has used, and sends Prepare(n) to all acceptors.
  2. An acceptor receiving Prepare(n):
    • if n > promised_n (the ticket is higher than line 1): set promised_n = n and reply Promise(n, accepted_n, accepted_value), including the last thing it accepted, or "nothing";
    • otherwise: refuse (NACK).
  3. The proposer waits for promises from a majority.

Phase 2 (Accept / Accepted). 4. The proposer now picks the value it will propose:

  • if any promise reported an accepted value, it must use the one with the highest accepted_n among them, not its own;
  • only if no promise reported any value may it use its own.
  1. It sends Accept(n, v) to the acceptors.
  2. An acceptor receiving Accept(n, v): if n >= promised_n, record accepted_n = n, accepted_value = v, reply Accepted. Otherwise NACK.
  3. When a majority has accepted the same n, the value is chosen. Note that no single participant necessarily knows this at the moment it happens. That is why learners exist.

10. What can go wrong, and what survives each time?

In one breath: Crashes stall the school or change nothing. Late and duplicated notes are shrugged off by ticket numbers. Two rival class reps can argue forever. Only one failure breaks the never-two-trips promise: a teacher who forgets her page.

A minority of teachers is off sick. 2 of 5 down, 3 alive: a majority still exists, so everything works normally. This is the whole point of majorities. You buy tolerance of ⌊(N−1)/2⌋ failures. It is also why clusters use odd sizes. With 4 acceptors a 2–2 split means nobody has a majority. You paid for an extra machine and got zero extra tolerance plus a new stall mode.

A majority is off sick. No quorum can form; nothing is ever decided. The system stalls but stays correct. It never invents an answer. Losing liveness is survivable; losing safety is not.

A class rep gives up half way. Nothing special happens. Its half-finished proposal simply sits there. Another proposer picks a higher number, discovers whatever was accepted, and carries it forward. There is no cleanup, no timeout-based rollback, no "abort" message. Paxos has no notion of a transaction that needs undoing.

A note sits in a bag and arrives very late. This is the dangerous one, and it is handled by proposal numbers. An Accept(1, "Zoo") that arrives after the teacher has promised ticket 2 is refused outright:

What arrives at teacher A Line 1 of A's page says A's reply
Accept(n=1, "Zoo"), delayed 2 (already promised to P2) NACK(n=1; already promised 2)

A note is delivered twice. Harmless. Accepting (n, v) twice is the same as accepting it once. The operations are idempotent by construction. Proposers track promises and acceptances in sets, so a duplicate never counts twice toward a majority. (The simulator confirms this: re-delivering an already-delivered ACCEPT leaves every acceptor byte-identical and produces no second decision event.)

The school splits in two. A network partition. Paxos treats it as "some acceptors are unreachable." The side holding a majority continues; the side without one stalls. Compare this with the CAP theorem's CP corner in the sibling lab ../24-cap-theorem/, where this exact behaviour is the thing being traded for.

🔮 Predict: Two class reps keep out-numbering each other. No crash, no lost note. Which property fails: safety, liveness, or both?

Reveal Liveness only. Nothing wrong is ever decided (safety holds perfectly); nothing is decided at all (liveness fails). If you said "both", notice that no teacher ever writes anything down, so there is nothing to be wrong about. The trace below shows the mechanism, and why the fix is a leader, not a rule change.

("Gazumped" is the estate-agent's word: outbid at the last moment.) Each proposer invalidates the other's promise just before it can finish. This can in principle go on forever. It is not a bug in Paxos; it is a consequence of the FLP result (section 11).

11. Why can it never choose two trips, and why might it choose none?

In one breath: Two decisions would need two groups of three teachers that share nobody, and there are only five teachers. Deciding nothing is always possible, and no protocol can fix that.

The proof, as a story. Why can the school never end up with two destinations? Try to build it. Pick any three teachers who wrote down Zoo. Now pick any three teachers for a later attempt. You cannot do it without reusing at least one teacher: there are only five. That shared teacher still has Zoo on her page. The rule says she must report it, and the rule says the new class rep must carry what she reports. So the later attempt proposes Zoo too. Repeat that for every later attempt and there is never a second destination.

That paragraph is the proof. Written the way papers write it:

If a value v has been chosen, then every proposal issued afterwards with a higher number also has value v.

Chain the reasoning. A value is chosen when a majority M1 (the first three teachers) accepts it at number n1. Any later proposal n2 > n1 must first collect promises from some majority M2 (the later three). "At least one shared teacher" is M1 ∩ M2 ≠ ∅. So at least one acceptor in M2 accepted (n1, v). Because it promised n2 after accepting, and promising means refusing everything lower, it reports v. The proposer is then forced by the phase-2 rule to propose v.

Repeat inductively for every higher number. Hence at most one value is ever chosen. That is Lamport's P2c.

Safety (guaranteed, unconditionally): at most one value is chosen. This holds under any pattern of message loss, delay, reordering, duplication, and crash.

That last sentence is a proof, not a measurement. But the measurement is worth having, because it is the shape of what you would run against a real implementation. A soak like this is evidence that the lesson's model behaves as the proof says. It is not a proof: a soak can only ever fail to find a counterexample. And it is not a statement about any production implementation. The proof above is the reason to believe safety. The soak is the reason to believe this lab is not lying to you about the proof.

Liveness (NOT guaranteed): Paxos may never decide. The livelock above is the everyday version. The formal statement is the FLP impossibility result (Fischer, Lynch, Paterson, 1985): in an asynchronous system where even one process may crash, no deterministic protocol can guarantee both safety and termination. You have a whole lab on this, ../33-flp-impossibility/, whose adversary scheduler defeats rotating-coordinator Paxos for 5,000 steps without dropping a single message.

Every consensus system alive today makes the same choice Paxos does. Keep safety absolutely, and get liveness in practice by assuming the network is usually well behaved (partial synchrony), plus timeouts and randomized backoff.

How much is a leader actually worth? Re-run the identical soak: same seed, same loss, same crashes. Change one thing. A single class rep owns every attempt, and waits for her own round to settle before starting another:

Who proposes Runs that decided Safety violations
Two proposers duelling 36 / 500 0
One leader, rounds not overlapping 220 / 500 0

Six times the decision rate, from changing nothing but who is allowed to speak. Safety was never the problem, and a leader does not improve it. This one table is the entire argument for Multi-Paxos, Raft, ZAB and every other leader-based system you will meet. It is why "bolt a leader on top" is an engineering decision about throughput, not about correctness.

What Paxos assumes. Non-Byzantine failures: nodes may crash, sleep, or lose messages, but they do not lie or forge messages. A malicious acceptor breaks everything. That is the domain of PBFT and friends, not Paxos.

The obligation Paxos places on you: durability. A teacher must write her page to disk before she replies. In engineering terms, an acceptor must write promised_n, accepted_n and accepted_value to stable storage before replying. An acceptor that keeps them in memory only and then reboots will re-promise as if it had never voted. The quorum-overlap argument collapses, because the overlapping member no longer remembers. That is the production lesson, and it is bigger than Paxos: a randomized soak that comes back green is not evidence that your durability is correct. A fault schedule has to be aimed, not merely random, and the checker has to assert on a property that the workload actually exercises. 12. Which two rules do all the work?

In one breath: Rule A: propose the reported value with the highest ticket, or your own if nobody reported anything. Rule B: it is chosen when three teachers wrote the same value under the same ticket. Delete either and the school books two trips.

Everything in Paxos is plumbing except two decision rules. Both are pure functions: no state, no messages, no timing. If you can apply these two by hand to an arbitrary set of promises, you understand Paxos.

Rule A — the phase-2 value rule. You have a majority of promises. What do you propose?

  among the promises you collected, look at those reporting an accepted value
    none report a value            ->  propose your OWN value
    some report a value            ->  propose the one with the HIGHEST
                                       accepted_n among them

Note what is not in that rule. Not the most common value. Not the first one you heard. Not the one from the teacher you trust most. The highest accepted_n, full stop. It does not matter how many teachers report it. One teacher reporting (n=7, "Zoo") beats any number of teachers reporting (n=3, "Museum").

🔮 Predict: Three teachers reply. One says "I wrote Zoo under ticket 4". Two say "I wrote Museum under ticket 2". What do you propose?

Reveal Zoo. Ticket 4 is the highest ticket reported, and that is the whole rule. If you said Museum because two teachers outvote one, the "majority of reports wins" model is in your head. It is the most common wrong version of this rule, and the fourth row below is its counterexample.

Apply it by hand. Four promise sets, each a majority of 5; what does the class rep send in phase 2?

A: (1,"Zoo") · B: none · C: none Zoo A single report is enough: (1,"Zoo") might already be chosen by a majority the proposer cannot see
A: (1,"Zoo") · C: (2,"Museum") · D: (2,"Museum") Museum Highest accepted_n = 2. The count (2 vs 1) is a coincidence, not the reason

The last two rows are the same shape with the numbers swapped, and they give opposite answers. If you got the fourth row wrong, the "majority of reports wins" model is still in your head. Section 16 comes back to it.

Rule B — the decision rule. When is something chosen?

  a value v is CHOSEN when a majority of acceptors have accepted
  the SAME (n, v) pair

Not "a majority hold value v". The same n and the same v. Three teachers holding "Zoo" under three different ticket numbers is not a decision. Experiment 3's aftermath shows the shape: A and B sit holding Zoo under ticket 1 forever, while the Museum under ticket 2 is the value that was actually chosen.

14. What does a real system add on top?

In one breath: Disk writes that are not optional, a leader, learners who actually get told, a way to change the teachers, and a log instead of one decision.

In the story, the school books one trip and stops. A real system is a school that books a trip every minute for years, whose teachers are replaced while it runs, and whose coach company must actually be told the Zoo was chosen. Everything in the first table below is one of those gaps.

What this lab deliberately omits:

Omitted here What production does
Durability (a checkbox here) Mandatory fsync of promised_n/accepted_n/accepted_value before every reply. Non-negotiable, and, per the soak above, not something a random test will catch you missing.
Deciding a sequence Real systems need a replicated log, not one value. That is Multi-Paxos, section 15.
Leader election A stable distinguished proposer, chosen by lease or by a separate election, to avoid the duel. Worth 6× the decision rate in the measurement above.
Learner propagation Here an omniscient observer notices a majority. Real systems either have acceptors broadcast Accepted to all learners, or use a distinguished learner that then informs the rest.

15. How does one decision become a whole log?

In one breath: The school now needs a decision every month. Run one Paxos per month, and let a class rep who has already been promised keep her promise for every future month. That is Multi-Paxos, and the class rep is what everyone calls the leader.

Everything so far decides one value, once, forever. No production system wants that. A database wants to decide command #1, then command #2, then command #3: an ordered, unbounded sequence that every replica applies in the same order. That is a replicated state machine, and the sequence is a log. In the story: the school does not need one trip, it needs a trip every month, and every teacher's calendar must list them in the same order.

🔮 Predict: You have working single-decree Paxos. The obvious way to get a log is to run one independent instance of it per log position. What is wrong with that?

Reveal Nothing is wrong with its safety. It is perfectly correct, and it is genuinely how Multi-Paxos is defined. The problem is cost: every single command now pays two round trips and risks its own private duel. On a 50 ms link that is 100 ms per command, and two clients writing at once can livelock any individual slot. If you said "it's unsafe", go back to section 11: nothing about running it many times touches the proof. The refinement below removes the first round trip in the common case without touching safety.

Version 1: one Paxos instance per slot. Slot 1 decides the first command, slot 2 the second, and so on. Each slot has its own proposal numbers, its own promises, its own accepted values. Safety per slot is exactly the theorem you already have. Cost: two round trips per command, and a duel is possible on every slot independently.

Break version 1. Two clients submit at the same instant. Both proposers try slot 5. They duel. Experiment 4, but now it can happen on every slot of every write, forever, in production, at 50 ms per round trip.

The refinement: phase 1 once, for all slots. Here is the observation that turns Paxos into a usable system. Prepare(n) asks a teacher to promise she will refuse anything below n. There is no reason that promise has to be about one month. Let Prepare(n) mean "I claim number n for slot k and every slot after it".

Now a proposer that completes phase 1 once holds a standing promise covering all future slots. For each new command it skips straight to Accept(n, command): one round trip per command. Phase 1 runs again only when the leadership changes.

That proposer is what everyone calls the leader, and notice what it is: not a role granted by an election protocol, but simply the proposer that currently holds phase-1 promises from a majority. Leadership in Paxos is a consequence of the algorithm, not an addition to it.

Basic Paxos, per command Multi-Paxos, stable leader
Round trips per command 2 1
Phase 1 runs every command only on leader change
Duelling possible on every slot only during a leader handover
Where the cost went — into the leader being a bottleneck and a failure point

What the leader does not buy you. Re-read the soak table in section 11: a leader took decisions from 36/500 to 220/500 and safety violations from 0 to 0. It is a liveness and throughput optimization. If the leader is wrong, duplicated, or partitioned away, safety still holds. Two proposers both believing they are leader is exactly the duel, which is slow, not incorrect. This is the same result your ../42-split-brain/ lab reaches from the other direction: the goal is never to prevent two leaders, it is to make the loser harmless.

The part that surprises people: holes. Multi-Paxos slots are independent, so a leader can have slot 7 chosen while slot 6 is still undecided. That is a gap in the log. It may also accept commands out of order. Both are legal and both are used: they are what lets a leader pipeline many slots concurrently instead of waiting for each one.

The price is that a gap must be filled before the state machine can apply anything past it, since a state machine must apply commands in order. A new leader therefore begins by running phase 1 over the tail of the log to discover which slots are decided. It proposes a harmless no-op into any hole it finds so the log can move forward.

This is the single biggest structural difference from Raft, and you have the ../40-raft/ lab to compare against. Raft forbids holes. Its log is contiguous by construction. A follower's log is either a prefix of the leader's, or it gets truncated. That one restriction is most of why Raft is easier to reason about and to implement. Multi-Paxos trades that simplicity for out-of-order pipelining. Raft's term is Paxos's proposal number spent on shifts rather than on individual values. Raft's mandatory no-op on winning an election is the same mechanism as the hole-filling no-op above, applied to a different rule.

What still has to happen per slot. Even with a stable leader, three things do not change:

  • each command still needs a majority to accept it (that is the durability of the write, and it is irreducible);
  • each slot's decision is still permanent;
  • every acceptor still writes to disk before replying.

The leader optimization removes a round trip. It removes nothing from the safety argument.

16. Which wrong ideas feel right?

In one breath: Nine beliefs that survive a first reading and then fail in a design review. Each one is stated, made tempting, and then killed with the smallest counterexample.

These are the mental models most likely to survive this lab and then be wrong in a design review. Each is stated as the belief, why it is tempting, and the smallest thing that kills it.

"Chosen means someone announced it." Tempting because every human decision has a moment of announcement. But chosen is a global property of the acceptors' state, and it can be true while no participant on earth knows it. The proposer may have crashed before any Accepted reply reached it. Smallest counterexample: three teachers write (1,"Zoo"), then the class rep goes home sick. Zoo is chosen. Nobody knows. Any future proposer will discover it and be forced to carry it. This is why learner is a separate role, and why a client that times out cannot conclude its write failed.

"A majority holding the same value means it is chosen." Tempting because it is nearly true. But the rule is a majority accepting the same (n, v) pair. Three teachers holding "Zoo" under numbers 1, 3 and 7 is not a decision, because no single proposal ever achieved a majority. (In practice you rarely construct this, but the distinction is what makes the induction in the safety proof work at all.)

"The proposer with the highest number wins." Tempting because higher numbers do beat lower ones at the acceptors. But winning the right to propose is not winning the choice of value. A proposer with n = 9,000,000 that discovers (3,"Zoo") in a single promise must propose "Zoo". Proposal numbers order attempts, never values. The whole point is that you cannot buy your way past history with a big number. That is exactly what a naive "just use a bigger timestamp" scheme gets wrong.

"The value chosen is the one most acceptors reported." This is the wrong version of Rule A, and it is the most dangerous one because it agrees with the correct rule most of the time. Row 4 of the table in section 12 is the counterexample: one report of (4,"Zoo") beats two reports of (2,"Museum"). The consequence of getting this backwards is shown in the same section: a never-chosen value resurrected on top of a chosen one.

"Phase 1 is how the proposer gets permission." Half true, and it hides the important half. Permission (promised_n) is the mechanism. The purpose of phase 1 is to learn what has already been accepted. Delete the learning and keep the permission, which is variant B in section 12, and you get two decisions on a healthy cluster. If you remember phase 1 as "asking permission" you will eventually agree to a design that skips it as an optimization.

"Paxos elects a leader." It does not. Basic Paxos has no leader and no election. Any number of proposers may run at once and the protocol is correct regardless. A leader is something you add (section 15) to escape the duel, and two simultaneous leaders are a performance problem, not a correctness one. When someone says "we use Paxos for leader election", they mean they used Paxos to decide the value "the leader is node 3". That is consensus on a value. The value then gets interpreted as leadership, usually with a lease attached.

"Paxos is 2PC with more steps." Both have two phases and a coordinating role, so the confusion is understandable, and your ../45-two-phase-commit/ lab is the direct comparison. They solve different problems and fail differently.

2PC solves atomic commitment: all participants agree to commit one transaction, every participant has a veto, and if the coordinator dies after the votes the participants block forever holding locks. Paxos solves consensus: a majority is enough, no participant has a veto, and if the proposer dies any other proposer completes the work by picking a higher number. 2PC needs everyone; Paxos needs half plus one.

"Paxos makes the system available." It makes the majority side available. The minority side refuses service. That is the entire point, and it is CAP's CP corner made concrete (../24-cap-theorem/). A Paxos group is strictly less available than a single node for writes when you count the partition cases. What you bought was that it never disagrees with itself.

"More acceptors means more throughput." The opposite. Every additional acceptor adds a machine that must be waited on by some quorum and adds nothing to write capacity. Five acceptors tolerate two failures; seven tolerate three and are slower. Scaling out means many Paxos groups, one per shard. That is the same conclusion your ../40-raft/ lab reaches as multi-raft, and why ../35-consistent-hashing/ turns up next to consensus in every real design.

Acceptor The voting, remembering role (the teacher); holds promised_n, accepted_n, accepted_value.
Learner The role that only wants to discover the outcome (the coach company).
Quorum / majority More than half the acceptors; any two quorums always share a member.
Proposal number n The unique, increasing ticket for one attempt. Orders attempts, never values.
Prepare(n) Phase-1 request: claim number n, and ask what has already been accepted.
Promise(n, …) Phase-1 reply: granted, lower numbers refused from now on, last accepted entry attached.
Accept(n, v) Phase-2 request: write value v under number n.
Accepted(n, v) Phase-2 reply: written down.
NACK Refusal, because a higher number was already promised.
Accepted (by one acceptor) One acceptor wrote it — not a decision.
Chosen / decided A majority accepted the same (n, v) — permanent, even if nobody knows yet.
Rule A (the value rule) With a majority of promises: propose the reported value with the highest accepted_n, or your own if none reported anything.
Rule B (the decision rule) A value is chosen when a majority have accepted the same (n, v) pair.
Safety At most one value is ever chosen — holds under every message ordering.
Liveness Some value eventually gets chosen — not guaranteed (duelling proposers, FLP).
Adoption A proposer abandoning its own value for the highest-numbered accepted value phase 1 revealed.
Witness The overlap member of two majorities — the acceptor whose report forces adoption (amber outline in the simulator).
Livelock Proposers endlessly invalidating each other's promises; safe but never done.
Stable (durable) storage Disk-flushed acceptor state; answering before flushing is the amnesia bug.
Single-decree Paxos Agreement on exactly one value — sections 1–14 of this lab.
Multi-Paxos One Paxos instance per log slot, with a stable leader whose single phase 1 covers all future slots — one round trip per command.
Slot One position in the replicated log; one independent Paxos instance.
Hole / gap A slot decided later than a slot after it; legal in Multi-Paxos, forbidden in Raft, filled with a no-op.
No-op A harmless command proposed into a hole so the state machine can move past it.
Leader In Paxos, simply the proposer currently holding phase-1 promises from a majority. Not elected by the protocol; worth ~6× the decision rate in this lab's measurement.
Replicated state machine Every replica applying the same commands in the same order — what a consensus log is for.
Partial synchrony The practical assumption that the network is usually well behaved, which is where real liveness comes from.
FLP impossibility The proof that no deterministic asynchronous consensus can guarantee liveness.
Byzantine failure A node that lies rather than merely crashing — outside Paxos's assumptions entirely.

Part 0 — Explain & identify

0.1 One honest version: Several machines must end up believing the same single answer even though any of them can be off at any moment and their messages can vanish. Just announcing an answer fails because two announcers can each convince a different part of the group, and nobody can even tell whether "the" answer exists yet. The fix: before pushing your answer, ask more than half the group what they have already written down — because any two more-than-half groups must share someone, you are guaranteed to meet a witness of any earlier answer, and the rule is that you must then push THAT answer instead of yours. So the first answer to reach more than half can never be displaced — everyone later just ends up spreading it further.

0.2 Accepted: one acceptor wrote the value in its notebook — a local fact, and one that may never be joined by enough others. Chosen: a majority wrote the same proposal — a global, permanent fact that no one may even know yet. Experiment 3 exists purely for this gap.

0.3 (a) The stars sit on the three teachers of the first deciding majority (A, B, C) — members of the quorum that made the decision. (b) C has the amber witness outline: it is the overlap member that saw the Zoo decided and reported it to Rep 2. (c) On screen: "promised #n" and "wrote: value / under slot #n" — in the engineering model: promised_n and (accepted_n, accepted_value).

0.4 Rule A, the value rule: with a majority of promises in hand, propose the reported value carrying the highest accepted_n; only if no promise reported any value may you propose your own. Glossary term: adoption. Delete it and experiment 2 breaks — measured outcome chosen = ["Zoo", "Museum"], with witness C reporting the Zoo and being ignored.

Rule B, the decision rule: a value is chosen when a majority of acceptors have accepted the same (n, v) pair. Glossary term: chosen. Weaken it to "a majority hold the same value" and you would declare decisions that no single proposal ever achieved.

0.5 Evidence the second proposer ran: C, D and E all show p=2 — a promise number only a second round could have set — and C's value has been rewritten under @2. Evidence it did not get what it wanted: the value everywhere is Zoo, and P2 wanted Museum. The acceptor responsible is C, the only member of both {A,B,C} and {C,D,E}. It reported (1,"Zoo") in its promise and Rule A did the rest.

Part 1 — Predict

1.1 A -> P1 PROMISE(n=9) [already accepted (n=5, "Zoo")], and A becomes A[p=9 wrote=Zoo@5].

It reports the accepted pair (5,"Zoo"), not the promise 7. Those are two different facts. promised_n = 7 means "I told someone they could have number 7", which may have gone nowhere — the proposer holding 7 might have crashed before phase 2 ever happened. accepted = (5,"Zoo") is the last thing actually written down, and it is the only thing that could be part of a decision. A proposer needs to know what might already have been chosen; an unused promise never was.

1.2 Accepted — A replies ACCEPTED(7, "Museum") and becomes A[p=7 wrote=Museum@7].

>= is necessary because granting a promise sets promised_n = n. With a strict >, an acceptor would reject the phase-2 message from the very proposer whose phase-1 request it just granted — n == promised_n is the expected case for a well-behaved proposer. The measured consequence in stage 12: chosen = [] with 5 NACKs, one proposer, no faults, all five acceptors reachable. Paxos could not complete a single round, ever.

1.3 With A, D, E: P2 proposes "Zoo". With C, D, E: P2 proposes "Museum".

The difference is who was asked. Only A had written "Zoo" down, so a quorum containing A discovers it and a quorum avoiding A does not. This is the honest and slightly uncomfortable truth about Paxos: when nothing has been chosen, the outcome genuinely depends on who you ask — and that is fine, because there is no decision to protect. The moment a majority has accepted, no quorum can avoid all of them, and the dependence on "who you ask" disappears. That transition is the entire safety property.

1.4 The light never turns red and the decision field never leaves none. Safety is preserved (at most one value is ever chosen — zero is at most one); liveness is violated (nothing is chosen despite no crashes and no lost messages). The end state, verified:

  A[p=8 wrote=-]  B[p=8 wrote=-]  C[p=8 wrote=-]  D[p=0 wrote=-]  E[p=0 wrote=-]

Every wrote= field is empty. Eight rounds of perfectly healthy networking produced not one written value.

1.5 Quorum for 4 is 3, tolerating 1 failure — exactly the same as a cluster of 3 (quorum 2, tolerates 1). A cluster of 5 has quorum 3 and tolerates 2. The fourth machine bought you nothing: no extra fault tolerance, one more machine that can fail, one more to pay for, and a new failure mode where a 2–2 split leaves neither side able to form a quorum. This is why real deployments are 3, 5 or 7.

1.6 First set {A:(4,"Zoo"), C:(2,"Museum"), D:(2,"Museum")} → propose Zoo. Second set {A:(1,"Zoo"), C:(2,"Museum"), D:(2,"Museum")} → propose Museum.

The count is a trap and it is worth saying out loud: the number of acceptors reporting a value has nothing to do with the answer. Both sets have two reports of Museum and one of Zoo. The answer flips purely because accepted_n moved from 4 to 1. The reason is that a higher accepted_n means a later round got that value accepted somewhere, and that later round might have completed as a majority at acceptors you did not talk to. Older news is provably not the decision; newer news might be.

Part 2 — Modify

2.1 Smallest schedule: P1 gets Zoo chosen by {A,B,C} at n=1; P2 then runs at n=2 with {C,D,E}. C reports (1,"Zoo"), P2 ignores it, proposes Museum, and C, D and E accept. Measured: chosen = ["Zoo", "Museum"].

The invalidated sentence is the last step of the stage-11 argument: "The proposer is then forced by the phase-2 rule to propose v." Quorum overlap still happened — the overlapping acceptor still reported the chosen value — but the proposer ignored the report. Overlap only buys you safety if you act on what it tells you.

2.2 With quorum = 2 on five acceptors, {A,B} and {D,E} are both "quorums" and they share nobody. Measured: {A,B} accept Zoo, {D,E} accept Museum, chosen = ["Zoo", "Museum"], neither group ever hearing of the other.

N/2 + 1 is the smallest number that forces intersection: two sets each larger than half of N cannot be disjoint, because their sizes would sum to more than N. It is not a safety margin someone chose conservatively — it is the exact threshold at which the pigeonhole argument starts working, and one below it the argument does not weaken, it disappears.

2.3 Safety breaks, and it is the same bug as 2.1 seen from the other end. In 2.1 the proposer refuses to listen; in 2.3 the acceptor refuses to speak. Safety needs the information to be both reported and acted on, so breaking either half breaks the same guarantee.

It is arguably worse than 2.1 in practice, for two reasons. The proposer is correct and cannot detect the problem — a promise reporting "nothing accepted" is indistinguishable from a genuinely fresh acceptor. And a single misbehaving acceptor is enough, whereas 2.1 requires the proposer implementation itself to be wrong. This is also the closest thing in this lab to a Byzantine failure: an acceptor that does not lie about its promise but does lie about its history.

2.4 On a NACK, wait a random number of rounds (exponential backoff with jitter is the usual shape) before issuing the next Prepare. The duel ends as soon as one proposer happens to get through both phases before the other's next prepare; with independent random delays that typically takes a handful of rounds, and the probability of continuing decays geometrically.

It is probabilistic, not a guarantee, and FLP says it cannot be otherwise: in an asynchronous system where even one process may crash, no deterministic protocol guarantees termination. Backoff does not defeat the theorem; it makes the bad case unlikely under network conditions that are usually benign. Your ../33-flp-impossibility/ lab is the sharp version of this: its adversary defeats rotating-coordinator Paxos for 5,000 steps while delivering every single message, so "we added backoff" is not an answer to it. The stronger practical fix is a leader, which removes the contention rather than randomizing it — but electing a leader is itself subject to FLP, so the impossibility is relocated, never removed.

2.5 Achievable for 2 of 5, impossible for 3 of 5 — and "impossible" here is not "hard", it is ruled out by the protocol. Once 3 of 5 have accepted the same proposal number, every future quorum of 3 contains at least one of them, that acceptor reports the value in its promise, and Rule A forces the new proposer to re-propose it. The exact step at which the second attempt is forced to change its mind is the moment its third promise arrives — that is when it applies Rule A and discovers it is a courier, not an author. You will find you cannot construct the schedule no matter how you order or drop messages, which is exactly what the proof says.

2.6 No, gossip does not let you drop phase 1, and the reason is the interesting part. Gossip can tell an acceptor what other acceptors have already accepted. It cannot tell it what is in flight — an Accept(n,v) that has left a proposer and not yet arrived. A proposer that skipped phase 1 on the strength of gossip would be reasoning about a snapshot that a delayed message can invalidate after the fact.

Phase 1 does not gather information about the present; it changes the future, by making every acceptor in a majority refuse everything below n. That is why it cannot be replaced by a read of any kind, however fresh. This is the same shape as the optimistic-concurrency argument in ../10-optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking/: checking a value and acting on it are only safe together if something prevents the value changing in between.

3.1

  start        A[p=0 -]      B[p=0 -]      C[p=0 -]      D[p=0 -]  E[p=0 -]

Zoo was never chosen. It reached exactly one acceptor. A is left holding it forever, and that is correct rather than a leak — an acceptor's notebook records what it was asked to write, not what was decided.

3.2 At step 3, A, B and C all accept, so Zoo reaches 3 of 5 and is chosen the instant the third acceptance lands. At step 4, C now reports (1,"Zoo") in its promise to P2. At step 5, Rule A fires. At step 6 the value is Zoo, and the final state is:

  A[p=1 Zoo@1]  B[p=1 Zoo@1]  C[p=2 Zoo@2]  D[p=2 Zoo@2]  E[p=2 Zoo@2]
  chosen = ["Zoo"]

C forced the difference. It is the only acceptor in both {A,B,C} and {C,D,E}, and it could not have been avoided: any three of five must meet any other three. Note also what happened to D and E — they had never heard of the Zoo, they were recruited by the proposer who wanted the Museum, and they end up holding the Zoo. The second round did not fail; it succeeded at carrying somebody else's value.

3.3 At step 3 all three acceptors refuse P1's Accept(1,"Zoo"), because each has promised_n = 2 and 1 >= 2 is false. Each replies NACK. Verified end state:

  A[p=2 wrote=-]  B[p=2 wrote=-]  C[p=2 wrote=-]   chosen = []

P1's view of the world is stale: it holds a majority of promises that are no longer worth anything, because a promise is a commitment to ignore older numbers and someone newer has since arrived. What P1 should do is abandon number 1, pick a number higher than anything it has seen in the NACKs, and start again from phase 1 — not retry the same Accept, which will be refused forever.

There is no failure anywhere in this trace: no crash, no lost message, no misbehaviour. This is the ordinary case that makes proposal numbers necessary, and it is also exactly the mechanism that produces livelock when it happens repeatedly. The same rule that makes a late message harmless is the rule that can stop progress — which is why safety and liveness pull in opposite directions here.

Part 4 — Spot the flaw

4.1 The bug is replying before the state is durable. It breaks the durability obligation from stage 11 and, through it, the safety invariant: an acceptor that acknowledges a promise or an acceptance and then reboots before the write lands comes back believing it never voted. The quorum-overlap argument then fails silently, because the overlapping acceptor no longer remembers what it must report. Reproduced deliberately by experiment 5:

  A[p=2 Museum@2]  B[p=2 Museum@2]  C[p=2 Museum@2]  D[p=0 -]  E[p=0 -]
  chosen = ["Zoo", "Museum"]        <-- SAFETY VIOLATED

CI never caught it because the tests never crash a node between the reply and the flush — the window is invisible unless the harness models durability explicitly.

And "we ran a chaos test and it was green" is not a defence, which is the part worth internalising. Stage 11 ran 500 randomised schedules with acceptors that forget everything on restart — the bug present, deliberately — and found zero safety violations, identical to the durable run. Breaking safety this way needs two decisions in one run, and decisions are rare under packet loss; random chaos almost never reaches the state where durability matters. The bug is trivial to reproduce when aimed at (six deliberate steps) and nearly invisible when not. A green soak is evidence about your soak's coverage, not about your durability.

The fix is a synchronous flush before every reply. Yes, it costs a disk round trip per message; that cost is the algorithm. Batching many proposals into one flush is the legitimate optimization. Replying early is not.

4.2 Measured counterexample:

  blind Accept(5, "Zoo")    to {A, B, C}  ->  chosen = ["Zoo"]
  blind Accept(9, "Museum") to {C, D, E}  ->  chosen = ["Zoo", "Museum"]
  C ends at [p=9 wrote=Museum@9]           (C accepted because 9 >= 5)

C accepts the Museum because 9 is not below its promise, and {C,D,E} is a second legitimate majority. Two different values chosen, no faults, no misbehaviour.

Phase 1 is not for permission — it is for discovery. The promise is almost a side effect; the payload that matters is the acceptor telling the proposer what has already been accepted. Skip phase 1 and a proposer chooses a value while blind to the past, which is the broken "Attempt 3" from stage 4 wearing a proposal number.

The subtlety worth carrying: Multi-Paxos genuinely does skip phase 1 for later slots — but only after a leader has run it once for that ballot, so the discovery has already happened and the promise already covers those slots. It is an amortization, not a removal. If you remember phase 1 as "permission" you will not be able to tell the legitimate optimization from this broken one.

4.3 The bug is waiting for all acceptors' promises instead of a majority. Safety is untouched — a stricter requirement never chooses more — but liveness now requires every single acceptor to be alive and reachable.

This is "Attempt 2" from stage 4: require all five to agree, and one absent teacher stops the school. It is a realistic mistake because "wait for everyone" looks safer, and it is: it is also useless, because the entire point of a majority is that it is the largest quorum you can demand while still tolerating failures. One crashed acceptor out of five now stalls the cluster forever, and the failure is silent — no error, no NACK, just a proposer waiting.

4.4 Smallest disagreeing promise set: {A:(4,"Zoo"), C:(2,"Museum"), D:(2,"Museum")}. Most-common says Museum; highest-n says Zoo.

Full schedule where the wrong rule resurrects a dead value (measured):

  1. P1 at n=1 with {A,B,C}; Accept(1,"Zoo") reaches A ONLY   -> chosen = []
       (Zoo is on one acceptor: a minority, never chosen)
  2. P2 at n=2 with {C,D,E}; all three accept Museum          -> chosen = ["Museum"]
  3. A third attempt at n=3 asks {A,C,D} and sees
       A:(1,"Zoo")  C:(2,"Museum")  D:(2,"Museum")
     highest-n  -> Museum   (correct: Museum is the chosen value)
     most-common-> Zoo      (wrong)
  4. With the wrong rule: proposes Zoo, {A,C,D} accept        -> chosen = ["Museum", "Zoo"]

The value that was never chosen is written on top of the one that was. Note the shape of the trap: the wrong rule agrees with the right one in the overwhelming majority of real schedules, so a test suite that does not deliberately construct this shape will pass.

4.5 Yes, easily — for example:

  A[p=2 wrote=-]  B[p=1 wrote=-]  C[p=1 Zoo@1]  D[p=2 Museum@2]  E[p=2 Museum@2]
  chosen = []     safety holds

C holds Zoo, D and E hold Museum, and nothing is wrong. Replicas disagreeing is the normal, expected state of a Paxos cluster — an acceptor's notebook is a record of what it was asked to write, not a claim about what was decided. Only a majority holding the same proposal number constitutes a decision.

It becomes a violation the moment two different values each reach a majority at some point in time — 3 of 5 for Zoo and, at any later moment, 3 of 5 for Museum. Note "at some point": it is not about the final state. Even if the cluster later converges to a single value, if two different values were each chosen at any instant, consensus was broken and something downstream may already have acted on the wrong one.

Part 5 — Handle a failure

5.1 (a) Exactly three fields: promised_n, accepted_n, accepted_value. (b) Flushed before the reply leaves — not after, not concurrently, not "shortly". (c) On restart, the acceptor must reload all three and must not answer any message until it has; an acceptor answering from a blank slate while its disk read is still in flight is the same bug with a smaller window.

The write barrier matters more than the fields. Reply-then-flush means a crash can land in the gap where the outside world holds a Promise or an Accepted that the acceptor itself no longer remembers — experiment 5's amnesia, shrunk from "reboot without a disk" to "reboot inside a two-millisecond window". With flush-before-reply, a restarted acceptor rejoins knowing every promise it ever made, and experiment 5's schedule replayed against it ends with one decision: A, B and C come back still remembering the Zoo, and Rep 2's phase 1 meets a witness instead of a blank room. That control run is verified — identical crash, durability on, chosen = ["Zoo"].

Why neither a unit test nor a random soak reliably catches it: a unit test does not model the crash window at all unless someone thought to write it, and the random soak was measured in stage 11 finding zero violations with the bug present. You catch this with an aimed fault — crash between reply and flush, restart, run a second proposer, assert one value — which is the entire premise of ../44-jepsen/.

5.2 The retry must propose the same request, carrying the same client-chosen request id — never a "fresh" attempt, because the first attempt may already be chosen. The system may never assume the first attempt failed: no reply means unknown, not no.

The only honest resolution is a read: consult the protocol (or a learner) and see what was actually chosen. If it is your request id, your write happened, the retry is a duplicate, and idempotency absorbs it. If a different value won, your write lost and the client must be told the truth rather than have the system quietly pretend.

Note what is doing the work: Paxos guarantees one value is chosen; it says nothing about whose value. Making the retry safe is the request id — that is ../49-idempotency/'s job, not consensus's. A great many systems get consensus right and then lose money on this exact seam.

5.3 The surviving two can read whatever they already hold, and they can tell you honestly that they do not know the current state. They cannot decide anything — no quorum can form — and they must not pretend otherwise. Liveness is gone; safety is intact. The cluster is doing precisely what it was built to do.

"Just lower the quorum to 2 so we can keep serving" is the most dangerous sentence in the incident because it retroactively breaks every decision the cluster has ever made. The three lost acceptors may hold a chosen value that neither survivor knows about; a quorum of 2 among the survivors can decide a different value for the same slot, and now two different answers were each chosen. Stage 12's first counterfactual is exactly this, measured: chosen = ["Zoo", "Museum"]. Worse, it fails silently — the cluster comes back up and serves traffic, and the divergence surfaces days later in somebody's reconciliation report.

The actual recovery is reconfiguration: bring machines back with their disks intact if at all possible (their state is the point), and if the disks are genuinely gone, perform a deliberate, human-authorised membership change that is recorded as a decision, accepting explicitly that any decision made by the lost majority and unknown to the survivors is lost with it. The cost is downtime and a written acknowledgement of possible data loss. That is the honest price, and it is much lower than the price of a silent second decision.

Part 6 — Multi-Paxos

6.1 The log has slot 11 chosen and slot 10 undecided — a hole. The state machine may apply nothing at or past slot 10, because a state machine must apply commands in order; slot 11's command sits decided but unapplied. The leader must retry slot 10 — it still holds phase-1 rights at n=5, so it can resend Accept(5, cmdA) without redoing phase 1.

This is the everyday case, not an error. Pipelining slots is why Multi-Paxos permits holes, and a lost message costs a retransmission rather than a stall of everything behind it.

6.2 Before accepting a new command at slot 12, the new leader must run phase 1 at n=6 over the tail of the log — every slot from its last known applied position onward. For each slot the promises tell it one of three things: a value was accepted (it must re-propose that value, Rule A, per slot); nothing was accepted (the slot is free); or the slot is already known-decided.

It proposes a no-op into any hole rather than skipping it because slot numbers are the ordering, and skipping one would mean the replicas disagree about what position 10 contains — one replica applying 9, 11, 12 and another applying 9, 10, 11, 12 are no longer the same state machine. A no-op is a command that changes nothing but occupies the slot, letting the log move past it with every replica agreeing on what happened there. This is the same mechanism as the no-op a new Raft leader appends on winning an election.

6.3 Multi-Paxos permits holes: it buys out-of-order pipelining — a leader can have many slots in flight and one slow slot does not block the others — and it costs a much harder recovery path, since a new leader must run phase 1 across a range of slots and reason about each independently. Raft forbids holes: it buys a contiguous log, so a single (index, term) comparison certifies unlimited history by induction and log repair is one truncation, and it costs head-of-line blocking, since a follower missing entry 10 cannot usefully receive 11.

For a configuration store at a few writes per second, take Raft's restriction. You have no throughput problem for pipelining to solve, and the thing you will actually spend your life on is understanding the system at 3am during a failover. Trade throughput you do not need for recovery you will definitely use. (The honest counter-argument: if you are running many replication groups per machine, per-group simplicity matters less than aggregate throughput — which is why high-write systems still reach for Paxos-family designs.)

6.4 Stage 11 measured the same soak twice: duelling proposers decided 36 of 500 runs, a single non-overlapping leader decided 220 of 500, and safety violations were zero in both. The leader changed how often the system got anything done and did not touch what it was allowed to do. It is a throughput mechanism.

So if your leader-election logic is buggy and produces two leaders at once, the precise consequence is that the two leaders duel. Each invalidates the other's promises, both keep bumping proposal numbers, throughput collapses toward zero, and the log stops advancing. Nothing is decided twice; nothing wrong is committed. You will see it as a latency and availability incident — rising NACK rates, leadership flapping in your metrics — not as data corruption. That is exactly the shape your ../42-split-brain/ lab argues for: the goal is never to prevent two leaders, it is to make the loser harmless.

Part 7 — Design

7.1 A worked answer to compare against; yours may differ defensibly.

  • (a) Acceptors: 5, spread 2 + 2 + 1. Losing any one datacentre leaves at least 3 — a quorum — alive. Three acceptors (1+1+1) also survives a DC loss, but then a single machine failure in either surviving DC stalls you; 5 buys real headroom. Even counts buy nothing (stage 10's odd-size point). Nine (3+3+3, quorum 5) is defensible if you can afford the extra cross-DC latency per decision.
  • (b) One stable leader. Duelling proposers at 50 ms per round is experiment 4 with a latency bill attached: every gazump costs a cross-DC round trip. Place the leader in the DC nearest the write-heavy clients. Leader election is itself a Paxos decision, and the lease it holds is what kills the duel.
  • (c) Steady state: roughly one cross-DC round trip, ~50–100 ms per decision — the leader already holds phase-1 rights and needs only phase 2 to a quorum, which must include at least one remote DC. Failover: detection timeout plus a full two-phase round for the new leader to re-prepare across the log tail, so a few hundred milliseconds to seconds, tunable by how twitchy you make detection. Too twitchy means spurious leader flapping, which is the duel again.
  • (d) Dashboard: (1) leader identity over time — flapping is the system showing you experiment 4; (2) commit latency percentiles — a creeping p99 means a slow quorum member; (3) stalled log slots / holes not yet filled — the thing that blocks the state machine while everything else looks healthy. The one alert: leader elections per minute above a threshold. It fires before users notice and it catches nearly every real problem, because both network trouble and machine trouble show up as election churn first.
  • (e) Survives unchanged from this lab: acceptor state and its flush-before-reply rule, Rule A and Rule B, quorum intersection as the entire safety argument. Added for production: the log of instances (Multi-Paxos), leader election and leases, snapshots and compaction, membership change, learners and catch-up for lagging replicas, and client request ids (your 5.2 answer). That is stage 14's omissions list, and every item on it is real work.

7.2 The comparison that matters:

Basic Paxos per seat (LWT-style) One Raft group, seats in the state machine
Cost per operation Two round trips plus a read-modify-write; roughly 4× a plain write One round trip through the leader
Blast radius of one slow node Contained — it only affects quorums for the seats it holds The leader being slow degrades everything
At 10× the seats Scales naturally; each key is independent, no coordination between them The single group becomes the bottleneck; you must shard into many groups
What on-call sees when it breaks Elevated latency and contention on specific hot keys Leader elections, and one incident that covers all seats at once

Ship the Raft group for a bounded seat inventory — one event, one venue, tens of thousands of seats. You get one round trip per reservation, one thing to operate, and the state machine can enforce invariants across seats (no double-booking, hold limits per customer) that per-key consensus simply cannot express. Per-key Paxos gives you consensus on each key and no way to say anything true about two keys at once.

What would change my mind: contention shape. If reservations are spread across a very large key space with almost no cross-key invariants — millions of independent items, each contended by a few clients — the per-key design's independence becomes the dominant factor and the single leader becomes the liability. That is precisely the trade Cassandra's lightweight transactions make, and it is why they exist despite costing 4×.

7.3 There is no single answer; the point is that the scenario must be small, concrete, and end in something a customer notices. A good answer names five acceptors, real proposal numbers, an explicit message order, and finishes with two decisions or a permanent stall — not with "and then it could get inconsistent".

The four safety-breaking counterfactuals in stage 12 are the calibration set: quorum below a majority (two disjoint deciders), Rule A deleted (the witness is ignored), Rule A inverted (a dead value resurrected), and phase 1 skipped (blind accepts). If your scenario needed more than about six messages, there is probably a smaller version of it — and finding the smaller version is the exercise. If it needed fewer, you may have skipped a step that the protocol would actually have caught; walk it again message by message against the acceptor rules in stage 9.
