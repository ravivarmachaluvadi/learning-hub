Sibling lab: ../48-transactional-outbox/ — how each saga participant reliably publishes its events. This lab assumes messages get delivered and focuses on the layer above: coordinating many steps. The two patterns are almost always deployed together.

1. WHY does this exist?

One business action often spans several services, each with its own database: "book a trip" touches flights, hotels, and car rentals. A database transaction can make several writes inside one database all-or-nothing, but nothing built into databases makes writes across three different services' databases all-or-nothing. The saga pattern exists to get the effect of all-or-nothing across services — without any shared transaction.

2. THE PROBLEM, as a story

You run a travel agency. A customer wants a package trip: a flight, a hotel room, and a rental car — all three or none. Half a trip (flight but no hotel) is worse than no trip: the customer pays for a flight to a city where they'll sleep in the airport.

The airline, the hotel, and the car-rental company are three separate businesses. Each has its own booking book, its own phone line, its own policies. There is no planet on which all three slam their books shut and write your booking in perfect unison. You book with one phone call at a time — and between calls, the world can go wrong: the car office can be sold out, your own office can lose power mid-way, a fax can arrive twice.

3. THE NAIVE SOLUTIONS

Naive #1 — "just use one transaction." Wrap all three bookings in one @Transactional method. Doesn't exist: a transaction is a promise made by one database about its own writes. Three services, three databases, three independent commits. The annotation covers whichever database you're connected to and silently ignores the two network calls.

Naive #2 — "then make all three promise together" (this one has a real name: two-phase commit, 2PC). A coordinator asks all three: "prepared to commit?" — everyone locks their row and says yes — then "commit!". This genuinely gives atomicity, and it is exactly what the outbox lab called "the road not taken", for the same reasons: every participant must speak the protocol (most SaaS APIs, message brokers, and NoSQL stores don't), everyone holds locks while waiting on the slowest participant, and if the coordinator dies after "prepare", participants sit blocked, rows locked, unable to decide, until it returns. Availability of the whole is the product of the availability of everyone. Across microservices — let alone across a real airline's API — 2PC is a non-starter.

Naive #3 — "just call them one by one and hope." Book flight, book hotel, book car. Works until the car call fails — and now you hold a flight and a hotel you never asked to keep, with no plan for getting rid of them.

4. WHY NAIVE #3 FAILS, concretely

Flight booked (₹8,000 charged). Hotel booked (₹5,000 charged). Car: "sold out." The code throws an exception, returns HTTP 500 — and the flight and hotel bookings stay. They were separate commits in other services; your exception rolls back nothing anywhere else. The customer is charged ₹13,000 for a trip that doesn't exist. Retrying later double-books the flight. Naive #3 isn't wrong about calling one at a time — that part is forced by physics. It's wrong because it has no plan for partial success.

5. THE KEY IDEA

Accept that the steps commit one at a time — and prepare, for every step, an explicit "undo" action. Run the steps in order; if one fails, run the undo actions of every completed step in reverse order. You can't roll back another service's committed transaction, but you can cancel a hotel room. All-or-nothing stops being a database guarantee and becomes a choreographed sequence of ordinary transactions — forward to the end, or backward to the start.

6. TERMINOLOGY

  • Saga — a sequence of local transactions where each step has a defined undo; on failure, completed steps are undone in reverse. (The name comes from a 1987 paper by Garcia-Molina & Salem about long-running transactions in a single database; microservices adopted it wholesale.)
  • Local transaction — an ordinary ACID transaction inside ONE service's own database (hotel writes its own bookings row).
  • Compensating transaction (compensation) — the explicit undo for a step: cancelBooking compensates book. A new forward transaction that reverses the business effect — not a database rollback.
  • Orchestration — one coordinator (the orchestrator) tells each service what to do next and decides when to compensate. Flow lives in one place.
  • Choreography — no coordinator: each service reacts to the previous service's events ("HotelBooked" → car service books). Flow is distributed across subscriptions.
  • Saga log — the orchestrator's durable record of which steps have completed, so a crashed orchestrator can resume where it left off.
  • Compensatable / pivot / retryable steps — the three roles a step can play: compensatable (has an undo, runs before the point of no return), the pivot (the go/no-go step — once it commits, the saga must finish), and retryable (after the pivot; cannot fail permanently, only be retried until they succeed).
  • Semantic lock — marking mid-saga state explicitly (status=PENDING instead of BOOKED) so other transactions know it's provisional — a business-level substitute for the isolation sagas don't have.
  • Idempotent — safe to apply twice (see the outbox lab, section 6 — same word, same reason: retries are everywhere).

7. MENTAL MODEL

Carry this picture: a saga is a two-way staircase. Each stair is a committed local transaction. You climb one stair at a time; at every stair you can still walk back down, because each stair has a matching "down" action. Somewhere there is a landing — the pivot — after which there is no down staircase, only up: the remaining stairs must eventually be climbed. And crucially, the staircase is in public: anyone can see you standing mid-way, holding bookings that might be gone tomorrow.

9. THE ACTUAL MECHANISM, step by step (orchestration)

Concrete case: trip-42, each service starts with capacity 3.

Step 0 — start. The orchestrator writes SAGA_STARTED for trip-42 to its saga log (a durable table in the orchestrator's own database), then begins.

Step 1 — flight. Orchestrator → flight service: book(trip-42). The flight service, in one local ACID transaction of its own, checks capacity, decrements 3→2, records trip-42: BOOKED. Orchestrator appends flight:done to the log.

Step 2 — hotel. Same shape: hotel books, capacity 3→2, log gets hotel:done.

Step 3 — car. Same shape; on success the log gets car:done and then SAGA_COMPLETED. Trip exists. Notice what never existed: any distributed transaction, any cross-service lock, any moment where two databases had to agree simultaneously.

The failure path (car sold out) is section 10(a). The lab implements exactly this: Orchestrator.run and SagaLog in SagaLab.java.

    O->>F: book(trip-42)
    F-->>O: booked (local commit, cap 3→2)
    O->>O: log flight:done

    H-->>O: booked (local commit, cap 3→2)
    O->>O: log hotel:done

    C-->>O: booked (local commit, cap 3→2)
    O->>O: log car:done, SAGA_COMPLETED

The same saga as choreography has no orchestrator and no saga log; the flow lives in subscriptions: the flight service listens for TripRequested, books, and publishes FlightBooked; the hotel listens for FlightBooked; the car listens for HotelBooked. Failure events (CarFailed) trigger the cancellation chain backwards (HotelCancelled → flight cancels). Where do those events travel, and how does "book + publish event" stay atomic inside each service? That is literally the transactional outbox — each participant writes its booking and its event in one local transaction, and a relay delivers it. Saga on top, outbox underneath.

10. FAILURE SCENARIOS — walk each one

(a) A middle/late step fails (car sold out). The orchestrator reads its log — flight:done, hotel:done — and runs compensations in reverse order: hotel.cancel(trip-42) (capacity 2→3), then flight.cancel (2→3), then logs SAGA_ABORTED. Reverse order matters when steps depend on each other (you release the hotel booked for those flight dates before releasing the flight). Run: java SagaLab.java car-fails-compensate.

    car.book(trip-2) -> FAILED: car: sold out
    COMPENSATE hotel.cancel(trip-2) -> cancelled (capacity back to 3)
    COMPENSATE flight.cancel(trip-2) -> cancelled (capacity back to 3)
    saga trip-2: ABORTED (all completed steps undone)

(b) The orchestrator crashes mid-saga. ⚠️ The important one. Say it dies after hotel.book() succeeded but before writing hotel:done to the log. A new orchestrator starts, reads the log for trip-42: flight:done — skip flight; nothing about hotel — call hotel.book(trip-42) again. The hotel service sees trip-42 already in its bookings table and answers "already booked" without charging again. This is the same lesson as the outbox relay's gap, one layer up: a durable log tells the coordinator where it roughly was; participant idempotency absorbs the uncertainty of "did my last command land?". A saga whose participants aren't idempotent double-books on every recovery. Run: java SagaLab.java orchestrator-crash-recovery.

(c) A compensation fails. The hotel's cancel endpoint times out while the saga is aborting. The orchestrator cannot shrug — the saga is half-undone — so it retries the compensation until it succeeds (with backoff in real life, and a human-alert escape hatch after N attempts). This is why compensations must be retryable and idempotent, and must be designed to always eventually succeed (a cancel can't be "sold out"). Run: java SagaLab.java compensation-retry.

(d) A command or event is delivered twice. Broker redelivery, relay crash (outbox lab, scenario d) — the hotel receives book(trip-42) twice. Same answer as always: idempotency by saga id. Every arrow in the sequence diagram must tolerate duplication.

(e) The isolation anomaly — the one ACID letter sagas drop. While trip-42 is mid-flight, hotel capacity already reads 0. Another customer asks "any rooms?" and is told sold out. Then the car step fails, the saga aborts, the room comes back. That customer read state that was later undone — a business-level dirty read. Related anomaly, the lost update: a second transaction changes the same row mid-saga, and the compensation later stomps on it. Sagas do not prevent these; you must manage them with countermeasures:

  • Semantic lock — write status=PENDING_TRIP_42 instead of silently taking the room; other transactions can then decide to wait, warn, or treat pending as unavailable-but-explainable.
  • Commutative updates — design updates so order doesn't matter (reserve/release counters rather than absolute writes).
  • Reread value / version check — compensation verifies the row is still what it wrote before undoing it. Run: java SagaLab.java dirty-read.

(f) Choreography-specific failure: the invisible flow. Nothing "fails," yet nobody can say where trip-6b is stuck, because the flow exists only as subscriptions scattered across services — and a cancellation chain is easy to wire wrong (miss one event type and hotels leak). Run: java SagaLab.java choreography and read the event chains.

11. CORRECTNESS — what is and isn't guaranteed

Guaranteed (if you build it right):

  • Eventual atomicity-in-effect: every saga eventually ends in exactly one of two states — all steps done (COMPLETED) or all completed steps compensated (ABORTED). Nothing stays half-done forever.
  • Durability of progress: the saga log survives coordinator crashes; recovery resumes, never restarts from scratch.

Explicitly NOT guaranteed:

  • Isolation. The "I" of ACID is gone — mid-saga state is visible to everyone (scenario e). ACD, not ACID. This is the pattern's defining cost, and the reason the countermeasures vocabulary exists.
  • Instant atomicity. Between first commit and last compensation the system is inconsistent; the guarantee is only that it converges.
  • Rollback. Compensation is a business undo, not a time machine: the charge happened and was refunded (two ledger lines, maybe an email and a fee) — not erased. Some actions are un-compensatable (an email cannot be unsent; a mug already shipped can only be recalled) — those must be sequenced after the pivot, as retryable steps.

Traded away: simultaneous consistency and isolation — in exchange for no distributed locks, no blocking protocol, participation by anything that can process a request (including third-party APIs), and availability that doesn't multiply failure probabilities across services.

12. THE CODE

SagaLab.java, ~250 lines of mechanism:

  • Service — one per business; own state, book/cancel, both idempotent by booking id; knobs to force failures.
  • SagaLog — durable append-only record per saga id.
  • Orchestrator.run — the forward loop: skip logged steps, book, log, handle StepFailed by calling compensate (reverse order, retry ConnectionError forever). Crash injection sits in the book-succeeded-but-not-logged gap.
  • Bus + wireChoreography — the same saga as pure event reactions, compensation chain included.

What one orchestration step looks like in a production Java stack (illustrative, not part of the lab):

// inside the orchestrator, on receiving BookHotelReply.success
sagaLog.append(sagaId, HOTEL_DONE);                 // durable first
commandBus.send(new BookCar(sagaId, dates));        // then next command
// on BookCarReply.failure:
for (Step s : completedStepsInReverse(sagaId))
    commandBus.send(s.compensationCommand(sagaId)); // cancel hotel, flight

(Real frameworks — section 14 — generate this loop from a declarative definition; the mechanism underneath is exactly the lab's.)

14. PRODUCTION — what the lab omits and how real systems do it

Lab simplification Production reality
Commands are direct method calls Commands/replies and events go through a broker, and each participant publishes via a transactional outbox (your other lab) so "commit my booking + emit my event" is atomic.
Saga log is a list in memory that we keep alive A real table (or the workflow engine's event-sourced history). Writing the log entry and the orchestrator's own state change is — again — a local-transaction-plus-outbox problem.
Retry loop is while True Backoff + max attempts + dead-letter / human escalation: a compensation that fails for hours becomes a support ticket, not an infinite loop. Timeouts are first-class: "hotel hasn't replied in 30s" is itself a failure that triggers compensation.
One orchestrator instance Frameworks do the heavy lifting: Temporal (durable workflow code — your saga is literally a function whose every await is checkpointed), Camunda/Zeebe (BPMN-modeled), AWS Step Functions, Axon / Eventuate Tram (JVM saga orchestrators), MassTransit / NServiceBus (.NET). Rolling your own is reasonable at small scale — it's this lab plus persistence.
Steps in a fixed list Real definitions mark each step compensatable / pivot / retryable, and put un-compensatable effects (send email, ship goods) after the pivot.
Capacity counters visible to all Semantic locks (PENDING states), commutative reserve/release operations, version checks — the countermeasures of section 10(e), plus explicit UX for "provisional" (your airline seat "held for 20 minutes" is a semantic lock you have met in real life).
Nobody watches Observability is the operational pain: every saga gets a correlation id carried through every command/event; dashboards show sagas by state; alerts fire on sagas stuck in one state too long. Orchestration makes this dramatically easier than choreography — one place knows every saga's state.

Orchestration vs choreography, the honest rule of thumb: choreography is fine for 2–3 steps with trivial failure handling; the moment the flow has branches, timeouts, or someone asks "where is order 4711 stuck?", you want orchestration. Modern practice (and Temporal's existence) leans strongly orchestration for anything non-trivial — with the orchestrator kept to coordination only (no business logic), so it doesn't become a god-service.

16. RELATED CONCEPTS — and when to choose which

  • Two-phase commit (2PC/XA) — true atomicity + isolation, at the price of blocking, locks, and universal protocol support; dead across microservices and third-party APIs. Saga = availability over isolation; 2PC = the reverse. (Longer comparison in the outbox lab.)
  • Transactional outbox — the layer below: reliable delivery of one participant's events. Saga assumes it; nearly every real saga rides on it.
  • TCC (Try-Confirm-Cancel) — a cousin: every service exposes a reserve ("try": hold the room, don't book it), then the coordinator confirms or cancels all holds. Buys back some isolation (holds are invisible-ish) at the price of every participant implementing three operations and holds needing expiry. Think "application-level 2PC with timeouts".
  • Process manager / workflow engine — the orchestrator generalized: a state machine reacting to events with commands. A saga orchestrator is a process manager whose specialty is compensation; Temporal/Camunda are process managers you rent.
  • Event sourcing — orthogonal storage choice; pairs naturally (the saga log becomes just another event stream), but neither requires the other.

Rule of thumb: one business action, several services' databases, and "undo" is expressible in business terms ⇒ saga (orchestrated, on top of outboxes). If undo is not expressible and the steps can't be reordered behind a pivot — reconsider the service boundaries; that pain usually means one service should own the whole invariant.

Solutions

Tier 1 — Predict

P1. Sequential case: A completes (all capacities 1→0); B is refused at the flight step and aborts having booked nothing — correct and fair. Interleaved case, actual output: A pauses holding flight+hotel; B is refused at its first step (flight: sold out) and ABORTS; A resumes and COMPLETES (car capacity was untouched by B). B lost — here to a trip that did ultimately happen, so no harm was done this time. The anomaly is that B's rejection was based on provisional, in-flight state: had A's car step failed instead, A would have aborted, capacity would have returned, and B was turned away for a room that was never really sold — the business-level dirty read of section 10(e). Whether the provisional read "turns out fine" is decided by A's future, which B cannot see; the countermeasure (semantic lock / PENDING status) doesn't prevent the read, it makes the provisionality explicit so the business can choose a policy (waitlist B, hold B for 10 minutes, oversell by one).

P2. Recovery reads the log: it contains SAGA_STARTED only (the crash hit before flight:done was written). So the new orchestrator re-calls flight — which answers "already-booked (idempotent)" because the crash in P2 happened after the service committed — then proceeds to call hotel and car normally (first time each). In experiment 3 the crash was after hotel's book: flight WAS logged (skipped without any call), hotel was re-called and deduped, car ran fresh. The general rule: the step in the "acted but not yet logged" gap is exactly the one that gets a duplicate call; everything before is skipped by the log, everything after runs normally.

P3. Expected bus history: TripRequested → FlightBooked → HotelFailed → FlightCancelled — one compensation event (FlightCancelled, undoing the flight); the car service is never touched (its trigger, HotelBooked, never fired). Compensation count scales with how deep the saga got, not with saga length.

P4. The pivot must sit before shipping — at the card charge: order = reserve (compensatable) → charge (pivot) → ship (retryable) → email (retryable). "Charge fails after the parcel shipped" must be made impossible by ordering, because shipping can't be undone and a failed charge can't be tolerated after it. If the business insists on ship-before-charge, then the charge must be made retryable — i.e. it is not allowed to fail permanently, which means pre-authorizing the card (a hold) before shipping… which is a reserve step, which is the same answer wearing a different hat: un-compensatable and can-fail steps must be separated by a pivot, and "can-fail" is converted to "can't-fail" by reserving earlier.

Tier 2 — Modify

M1. test_compensations_run_in_reverse_order fails ([flight, hotel] instead of [hotel, flight]). Concrete case where forward order is wrong, not just untested: step 1 creates a customer record, step 2 creates an order referencing that customer. Forward-order compensation deletes the customer first — and the order-cancellation then runs against a dangling reference (or a foreign-key error, and now the compensation itself fails). Undo must unwind dependencies in the opposite order they were built — same reason destructors and defer stacks run LIFO.

M2. test_recovery_retry_does_not_double_book_thanks_to_idempotency fails: hotel capacity ends at 1 (two seats taken for one trip; expected 2). The broken pair: logging after acting means a crash in the gap makes the recovery re-act; that's only safe if re-acting is a no-op — i.e. the participant is idempotent. The two candidate fixes: (1) make participants idempotent by saga id — generally available, the standard answer; (2) make "act + log" atomic — only possible when the orchestrator and participant share one database (a modular monolith), which across services they don't. (Logging before acting just moves the hole — see D1.)

M3. The peeking customer now sees capacity 0 but with the room in pending, not bookings — the system can now say "1 room held provisionally, may free up" instead of lying "sold out"; and after the abort, the pending entry vanishes without a CANCELLED tombstone. The new operational question: a hold with no confirm is a leak — the orchestrator might die forever between last step and the confirm sweep, so every hold needs an expiry (and expiry of a hold whose saga actually completed but whose confirm was lost = a booked customer losing their room — so confirm must beat expiry, with margin, and expiry must check the saga's fate first). You have re-invented TCC (Try-Confirm-Cancel): book→pending is Try, confirm is Confirm, compensation is Cancel, and the expiry is TCC's timeout. Section 16's line "application-level 2PC with timeouts" should now feel earned.

M4. Choreography: you must add/change at least four wirings — subscribe insurance to CarBooked; give insurance its own InsuranceBooked/InsuranceFailed events; subscribe a handler for InsuranceFailed that cancels the car and publishes CarCancelled; and subscribe CarCancelled to the existing hotel-cancel handler (extending the backward chain by one link). Miss the last one and a failed insurance strands a booked hotel+flight — silently. Orchestration: append "insurance" to STEPS, register the service — the compensation chain comes free from completed.reversed(). Lesson: choreography's complexity grows in the edges (every step touches its neighbors' subscriptions, failure paths are hand-wired); orchestration's grows in one node.

Tier 3 — Debug

D1. test_orchestrator_crash_then_recovery_completes_saga — with intent-logging, the crash-after-book run has already written hotel:done… but so has a crash before book, and that's the killer: log says done, service never acted. Concretely: crash between log.append(sagaId, "hotel:done") and hotel.book() → recovery skips the hotel → saga "COMPLETES" with no hotel room — the customer lands with nowhere to sleep (losing a booking and the customer; arguably worse than double-charging, which idempotency can prevent and support can refund). The general law, same as outbox M2: with two non-atomic acts, the one you do FIRST is the one that can end up done-without-the-other. Record-then-act risks phantom completions (at-most-once); act-then-record risks duplicate actions (at-least-once) — and you choose act-then-record because duplicates are absorbable by idempotency, while phantoms are unrecoverable silence.

D2. Timeline: car.book command sent; car service is slow (GC pause, lock wait); orchestrator times out at 30 s, treats it as StepFailed, compensates hotel and flight, logs SAGA_ABORTED; the car service then finishes processing the still-queued command and books — after the saga closed. A timeout is not a failure — it is unknown outcome. The two missing properties: (1) compensate the timed-out step too — the abort path must send car.cancel(trip) even though car never confirmed booking, and idempotent-cancel ("nothing to cancel" is a fine no-op) makes that safe whether the book landed or not — the lab's Service.cancel no-op branch is exactly this; (2) at the delivery layer, commands need to be cancellable/fenced (the cancel must not be overtaken by the stale book — ordering per saga id, e.g. same partition key, per the outbox lab's ordering section). Cancel-the-unknown + ordered delivery closes the hole.

D3. test_failing_compensation_is_retried_until_it_succeeds fails — the hotel booking stays BOOKED, capacity stays 2, while the log claims hotel:compensated: the log now lies, which is worse than being behind, because recovery trusts it. For the nightly job to be correct it would need: a durable record that the compensation is pending (not "done" — so a new log state, compensation-pending), idempotent cancels (have), detection of which cancels actually landed (reconciliation against the service's state), retry with escalation, and ordering guarantees against in-flight duplicates. At that point the "nightly job" has become… a message relay draining a durable to-do table — i.e. the transactional outbox's relay, rediscovered. The correct design was never "log compensated and hope"; it is "log compensation-requested durably, let a reliable deliverer own the retries" — which is what real orchestrators do by emitting compensation commands through their outbox.
