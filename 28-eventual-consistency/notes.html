Lab 28 · Why Distribution Is Hard

Eventual Consistency — the weakest promise that still means something

You have spent ten labs buying stronger guarantees: overlap ([quorums-and-replication]), one agreed order ([raft]), real-time recency ([linearizability]), cause-before-effect ([causal-consistency]), convergence-by-construction ([crdts]). This lab is about the thing all of those were upgrades from — the two words at the bottom of every vendor page: eventually consistent.

The surprise of the lab: those words are not a weak version of "consistent". They are a different kind of statement altogether — a promise about a future limit that says nothing checkable about any present moment — and knowing exactly where its edges are is what separates "we use Cassandra" from "we know what Cassandra promised us".

Depth layers: BEGINNER (stages 1–9) → INTERMEDIATE (stage 10) → ENGINEERING (stages 11–12) → PRODUCTION (stage 13). Checkpoints mark each transition.

1. WHY — two words that decide real designs

Somewhere in your system's documentation right now sits the sentence "reads are eventually consistent." On the strength of those two words you must decide: can the password-change screen read from this? The shopping cart? The account balance? If nobody pins down what the words actually promise, engineers fail in both directions: they over-trust it (build a read-modify-write on it and silently lose customer data — no error, no log line), or they under-trust it (route every read to the leader "to be safe" and pay double latency and half availability for reads that never needed it). Both failures are common, both are expensive, and both come from treating "eventually consistent" as a vibe instead of a contract.

2. PROBLEM — the two noticeboards of Deodar

The village of Deodar has two public noticeboards: one in the north square, one in the south square. Villagers post notices on whichever board is nearest, and read whichever board is nearest. Every evening, a volunteer named Kavi walks between the squares and copies notices from each board onto the other.

At 9:00 on Tuesday, Tara pins a notice to the north board: "Market moved to Sunday." At 9:05, Ben reads the south board. It still says "Market on Saturday" — Kavi hasn't walked yet. Ben plans his week around a fact that is five minutes out of date.

Ben complains to the parish council. The council decides to print a promise at the top of each board, so readers know exactly how much to trust what they see. The whole lab is the council trying to write that sentence honestly.

3. NAIVE SOLUTION — promise the boards always match

The council's first draft: "Both boards always show the same notices."

To keep that promise, a notice can't appear on one board until it is on both. So Tara, pinning her notice in the north square, must wait while a runner carries it south and returns with confirmation — and if the road between the squares is flooded, nobody can post anything anywhere until it drains. You have seen this exact trade before, measured: this is the coordinated corner of [cap-theorem] — the [causal-consistency] lab measured it: a linearizable store answered 0 requests on the minority side of a partition while the available design answered 600/600. The council refuses: the boards must stay useful when the road is flooded. Posting and reading must always work, locally, immediately.

So the council swings to the weakest wording it can think of:

"The two boards will eventually show the same notices."

It sounds humble and honest. Now watch it fall apart.

4. FAILURE — the humble promise forbids nothing

Check the draft against four situations. Every one of them satisfies the promise as written, and a reader would call every one of them broken:

  • The lazy week. Kavi skips his rounds for eight days. The south board shows nothing new all week. Has the promise been broken? No — "eventually" names no deadline. It is compatible with an hour, a week, a decade.
  • Time runs backwards. Ben reads the north board at 9:10 and sees Tara's new notice. He walks south, reads at 9:20, and sees the old state of the world. The promise says nothing about one reader's experience across boards. Perfectly compatible.
  • The contradiction. At 9:00 Tara posts "market → Sunday" on the north board; at 9:02 the market master posts "market cancelled" on the south board. The boards will "eventually show the same notices" — which one? The promise doesn't say. If both boards eventually settle on Tara's out-of-date notice and the cancellation quietly vanishes, the promise as written is satisfied.

And the fourth situation is the sneakiest, so look at it slowly:

  • The unbreakable promise. Suppose the council secretly never intends to sync the boards again. On Wednesday a villager accuses them: "you broke your promise!" The council answers: "not yet — wait longer." On Thursday: "wait longer." There is no moment at which the accusation can ever be proven, because at every finite moment the promised event is still allowed to be in the future.

🔮 Predict: Three stores each claim "eventual consistency." Store A returned a 3-year-old value for a key written yesterday. Store B showed reader X the new value, then showed reader Y (who asked later) the old value. Store C's two replicas have both been idle and reachable for a month and still disagree. Which stores have provably broken the claim?

Reveal Only **C** — and only because it has been *quiescent* (no new writes) with working communication for a long time and still disagrees; convergence-after-updates-stop is the one thing the claim contains, and C's month of idle disagreement is (informally) the witness. A's staleness is legal: no deadline was ever promised. B's time-travel is legal: nothing about the order of different readers' views was promised — that would be [linearizability]'s real-time rule, a different and much more expensive contract. Strictly speaking even C is only "violated in the limit" — a lawyer can still say "wait longer" — which is exactly the unfalsifiability problem stage 6 names.

The deeper problem the council has discovered: their promise talks only about a future limit, and never about any present moment. A reader standing in front of a board now gets nothing from it. Any promise with teeth must either constrain the present (expensive, as stage 3 showed) — or be honest that it doesn't, and say precisely what it does constrain.

5. THE REAL REQUIREMENT — what the sentence must contain to be non-vacuous

For "the boards will eventually agree" to mean anything at all, two clauses have to be true underneath it, and the council should write both down:

  1. Delivery clause — every notice, once posted, keeps being carried toward every board until it arrives, no matter what. Kavi's rounds must never permanently stop; a lost copy must be re-carried. Not "we tried once".
  2. Decision clause — two boards that have received the same set of notices must display the same thing. What a board shows must be a function of the set it has received, never of the order or number of times deliveries happened to arrive.

Clause 1 makes "eventually" arrive; clause 2 makes "consistent" mean one agreed answer when it does. Everything else a reader might want — a deadline, no-time-travel for one reader, cause before effect, a say in which conflicting notice wins — is a separate, stronger promise with its own separate price, and you have already bought each of them in a previous lab. This lab is what you get when you buy none of them.

6. KEY IDEA — a promise about the limit, not the path

Eventual consistency: if updates to an item stop, then eventually all replicas converge to the same value for it — and until then, a read may return anything a past state could justify.

One sentence, three consequences, and the three consequences are the rest of this lab:

  1. It is a liveness property, so no finite observation can ever falsify it. Every "violation" you catch can be answered with "wait longer". You can test a safety property by exhibiting one bad state; eventual consistency has no bad state to exhibit.
  2. It is silent about what value wins. "Converge" names an agreement, not an answer. Some resolver — a rule choosing among concurrent writes — must be supplied, and the resolver, not the promise, decides whether your data survives.
  3. Everything that makes it usable in practice is un-promised. Real systems are usually fresh within milliseconds — but that number lives in measurements and dashboards, never in the contract. Engineering an eventually-consistent system means engineering its un-promised behavior.

7. TERMINOLOGY — the professional names

Now that you have felt each idea, its industry name. Load-bearing terms get the full treatment; the rest get a line.

Eventual consistency (EC). Plain: "if you stop changing it and wait, everyone will agree on it." Technical: in an execution where updates to an object cease, there is a point after which all reads of that object at all replicas return the same value (Werner Vogels' formulation, from the Dynamo lineage). The term exists to name the weakest replication contract worth advertising — the one that promises convergence and nothing else. Example: Tara's notice, five minutes old on the north board only, with Kavi's evening round guaranteeing the south board catches up. Connects to: this is the floor under everything on the [linearizability] / [causal-consistency] ladder.

Safety vs liveness (applied to consistency contracts). Plain: "nothing bad ever happens" vs "something good eventually happens." Technical (Alpern & Schneider): a safety property is one whose every violation has a finite witness — a prefix of the execution after which no continuation can repair it; a liveness property is one no finite prefix can ever violate. You met the pair in [raft] as two things a protocol can lose independently; here it classifies promises: linearizability is safety (one bad read is a witness — that's how the linearizability lab's checker could return verdicts), eventual consistency is (almost) pure liveness — which is why no checker for it can exist in the same sense, and why the visualization's EC judge has no red light at finite time. The term pair exists because these two kinds of promise are verified, monitored, and broken in categorically different ways. Example: the council's "wait longer" defense is the definition of liveness in one line.

Convergence. Plain: ending up equal. Technical here: the decision clause — replica state is a deterministic function of the set of updates received. You saw convergence engineered to perfection in [crdts]; this lab names the minimum version every EC system needs, CRDT or not.

Quiescence. Plain: the writes have stopped. Technical: a period with no new updates, which is the only condition under which classic EC promises anything observable. "Quiescent consistency" is the academic name for exactly this guarantee-shape. The term exists because the promise is conditional and the condition needs a name — production systems are never quiescent, which is the sharpest single criticism of the contract.

Staleness, t-visibility, k-staleness. Plain: how old the value you read is. Technical: a read is t-stale if it returns a value overwritten more than t ago; t-visibility is the probability that a read issued t after a write's acknowledgment returns it; k-staleness counts versions behind instead of time behind. These terms exist because "eventually" is unbounded in the contract but tightly distributed in reality, and you need words for the reality. Example: "at t = 100 ms after ack, 99.9% of reads return the new value" is a t-visibility statement.

PBS — Probabilistically Bounded Staleness (Bailis et al., 2012). Plain: measuring "eventually" and reporting it as a distribution. Technical: a model + measurement method predicting t-visibility for Dynamo-style quorum stores from their replication delays. The paper's headline (paper-reported, not re-measured here): for production-like LinkedIn/ Yammer latency traces with N=3, R=W=1, reads were fresh with 99.9% probability within tens of milliseconds of a write — i.e. "eventual" in the contract, "milliseconds" in the measurement. The term exists because it turned an argument ("EC is fine!" / "EC is reckless!") into a number you can put an SLO on.

Consistency window. You've used this term since [cqrs]; it now has edges: the interval between a write's acknowledgment and the moment every replica would serve it — the window inside which all of stage 4's legal weirdness lives. Its width is a measured, moving quantity (PBS measures exactly this), never a promised one.

BASE. Basically Available, Soft state, Eventually consistent — the 1997 acronym (Fox et al.) coined as the deliberate opposite of ACID. One line each: basically available = requests get answers even during partitions (the 600/600 side of [cap-theorem]); soft state = a replica's local state may be revised later without any client asking (your value can change under you as convergence runs); eventually consistent = this lab. Minor term, big marketing career.

Resolver / conflict-resolution policy. The rule that makes the decision clause deterministic when writes were concurrent. Not new machinery to you — LWW, version-vector siblings, CRDT joins, all measured in [quorums-and-replication] and [crdts] — but this lab names their shared job: the resolver is the part of an EC system that decides what "the same value" ends up being, and therefore which writes die.

Anti-entropy, read repair, hinted handoff — known from [gossip-protocols] and [quorums-and-replication]; here they get their collective job title: the delivery clause's machinery. One-line refreshers where used.

8. MENTAL MODELS

Simple model (carry this one): two noticeboards and an evening courier. The promise printed on each board reads: "What you see here was true at some point. If everyone stops posting, the boards will match after the courier's next full round. We do not say when notices arrive, which of two same-day notices survives, or what you'll see if you walk from one square to the other." Every honest EC vendor page is a paraphrase of that paragraph.

Engineering model (reason with this one): each replica i holds a set Sᵢ of received updates and displays resolve(Sᵢ) for a deterministic function resolve. A background process forever moves updates between replicas so that every update eventually lands in every set. Eventual consistency = (delivery: ∀ update u accepted at any replica, ∀ replica i, eventually u ∈ Sᵢ) ∧ (decision: Sᵢ = Sⱼ ⇒ resolve(Sᵢ) = resolve(Sⱼ)). Reads return resolve(Sᵢ) of whichever replica you happened to hit — no clause relates that to any other read, any wall clock, or any cause.

Story Engineering model
A noticeboard A replica's set Sᵢ
Pinning a notice Accepting an update locally, ack immediately
Kavi's evening round An anti-entropy cycle
What the board displays resolve(Sᵢ)
Two same-day contradictory notices Concurrent updates — resolver decides
"The boards will eventually match" Delivery clause + decision clause
Reading whichever board is nearest No routing/session guarantee

Where the analogy breaks — three places, each hiding a real hazard:

  1. Kavi is scheduled and visible; anti-entropy is neither. If Kavi skips a week, the village notices a missing volunteer. If a repair daemon silently dies or falls behind, nothing in the contract — and often nothing in the system — makes a sound. The delivery clause is the half of EC that operationally rots (stage 11, F5).
  2. Noticeboards accumulate; registers overwrite. Deodar's boards can show both contradictory notices side by side. A key-value register must show one value, so convergence includes choosing a loser — the analogy has no place where data silently dies, but LWW does (stage 11, F3).
  3. A villager stands in one square; a client hops replicas mid-session. The story makes "one reader, one board" feel natural, which quietly imports a session guarantee the contract does not contain. Real load balancers send your next read anywhere — [causal-consistency] measured a roaming client missing its own write 334/500 times over a correctly-working store.

10. ACTUAL MECHANISM — building the two clauses

The engineering model needs two machines: one to make delivery true (updates reach every replica), one to make the decision deterministic (same set ⇒ same value). Build the obvious version of each, watch it break, and let the break force the real design.

10.1 Delivery v1: forward each write to the peers, once

A replica accepts a write, acks the client, and sends one copy to each peer. Three network facts you have already measured each kill it in a different way:

  • Loss. The forwarded copy dies; nobody retries; that update never arrives — the delivery clause is broken silently and permanently. [gossip-protocols] measured the shape: a rumor-mongering stop rule left 9 of 1,000 nodes never informed and never able to recover. One lost packet ≠ a late update; it's a forever hole unless something keeps re-offering old updates.
  • Duplication. Retries (the fix for loss) deliver copies twice. If applying an update twice differs from applying it once, replicas drift: [crdts] measured a sum-instead-of-max merge reading 11 where the truth was 2 after ten deliveries.
  • Reorder. Two writes take different routes and arrive at different replicas in opposite orders — routine, not exotic: [causal-consistency] measured apply-on-arrival designs anomalous in 160/300 runs with a single slow route.

🔮 Predict: Replica R1 receives write a then b; replica R2 receives b then a. Both use the rule "newest arrival wins." Both have now received the same set {a, b}. Kavi runs anti-entropy forever between them, always re-offering both updates. Do they converge?

Reveal **No — never.** R1 serves *b* (it arrived last there), R2 serves *a*. Every anti-entropy round re-offers updates the other side has already received, so sets never change, so nothing ever changes. Same set, different value: the **decision clause** is broken, and unlike a liveness miss this one has a finite witness — it is the one way an EC store is *provably* broken (viz scenario 4). "Newest arrival" made the display a function of the *delivery history* instead of the *set*.

10.2 The fixes the failures force

Loss forces repetition (keep offering until acknowledged-as-present); repetition forces idempotence (receiving what you already have must change nothing); reorder forces order-independence (the outcome must not depend on arrival sequence). You met this exact triple as the [crdts] merge laws and the [gossip-protocols] join rule — here is why they are not a CRDT luxury but the minimum for any eventually-consistent store: they are the decision clause restated as algebra.

Delivery v2 — anti-entropy: on a cadence, replicas pair up, compare what they hold (a Merkle digest, so comparing is cheap — [quorums-and-replication]), and transfer the difference; receipt goes through a merge that is idempotent and order-free. Because every round re-compares everything held, no single lost message can permanently orphan an update — loss only delays. Riders on top, both known, now placed: read repair (when a coordinator reads several replicas and sees one behind, fix it in passing — opportunistic, only touches what is read: measured fixing 100 of 200 stale keys) and hinted handoff (a stand-in holds writes for a dead replica and forwards later — best-effort, the hint holder can die with the only copy).

10.3 The decision machine: three resolvers, three prices

Concurrent writes are the case the set alone can't settle: {a, b} with neither a nor b "later" by any causal measure ([causal-consistency]'s ∥). Someone must pick. All three industrial answers are already in your vocabulary; the new frame is what each does to the decision clause:

Resolver How it decides Price, measured previously
Last-write-wins Total order from (timestamp, replica-id); biggest wins Concurrent writes die silently: 1000/1000 discarded ([quorums-and-replication]); a clock 60 s fast destroyed 1000/1000 genuinely-newer writes ([crdts]) — your clock IS the resolver
Version vectors + siblings Partial order; dominated versions dropped, concurrent ones all kept; the app merges at read Nothing dies, but every reader inherits a merge decision; sibling counts must be watched (Riak)
CRDT join The data type's lattice join is the resolver Convergence and no lost updates by construction (= SEC), paid in metadata: 50 records per visible cart item ([crdts])

The one-sentence summary worth keeping: the EC contract promises an agreement; the resolver decides who loses; picking a resolver is picking which of your writes are allowed to die.

10.4 A worked trace: LWW converging onto a lie

Concrete values, one step per line; derive each from the rules above. Three replicas, one key market, LWW resolver, R2's wall clock +60 s fast. (Same setup as viz scenario 3.)

t=0    all replicas: market = "Saturday" @ ts=1000
t=10   Ben  → R2:  write "cancelled"      R2 stamps ts = 10 + 60 = 1070   ack
t=25   Tara → R1:  write "moved to Sunday" R1 stamps ts = 25               ack
       (Tara wrote 15 s AFTER Ben; real-world-later; ts smaller: 25 < 1070)
t=30   anti-entropy R1↔R2: sets now equal {Sat@1000, cancel@1070, Sunday@25}
       resolve = max ts → both serve "cancelled"
t=40   anti-entropy R2↔R3: R3 catches up → serves "cancelled"
t=∞    quiescent, all replicas identical: "cancelled".  Tara's write is gone.

Every clause of the contract is satisfied — delivery worked, decision was deterministic, convergence happened, fast. The system will pass every eventual-consistency check forever. Converged and wrong are compatible, and no clause you bought says otherwise. (What would have saved Tara's write: a resolver that refuses to lose concurrent writes — siblings or a CRDT — or a store with a stronger contract that orders the writes by something real.)

10.5 When is "eventually"? Measuring the window

The contract refuses a number, but the machinery implies a distribution, and you already know its two terms: propagation cadence and pairing math. From [gossip-protocols]: push-pull anti-entropy at fanout 3 converges 500 nodes in ~8.3 rounds, and even 80% packet loss only stretches it 4.3× — delays, never breaks. So staleness ≈ (rounds needed × cadence) + luck, and that — not the contract — is what you measure: issue a write, read it back from each replica, record the delay, plot the distribution (t-visibility). This is PBS as an operational practice, and it is the answer to the stage-1 dilemma: you don't decide "is EC okay for the cart page" from the contract (which says nothing); you decide it from the measured window (usually milliseconds) plus the cost of the window's tail (stage 11).

✅ CHECKPOINT — intermediate model complete. You can now build the promise: an always-running re-offering sync + an idempotent, order-free, deterministic resolver — and you know the window is a measured distribution, not a clause. Next: the ways the machine and its contract fail people.

11. FAILURE SCENARIOS — seven ways to get hurt, only one of which is a breach

For each: how to create it → where to watch it → what happened and why → what the system does → which guarantee held / which was lost → how it shows up in production. Notice the running theme: F1–F3 and F5–F7 break users while satisfying the contract; only F4 breaks the contract itself.

F1 — The stale read after ack. Create: write to R1, immediately read from R3 before any sync (viz: any write, then read another replica; scenario 2). What happened: the ack meant "accepted at one replica", never "visible everywhere" — the consistency window is open. System response: none needed; converges on the next sync. Held: EC. Lost: nothing that was promised — only what the reader assumed. Production face: the #1 ticket pattern — "I updated my profile and the app still shows the old photo"; handled with session routing (below) or by measuring the window and confirming it's small.

F2 — Time runs backwards for one reader. Create: random routing; read R1 (fresh), then R3 (behind) — viz scenario 2. Why: no clause links two reads, even by the same client. Held: EC. Lost: monotonic reads — never promised, but humans assume it so deeply that its absence reads as data loss ("my edit vanished!… no wait, it's back"). The fix is routing, not storage — sticky sessions, or a session token carried by the client: [causal-consistency] measured the token taking a roaming client's anomalies from 3759 / 1611 / 356 / 726 to 0 / 0 / 0 / 0 (its four session guarantees). Production face: intermittent "flickering" bug reports that no server-side log will ever reproduce; the on-call move is to ask which guarantee the flow assumed (RYW? monotonic?) and add that guarantee, not to abandon EC.

F3 — Converged onto a lie (LWW + skew). Create: stage 10.4's trace; viz scenario 3 (skew slider +60 on one replica, two writes, settle). Why: LWW's total order is manufactured from clocks, and clocks answer a question ("what time is it?") that is not the question ("which write should win?"). System response: converges promptly, looks perfect. Held: EC, delivery, decision — all of it. Lost: a write, silently — and no monitor watching consistency will ever fire; only a monitor watching clock skew can warn you. Production face: Cassandra's default is exactly this (LWW per cell, microsecond timestamps, writer's clock); the operational rule it forces: NTP monitoring with eviction is a data-integrity control there, not a nicety ([hybrid-logical-clocks] measured what dishonest clocks do to every downstream timestamp).

F4 — Same set, different answer: the one true breach. Create: arrival-order resolver

  • opposite delivery orders (stage 10.1's prediction; viz scenario 4 — resolver switch to "arrival order"). Why: the displayed value depends on delivery history, so no amount of re-syncing changes anything: permanent divergence with a finite witness — the only failure in this list a checker could ever prove. Held: nothing worth the name. Lost: the decision clause = EC itself. Production face: hand-rolled "sync" code that applies whatever arrives ("it'll sort itself out") — the classic home-grown-replication bug; also any resolver that consults local non-replicated state. The audit question for any sync design you review: "is what a replica serves a pure function of the set of updates it has received?" If no, it is not eventually consistent, whatever the README says.

F5 — "Eventually" never comes: the rotting delivery clause. Create: viz — turn sync cadence to "off" mid-run; nothing turns red (the EC judge just says "not yet…" — that's the trap). Why: the delivery clause is a forever obligation with no finite deadline, so its death produces no event, no error, no failed request — reads keep answering, writes keep acking, entropy silently accumulates. Held: technically unfalsifiable-not-yet. Lost: the promise's entire practical value. Production face: the most under-monitored failure in AP systems — a dead repair daemon, a backlogged replication queue growing without bound (the [quorums-and-replication] lag-is-a-queue result: apply rate < write rate ⇒ unbounded). The alarms that catch it are liveness alarms: replication-lag age, time-since-last-successful-repair, oldest-undelivered-update age — you must alarm on the machinery's heartbeat, because the contract offers nothing to alarm on.

F6 — The dead rise: deletion meets delayed delivery. Create (hand trace, and [quorums-and-replication]/[crdts] both prepared this): delete a key → delete is stored as a tombstone (a "this died" record that must itself replicate) → tombstone is garbage-collected after its grace window → a replica that was unreachable longer than the window comes back carrying the pre-delete value → anti-entropy sees a value the others lack and dutifully re-spreads it: the deleted data resurrects everywhere. Why: GC destroyed the only evidence that the value was outdated, and the delivery machinery cannot distinguish "old data you deleted" from "new data you're missing". Held: EC (it converges — onto the resurrected value!). Lost: the delete. Production face: Cassandra's gc_grace_seconds (default 10 days) and its hard operational law — every node must complete repair within the grace window, or don't re-admit it; same reason [crdts] rule says never restore a CRDT replica from backup into a live cluster.

F7 — Read-modify-write on an EC read. Create: read balance from a stale replica, compute, write back (pencil exercise 5.2). Why: the read carried no promise of currency, and the write-back overwrites newer state the reader never saw — this is the [linearizability] lab's 87%-lost increment with the extra twist that distribution makes the window bigger. Held: EC. Lost: updates, at whatever rate you have concurrency. Production face: any "fetch, edit, save" flow against an eventually-consistent read path; the fixes are the whole [optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking] lab (conditional writes, versions) or routing the read strongly — EC reads are for display, never for decisions, unless the write path is conditional.

🔮 Predict: Your monitoring shows zero divergent replicas for 30 straight days. Which of these can you conclude? (a) the store is eventually consistent, (b) the anti-entropy machinery is working, (c) the consistency window is small, (d) none of these.

Reveal **(d)** — and the reason is the falsifying-query rule wearing a distributed-systems costume. "Zero divergence observed" is compatible with: divergence you didn't sample (the check reads a quorum that happens to overlap), a window your probe never lands in (the [linearizability] lab measured a broken design showing **0 violations in 600 calm runs**), and — worst — a dead repair daemon with divergence accumulating in keys nobody reads (F5: a pass on an unexercised dataset). To conclude (b) you monitor the machinery itself; to conclude (c) you measure t-visibility with injected probes *under injected network stress* — the "calm network proves nothing" lesson, now on its fourth lab.

12. CORRECTNESS — what you hold, what you traded, where it sits

Guaranteed (by EC + a lawful resolver): convergence at quiescence; availability of every read and write during partitions (the [causal-consistency] 600/600 side of CAP); no coordination on any request path; and — if and only if the resolver is a pure function of the set — determinism of the limit.

Explicitly NOT guaranteed, each refused by name: any bound on staleness (no deadline clause); recency (no real-time clause — [linearizability]'s whole right column); any two reads agreeing, even the same client's (no session clauses); cause before effect (no causal clause — a comment can surface before its post, [causal-consistency]'s opening wound); survival of concurrent writes (resolver's choice, F3); any cross-replica invariant — the [crdts] bank measured it: two partitioned warehouses each honoring "stock ≥ 0" locally merged to −2; an invariant needs a referee who can say no, and EC's definition is the absence of that referee ([raft] is its price).

The trade, stated once: you sold every safety clause about the path to buy liveness plus availability plus latency. Whether that sale was smart is a per-flow question — display flows usually yes, decision flows usually no (F7).

The formal seam of the lab: classic EC is a liveness property — unfalsifiable, untestable, un-alarmable directly (F5). Strong eventual consistency ([crdts]) is the safety upgrade of the same idea: "same set ⇒ same state, immediately" has a finite witness (exhibit two replicas, equal sets, different states), which is exactly why the crdts lab could fuzz its laws on 50,000 generated states while this lab's central property cannot be tested at all — only its companions (session guarantees, bounds, machinery heartbeats) can. When someone says "we tested that our system is eventually consistent", the true translations are: they tested convergence under quiescence in finite scenarios, or they tested SEC. The distinction is worth a design-review question.

13. PRODUCTION — you have been running this for years

The confession first: the most-deployed eventually-consistent system in your world is an ordinary SQL read replica. Postgres/MySQL async replication + a read-routing layer (@Transactional(readOnly = true) steering to the replica) is exactly this lab: ack at the primary (delivery clause = the WAL shipper — with resolver trivial, since a single primary orders all writes, replicas replay one log: no concurrent-write problem, only staleness), consistency window = replication lag, F1/F2/F7 all live and biting, and the lag gauge is the delivery-clause heartbeat. Every trap in stage 11 has a Java-shop costume: the saved-form-then-list-shows-old-row bug is F1; "sometimes the row flickers back" is F2 (a pool with two replicas); reading the replica inside a payment decision is F7. [isolation-levels]' warning gets its third repetition: a stale replica produces symptoms identical to read skew and no isolation level touches it.

The Dynamo lineage (Cassandra, Riak, Voldemort — the systems the term was coined for): everything in this doc composed — sloppy quorums + hinted handoff (delivery), read repair + scheduled anti-entropy with Merkle trees (delivery), LWW or vector-clock siblings (decision; Cassandra chose LWW and inherited F3, Riak chose siblings and later CRDTs), tombstones + gc_grace_seconds (F6), tunable R/W/N per request ([quorums-and-replication]'s dial — "eventual" is the R+W≤N region of that lab's table). Operational reality Cassandra makes explicit: anti-entropy is an operator responsibility — nodetool repair must complete within the grace window on every node; "we run Cassandra" means "we run a repair schedule", and the F5 alarm (time-since-last-repair) is a first-class pager.

DynamoDB prices this lab: an eventually-consistent read costs half a strongly consistent one — the CAP trade as a line item. DNS is the planet-scale specimen: TTL-bounded staleness, no session anything, converges by expiry — and everyone accepts it because the data (name→address) tolerates staleness, which is the real lesson of choosing EC: it's a property you match to data, not a belief. S3 is the cautionary tale in reverse: eventually-consistent overwrite-reads from 2006, then in December 2020 made strongly consistent — after 14 years of every S3-backed data lake carrying list-after-write workarounds. The vendor moved the contract because customers kept building F1/F7 bugs; when the platform can afford the stronger clause, un-promised behavior that users depend on eventually gets promised (Hyrum's law, priced).

What the on-call engineer actually watches (the whole stage-11 list, as a runbook): replication/apply lag age per replica and repair backlog age (F5 — the liveness heartbeats; alarm on age, not on rate); clock skew with eviction if LWW anywhere (F3); sibling counts if vectors (decision-machine backpressure); t-visibility probes under injected latency for the window SLO (F1, and the 11.7 falsifying-query lesson); tombstone/GC schedule compliance (F6). And the two questions for any incident in an EC system: which un-promised guarantee did this flow silently assume? and is the delivery machinery alive?

What the noticeboard model omitted (each a real subsystem): Merkle-tree digests (comparing sets cheaply at scale); hinted handoff's failure modes; membership — who the replicas even are, gossiped, [gossip-protocols]; per-key sharding of the whole story, [consistent-hashing]; backpressure when anti-entropy competes with foreground traffic; security of the sync channel; and cross-key anything ([distributed-transactions] is the other end of that road).

Commonly confused, one sentence each: EC vs SEC — SEC removes "eventually" from the decision half (same set ⇒ same state immediately), turning liveness into testable safety. 16. GLOSSARY

In order of appearance; terms carried from prior labs marked ↩.

  • eventual consistency (EC) — if updates stop, all replicas eventually serve the same value; nothing promised before that point.
  • consistency window ↩([cqrs], now with edges) — the interval between a write's ack and its visibility everywhere; measured, never promised.
  • safety property ↩([raft], sharpened) — "nothing bad ever happens"; every violation has a finite witness, so it is testable.
  • liveness property ↩ — "something good eventually happens"; no finite observation can violate it, so it is untestable and must be monitored via its machinery.
  • quiescence — a period with no new updates; the only condition under which classic EC promises anything observable.
  • quiescent consistency — the academic name for "converged whenever quiescent".
  • convergence / decision clause — replica output is a deterministic function of the set of updates received.
  • delivery clause — every update keeps being propagated until present at every replica; a forever-obligation.
  • staleness — how far behind an acknowledged write a served value is.
  • t-visibility — probability a read issued t after a write's ack returns it.
  • k-staleness — staleness counted in versions rather than time.
  • PBS (probabilistically bounded staleness) — modeling/measuring t-visibility as a distribution; "eventual" turned into an SLO-able number.
  • BASE — Basically Available, Soft state, Eventually consistent; the anti-ACID acronym.
  • soft state — replica state that may be revised by convergence without any client writing.
  • resolver / conflict-resolution policy — the deterministic rule choosing among concurrent updates; decides which writes die.
  • arrival-order resolver — the broken resolver (output depends on delivery history); produces provable divergence (F4).
  • last-write-wins (LWW) ↩ — resolver by (timestamp, id); silently drops concurrent and clock-disadvantaged writes.
  • siblings / version vectors ↩ — resolver that keeps all concurrent versions for the app to merge.
  • anti-entropy ↩ — cadenced compare-and-merge between replicas; the delivery clause's workhorse.
  • read repair ↩ — opportunistic fixing of stale replicas during reads.
  • hinted handoff ↩ — best-effort buffering of writes for an absent replica.
  • tombstone / gc_grace_seconds ↩ — deletion marker and its GC window; F6 lives here.
  • resurrection — deleted data re-spread by anti-entropy after tombstone GC.
  • session guarantees ↩ — RYW / monotonic reads / monotonic writes / WFR; path promises added per-client by routing or tokens.
  • bounded staleness — EC plus a promised ceiling (≤ K versions or T seconds behind).
  • strong eventual consistency (SEC) ↩ — same set ⇒ same state immediately; the safety upgrade of EC's decision clause.
  • tunable consistency ↩ — per-request R/W dials; EC is the R+W≤N region.
  • t-visibility probe — synthetic write-then-read-everywhere measurement of the window.

Solutions — eventual consistency

Rungs 7–8 are worked design discussions, not single correct answers.

Rung 1

1.1 "If everyone stops writing and you wait long enough, every replica ends up serving the same value; until then, no promises." Clause 1 — delivery (every update keeps being propagated until it is everywhere) is the time half: it makes "eventually" arrive. Clause 2 — decision (replicas holding the same set of updates must serve the same thing) is the agreement half: it makes the arrival mean one answer.

1.2 The week proved: under last week's load and last week's network, the window was small enough that nobody looked through it. It cannot prove the guarantee, because the promise is about an unbounded future ("eventually") and the dangerous behaviors live in conditions QA didn't create: the [linearizability] lab measured a design with no guarantee showing 0 violations in 600 calm runs and 109/600 under network jitter. Green under calm conditions is a statement about the conditions, not the store. What to tell the PM: "we measured the window, and we know which flows are safe because of the measurement, not because of the week of green."

1.3 The Monotonic-reads judge can point at a finished thing that already went wrong: read A saw a notice, read B (later, same person) hit a board that has never heard of it — no future event can un-happen that pair. The EC judge's promise is about what will eventually be true; at any moment you accuse it, the future in which it comes true is still allowed, so the accusation can never be proven from what has happened so far. The first kind of promise is called safety (finite witness), the second liveness (no finite witness).

Rung 2

2.1 Delivery clause: the "Copies on the road" strip plus the sync-round lines in the event log — that's the machinery making "eventually" arrive. Decision clause: each replica card's Holds vs Displays pair — the set, and the resolver's function of the set. The measured-but-never-promised number: the staleness histogram (and each board's staleness bar) — the consistency window as data.

2.2 Its "had seen" list contains only the old Saturday notice — not Ben's "market cancelled". So Asha, posting "Sunday", never observed "cancelled": the two writes are concurrent, and LWW's timestamp order between them is manufactured, not causal. A write is only knowingly replaced when some later write's "had seen" list includes it; "Sunday" (the write that dies in scenario 3) appears in no later write's context — which is exactly the Lost-updates judge's rule for calling it lost rather than superseded.

2.3 The decision clause: same set ⇒ same display. Identical Holds rows with different Displays rows are the finite witness that the display is a function of arrival history rather than of the set — the one provable breach of eventual consistency.

Rung 3

3.1 No judge objects — that is the answer. Ben reads the same board twice (sticky): a board's own set only grows, so monotonic reads holds; he wrote nothing, so read-your-writes holds; EC is open; lost-updates pending. He is arbitrarily stale the entire time and every session judge is green. Lesson: session guarantees are about self-coherence, not freshness — a consistently stale reader violates nothing. (Freshness lives in the staleness meter, i.e. in measurement.)

3.2 Worst: first read hits a fresh board (say R1), later reads hit boards the write hasn't reached — the first miss is a read-your-writes violation and, because she'd already seen her write, also a monotonic-reads violation; with three random reads she can collect several of each. Best: all reads land on R1 (or after the 2 s forward window) — zero violations. The single change: sticky routing (in real systems: session affinity or a session token, the [causal-consistency] lab's 3759→0 result). Storage untouched in both worlds.

3.3 With forwarding healthy the gap closes in 2 s (travel time). The two racing mechanisms: v1 forwarding (fast, gives up after one try) and the cadenced sync round (slow, re-offers everything). The number that matters operationally is the fallback: if the forward dies, staleness runs until the next round — so the cadence you configure is your staleness ceiling under failure, which is how you should choose it.

Rung 4

4.1 The delivery clause is now false — "eventually" will never arrive. No judge notices because every judge rules on events (reads, settlements), and a liveness breach produces no event: reads still answer, writes still ack, nothing diverges visibly unless someone happens to read a behind replica and even that is legal. The only signal that can notice: monitoring the machinery itself — time since last successful sync round, replication backlog age, an injected write-then-read-everywhere probe. Alarm on heartbeats, because the contract has no pulse.

4.2 With every clock +60, Ben's write stamps ts = 60 and Asha's later write stamps ts = 75 — the genuinely-newer write now wins. LWW ordering depends only on relative skew; a uniform absolute error is harmless to who-beats-whom. (It is not harmless generally: anything comparing stamps to real time — TTLs, "as of 5 p.m." reads, cross-system merges — inherits the +60 lie; the [hybrid-logical-clocks] lab's drift-bound story is this distinction at full depth.) The scenario-3 disaster needs clocks that disagree with each other.

4.3 Writes accepted at R2 or R3 can never reach R1 (once their one forward is lost, nothing re-offers them to R1) — the delivery clause is broken for exactly the writes that don't originate at the hub. Real-world costume: one-way hub-and-spoke repair ("we only repair from the primary DC"), or a firewall/ACL that lets sync traffic flow one direction. With forwarding on it is masked — everything looks fine until the first lost forward, which is the worst kind of misconfiguration: one that only acts under failure.

Rung 5

Stamps: "Sunday" at R1, t=2, skew 0 → ts 2. "cancelled" at R2, t=4, skew −30 → ts −26.

  • t=6, Ben reads R2. R2 holds {Saturday ts0, cancelled ts−26}. LWW takes the max: "Saturday" wins — ts 0 beats ts −26. Ben's own board refuses to display the write it just accepted: the slow clock stamped his notice into the past, where it loses even to the ancient default. (Read-your-writes is technically satisfied — his write is in the set — the resolver just out-voted it.)
  • t=10 sync round. All pairs exchange; every board now holds {Saturday ts0, Sunday ts2, cancelled ts−26} and displays "Sunday" (ts 2).
  • t=12, Ben reads R3: "Sunday". No monotonic violation — R3's set contains everything Ben had seen.
  • t=20 sync (nothing to copy), settle: converged on "Sunday" — EC judge: KEPT. Lost-updates judge: "cancelled" died — it is displayed nowhere and appears in no later write's context (nobody who wrote afterwards had seen it), so it was dropped unknowingly by the resolver.

The wrinkle worth keeping: a slow clock is as dangerous as a fast one — it doesn't delay your writes, it back-dates them into defeat.

Rung 6

The run: all three replicas hold k = v, ts 5. User deletes k on R1 → R1 removes the row. Next anti-entropy: R2 offers (k, v, ts 5); R1 has no row for k, so there is nothing to compare against — "missing key" looks identical to "key I never heard of", and R1 adopts v again. One round later every replica has k = v back: the delete is undone everywhere, permanently.

Missing mechanism: a delete must be data, not absence — a tombstone (k = ⟨deleted⟩, ts 9) that replicates and competes in LWW like any write, plus a GC grace window and the repair-deadline rule (rung 7.2). Prior-lab terms: tombstone and gc_grace_seconds from [quorums-and-replication]/[btree-and-lsm], and the resurrection story is README F6.

Is it still eventually consistent? Yes — and that is the instructive part. The state is a deterministic function of the update set, replicas converge, every clause holds. The delete was never an update in the system's model, so the contract cannot see it. "Eventually consistent" certifies convergence over the updates the design chose to represent; it says nothing about whether your intent was represented at all.

Rung 7 (worked discussion)

7.1 A monitoring page that beats the customer to each failure:

Failure Signal Why "compare replicas, alert on divergence" misses it
F1 window too wide Injected t-visibility probes: canary write, read back from every replica, plot the delay distribution; run some probes with injected latency/faults A compare check samples instants and exercised keys; the probe measures the window itself, tail included
F2 non-monotonic sessions Enforce affinity/session tokens and alert on token-validation failures; optionally a roaming canary client Divergence between replicas is normal here; the failure is in routing, which replica comparison never sees
F3 LWW clock loss Clock-skew gauge per node (NTP offset) with alert + eviction The store converges promptly and wrong — there is no divergence to find; only the clock reveals the danger
F4 non-deterministic resolver Periodic assertion: same content digest ⇒ same served value, across replicas Plain divergence checks that only compare stored data pass; you must compare digest AND what is served
F5 dead machinery Age alarms: time since last successful repair round per range; backlog age (not size); oldest-undelivered-update age Divergence accumulates in keys nobody reads and the comparer may sample none of them; a dead daemon with a quiet workload compares clean
F6 resurrection risk Node-down duration vs GC grace; repair-completion-within-window compliance; block re-admission past the deadline Nothing diverges until after GC has destroyed the evidence — by then the check confirms the resurrected value as "consistent"

The falsifying query for "our store is healthy": the t-visibility probe under injected network stress. Every other green is compatible with a calm network doing you a favor — the lesson measured four labs running ([linearizability]'s 0/600-when-calm), now applied to operations.

7.2 A delete becomes a tombstone write with its own timestamp, replicated and resolved like any update. Its lifetime must exceed (longest guaranteed interval between completed full sync rounds) + (longest replica outage you will tolerate re-admitting). The rule that ties them: every board must complete a full sync round within the tombstone lifetime — a board cut off longer forfeits its set: it re-enters empty and re-syncs from the others (the bootstrap rule, same as [crdts]' never-restore-from-backup). Cassandra ships exactly this contract as gc_grace_seconds + "repair every node within gc_grace".

Rung 8 (worked discussion)

  • (a) Profile-photo display: EC + session token (read-your-writes for the author — the one person who notices staleness), LWW register resolver (conflict = same user racing themselves; losing one is acceptable). SLO: sign something like "99.9% of reads fresh within 1 s cross-region", and measure it with canary probes including fault-injected runs, not with production complaint counts.
  • (b) Shopping cart: SEC — an OR-Set CRDT, add-wins. The [crdts] lab measured why: 2P-set re-add fails 0/100, OR-set 100/100, and merge survives any delivery disorder. Cart taps must work during partitions and merge across devices; refusing a cart add to get serializability is the wrong trade — this is the flow where strong-everywhere is the design error (80 ms × quorum on every tap, and unavailability during partitions, protecting data whose conflicts merge trivially).
  • (c) Password change + "log out everywhere": the credential record needs a strong (linearizable) write and read path — single ordering authority (leader/consensus-backed, or a single-region primary). "Log out everywhere" is a cross-replica invariant, and the [crdts] warehouse (−2 flour) is the proof that no coordination-free design can enforce one. Practical shape: strong store for credentials/sessions + revocation events pushed to validators; validators may cache with a bounded staleness only if the product accepts "logged out within T" — and that T goes in the contract, not in a wish.
  • (d) Balance next to "withdraw": split the flow. The display may be EC or bounded-staleness with honesty ("as of 12:01"). The withdraw decision must never be computed from an EC read (README F7): the write path enforces the invariant — a conditional/serializable debit at the strong ledger ([optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking]'s conditional write, or a real transaction). An EC balance read + unconditional debit is the textbook silent money-loser.

Grading yourself: full marks is not the strongest contract everywhere — it is a different contract per flow, each justified by what the data tolerates, plus the two honesty artifacts: a measured staleness SLO, and an explicit list of flows forbidden from deciding on EC reads.
