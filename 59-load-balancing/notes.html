Lab 59 · Running It In Production

Load Balancing — you are not spreading load, you are choosing a queue

Tell a friend: Omar stands at the door of Asha's shop and tells each arriving customer which of the eight counters to join. He scans nothing, he opens no new counter, and he makes nobody work faster. All he can change is who waits behind whom. So when Meera lands behind a fourteen-deep queue while the counter beside her stands empty, that is the whole of what went wrong. The hard part is that Omar cannot really see the queues, and a picture of them a quarter of a second old makes confident pointing worse than shutting his eyes.

BEGINNER MODEL — stages 1 to 6

1. Why is every counter healthy while customers give up?

In one breath: Eight servers, all busy, none overloaded, every one handed exactly the same number of requests. And one customer in a hundred still misses the deadline. Nothing is broken. The lines are simply uneven.

It is 14:00. Asha runs a checkout service on eight identical servers. Each one finishes about 50 requests a second. Eight of them is 400 a second, and 320 are arriving. That is 80% of what she can do, a load level most teams would call comfortable.

Her phone goes off. The mobile app is timing out for a noticeable slice of customers.

She opens the dashboard. Here is what makes the incident memorable a year later.

What Asha reads The number What it seems to say
CPU, on all eight servers between 74% and 86% busy, nothing near a limit
Error rate zero nothing is throwing
Requests a second, per server 40, on every one of the eight perfectly even sharing
Mean response time 140 ms well inside the 500 ms SLO
99th percentile response 561 ms past the 500 ms SLO

Nothing is broken. Every server is healthy. Every server is getting exactly its fair share. And about one request in a hundred is still missing its deadline.

There is no faulty machine to reboot. There is no bad deploy to roll back.

Now the part that should feel wrong. Take those same eight servers. Take the same 320 requests a second. Change nothing about the code or the hardware. Have all eight feed from a single shared queue: one line, eight counters, the way a bank does it rather than the way a supermarket does it.

Same fleet, same traffic, ρ = 0.8 mean p99 what actually changed
Eight separate queues, request sent to a random server 139.9 ms 561.5 ms nothing yet — this is today

The 99th percentile goes from a breached SLO to a comfortable one. No new server. No faster code.

Push the same fleet a little harder, to 90%, and the gap gets starker still. Sending each request to a server chosen at random gives a mean of 244 ms and a 99th percentile of 995 ms. Feeding the identical eight from one shared line gives 91.5 ms and 384 ms. Nothing broke, nothing was tuned, and no code got faster. That is 2.7× the latency, purely from where the line forms, and it is the entire budget a load-balancing algorithm is competing for.

There is a second fact that makes the subject hard, and stage 10 is where you meet it. The obvious way to spend that budget is to send each request to whichever server currently has the least work. That is the best rule in this lab, when its information is perfect.

Now spoil the information. Give that rule a view of server load which is 250 ms out of date, and it becomes 2.9× worse than choosing at random. Point it at a fleet where one backend has started failing instantly instead of working, and it sends that backend 66% of all traffic. Its more sophisticated cousin, which balances on observed response time, sends it 100%.

That is what load balancing is for. Not "spreading load" — the load was already perfectly spread, 40 requests a second to every server, and the fleet still missed its SLO. The job is subtler than spreading, and stage 4 is where the difference becomes visible.

One warning about the p99 numbers, before they mislead you. Even at 30% load the 99th percentile in this lab sits near 368 ms, against a mean service time of 80 ms. That is not queueing at all. That is the work itself.

Where the 370 ms floor comes from Value
mean time to do one request's work 80 ms
the shape of that time exponential, so a few requests are far longer
the unluckiest 1 in 100, from work alone 80 × ln(100) ≈ 368 ms

Treat about 370 ms as the floor of every p99 column in this lab. Everything above the floor is queueing. Queueing is the only thing a load balancer can do anything about at all.

2. What does Meera see when she walks in?

In one breath: Eight counters, three of them empty, one of them fourteen people deep. Meera has to pick a line the moment she walks in, and whoever picks for her decides her whole afternoon.

Give the eight servers names: A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H. Each has four worker threads and takes about 80 ms per request. So each finishes 50 a second.

Now the shop. Asha's shop has eight counters with the same names. Behind each counter, four assistants work in parallel. In front of each counter, one queue.

Omar stands at the door. When a customer walks in, Omar points at a counter. That is his entire job. He never scans an item and he never opens a counter.

A customer named Meera arrives at 14:00:00.000. Something must decide, in microseconds and with no time to think, which of A to H she goes to. In the service, that something is a load balancer: a box, or a library, or a kernel routing rule, that sits in front of the eight and forwards each arriving request to one of them. Omar is that box.

Here is the state of the fleet at the exact instant Meera arrives. These are real queue depths lifted from a run at ρ = 0.9.

   A       B       C       D       E       F       G       H
 ██████  ░       ███     ██████████████  ░       ██      ░       ████
   6       0       3            14        0       2       0        4

Thirty-five people are waiting across the eight queues. Three counters are idle. One counter has fourteen people in front of it. At 80 ms each and four assistants, that is about 280 ms of waiting before the next arrival is even looked at.

This is the whole subject in one picture. Send Meera to D and she waits 280 ms before anyone touches her work. Send her to B and she waits nothing.

🔮 Predict. Omar shuts his eyes and points at a counter at random. What is Meera's chance of landing in one of the three empty queues, and what is her chance of landing behind the fourteen?

Reveal Three empty queues out of eight is a **37.5%** chance of a zero wait, and a **12.5%** chance of the 280 ms wall. If you said "she will probably be fine because the average queue is only 4.4 deep", you have made the exact mistake the whole lab is about. Meera does not experience the average. She experiences one queue, chosen for her, and the spread across the eight is what decides her afternoon.

Nothing about the fleet is unhealthy. The average queue is 4.4 deep, which is fine. The problem is the spread.

Why is D so far ahead of B? Nobody did anything wrong. Requests arrive at random moments. They take random amounts of time. Over a few seconds, some counters get unlucky twice in a row and others get a gap. Randomness clumps. That is the entire explanation.

The analogy, and it is only an entry point. Asha's shop is a supermarket. Eight checkouts, and you have to pick a line the moment you walk up, with a trolley you cannot re-park. Everyone knows the feeling of picking the line that stalls. The bank across the road solved this decades ago: one line, and the next free teller takes the front of it. The bank's arrangement is strictly better and everybody knows it. The rest of this lab is about why a web service cannot simply have the bank's arrangement, and how close it can get.

3. Why not just take turns?

In one breath: Omar keeps a counter in his head and sends customer 1 to A, customer 2 to B, and so on round the eight. It costs nothing, it is exactly fair, and it recovers about half of what random pointing wastes.

The first idea any engineer has, and it is a genuinely good one: round robin. Keep a counter. Send request 1 to A, request 2 to B, and so on to H. Request 9 goes back to A. Perfectly even. Impossible to argue with.

Its virtues are real and they are not small.

  • It needs no information at all about the servers. No health data, no load data, no round trip to ask anybody anything. One integer.
  • It is exactly fair in counts. Over any window, every server gets one request in eight, to within one.
  • It is trivially cheap. It is what almost every load balancer ships with as the default. NGINX's default is round robin. HAProxy's roundrobin is its most-used algorithm.

And it works. Here are the figures against random dispatch at ρ = 0.9, where 360 requests a second arrive at a fleet that can finish 400.

ρ = 0.9, 8 backends mean p99 worst queue seen
random 244.0 ms 994.9 ms 80
round robin 156.8 ms 605.7 ms 42
one shared queue (the unreachable ideal) 91.5 ms 384.1 ms —

Round robin removes about half the penalty that random pointing pays. That is a real improvement, bought for the price of one integer.

So why does the story not end here?

4. Where does taking turns lose?

In one breath: Round robin is not an algorithm with a quality. It is a bet that every customer's trolley is the same size. It pays out exactly as far as that bet is true, and in a real service the bet is usually false.

Here is the answer to checkpoint 1, at ρ = 0.8, varying only how much request sizes vary.

The measure of that variation has a name you already own from queueing-theory. It is the squared coefficient of variation of service time, written c²ₛ. In words: how unequal the trolleys are. Zero means every customer has an identical basket. One is the ordinary case. Sixteen means a small tail of customers is doing a month's shopping.

c²ₛ what it means in the shop random p99 round robin p99 ideal p99
0 every basket identical, 80 ms exactly 277.7 ms 99.7 ms 99.7 ms
1 ordinary variation (exponential) 561.5 ms 429.5 ms 371.5 ms
4 some trolleys much bigger 1,324.9 ms 1,231.2 ms 855.8 ms
16 a heavy tail — 3% of trolleys cost 16× 3,847.2 ms 3,863.0 ms 1,460.0 ms

Read the two ends of the round-robin column, because that is the finding.

At c²ₛ = 0, round robin ties the ideal exactly. 99.7 ms against 99.7 ms. It is not an approximation of the shared queue. Under identical work it is the shared queue. Sending every eighth customer to A, when every basket costs the same, is precisely the same thing as A taking the front of one shared line.

At c²ₛ = 16, round robin is 3,863 ms and pointing at random is 3,847 ms. Round robin has become slightly worse than not thinking at all. Both are 2.6× the ideal.

So round robin is a bet: all requests cost about the same. It pays out in exact proportion to how true that bet is. In a real HTTP service it is usually not very true. GET /health and POST /checkout do not cost the same. A cache hit and a cache miss do not cost the same. A customer with 3 items and a customer with 300 do not cost the same.

Why does random dispatch still lose when every basket is identical?

Look again at the c²ₛ = 0 row. Random dispatch is 277.7 ms where the ideal is 99.7 ms. Every request costs exactly 80 ms. There is no variation in the work whatsoever. And random dispatch is still 2.8× off the ideal.

Where does that come from? Arrivals clump even when work does not. In any short window, some counters get three customers and others get none, purely by chance. Engineers call this the balls-in-bins effect. Round robin fixes exactly this, which is why it ties the ideal in that row.

But it tells you something important about what the enemy really is.

There are two independent sources of imbalance. One is randomness in where requests go. The other is randomness in how big they are. A dispatch rule has to defeat both. Round robin defeats the first completely and the second not at all. Random defeats neither.

Which failure is the one that pages Asha?

Both failures above cost you percentiles. This one costs you the service.

Take the eight-server fleet at ρ = 0.8. Now make server A five times slower than the rest. In the shop, that is Arjun on counter A, working through treacle: a bad disk, a noisy neighbour on the hypervisor, a GC pathology, a config that did not roll back cleanly.

Arjun has not stopped. He still greets every customer. He would still pass any health check that only asks "are you there?". He is simply five times slower. Engineers call this a gray failure: degraded, not dead.

Omar, taking turns and knowing nothing, sends counter A exactly 12.5% of the customers, as always.

🔮 Predict. Arjun can now finish 10 requests a second. Omar keeps sending him 40. The other seven counters are fine and the fleet as a whole is only at 80%. What does the fleet's p99 become?

A p99 of 331 seconds. Do not read that as a measurement of anything, because it is not one. Arjun at 5× service time can finish 10 requests a second. Round robin sends him 40. His queue grows by 30 requests every second, forever.

Run it longer and the number simply gets bigger.

simulated horizon round robin, fleet p99 what the number is telling you
5 minutes 172,782 ms the length of the run

It doubles when the window doubles. That is the signature of an unbounded queue. It is queueing-theory's ρ ≥ 1 cliff. And it was not reached because the fleet was overloaded, because the fleet is sitting at 80%. It was reached because the dispatcher insisted on giving a sick counter a healthy counter's share of the work.

And this is 12.5% of Asha's customers. Not 12.5% slower. Twelve and a half percent never answered. Seven perfectly good counters with spare capacity are standing right next to the queue that is eating her traffic.

5. What are we actually asking for, in plain words?

In one breath: Nobody should have to wait behind a long queue while a short queue exists. That is the entire requirement, and everything else in this lab is the difficulty of achieving it.

Three failures, one underlying demand. Before any vocabulary, here it is in plain language.

No request should be made to wait behind a long queue while a short queue exists.

Notice what that does not say.

  • It does not say "give every counter an equal number of customers". That is what round robin guarantees, and it was not enough. It is exactly what Asha's dashboard showed during the stage-1 outage while the SLO was breached.
  • It does not say "give every counter an equal amount of work". That is closer, but it is still a statement about counters. Meera does not care whether the shop is balanced. She cares whether her line is behind a wall.
  • It says nothing about efficiency, throughput or utilisation. Omar cannot change how much work gets done. All eight counters are finishing customers as fast as they can either way. He can only change who waits.

Stated as an engineering property, the requirement is about variance, not mean. The mean queue length is fixed by arithmetic: total work in, total capacity available. queueing-theory's Little's Law says so, and it is an identity rather than something an algorithm can argue with.

The only thing a dispatch rule controls is how that unavoidable total is distributed across the eight queues. The closer to equal it keeps them, the closer the fleet gets to behaving like one big server.

Which gives the sharp version.

A load balancer's job is to make N separate queues behave as much like one shared queue as it can, using information it does not really have.

The second half of that sentence is where the next ten stages live.

6. What if Omar simply looks at the queues?

In one breath: Stop guessing and look. Send each customer to whichever counter has the fewest people waiting. It is not one heuristic among many. It is the requirement, written out as an instruction.

If the problem is that some queues are long while others are short, the fix writes itself. Before pointing, look at the queues, and send the customer to the shortest one.

That is it. That is the central idea of load balancing, and the rest of the subject is consequences.

It has an attractive property that round robin lacks. Round robin had to assume that all trolleys are the same size. Looking at the queue assumes nothing. A counter that got three enormous trolleys has a long queue. A counter that got three small baskets has a short queue. Omar sees the difference without ever being told what a trolley costs.

A measured signal beats an assumed one.

Here is what happens at ρ = 0.9 when every request goes to the server with the fewest outstanding requests.

ρ = 0.9 mean p99 worst queue
random 244.0 ms 994.9 ms 80
round robin 156.8 ms 605.7 ms 42
fewest outstanding requests 93.9 ms 390.8 ms 9
one shared queue (the ideal) 91.5 ms 384.1 ms —

93.9 ms against an unreachable ideal of 91.5 ms. It captures 98% of the available gap.

It handles Arjun's slow counter automatically. His share drops to 3.0% instead of 12.5%, because a slow counter's queue stays long and so it stops being picked. It handles heavy-tailed trolleys too: at c²ₛ = 16 it lands on 1,472 ms against an ideal of 1,460 ms, while round robin was at 3,863 ms.

It looks finished.

INTERMEDIATE MODEL — stages 7 to 9

7. What do engineers call all of this?

In one breath: Six words. Two of them are pairs that get confused so often that the confusion is worth more attention than the definitions.

You have now met every idea in this lab at least once. Here are the names, with the distinctions that actually carry weight.

What exactly is a backend, and where is its real boundary?

In the shop, a backend is one counter: A, B, C and so on to H. Omar can send a customer to it, and after that the customer is in that counter's line and cannot move. Other names for the same thing are upstream, real server, origin, target, endpoint and pod.

Said precisely, a backend is an independently queueing unit of capacity. That phrase is the load-bearing one. What makes something a backend is not that it is a machine. It is that it has its own queue, which the balancer cannot see into and cannot reach across.

Why does the distinction matter? Because it tells you where the real boundary sits, and that is often not where the org chart says. Eight pods on one node sharing a CPU are eight backends to the balancer and roughly one queue in reality. One pod with 200 threads is one backend with a large internal shared queue. If two "backends" share a bottleneck, balancing between them buys you nothing. That is the most common reason a balancer change produces no improvement at all.

Vocabulary you already have from elsewhere: consistent-hashing called these nodes, microservices called them instances, Envoy calls the set of them a cluster and each one a host, and NGINX calls the set an upstream.

What is a dispatch rule, stated exactly?

In the shop, it is whatever Omar does in his head before he points. Products call it the algorithm, the balancing policy, or lb_policy.

Said precisely, it is a function. It takes three things and returns one backend.

What goes in In the shop Why it matters
the dispatcher's view of backend state what Omar thinks he can see this is where all the difficulty lives
some randomness Omar's coin it is what stops every doorman agreeing
the request itself who the customer is it is what makes stickiness possible

Those three inputs are the three axes on which every algorithm in this lab differs. The first one, the view, is where all the difficulty is.

Utilisation, ρ (rho) — whose ρ is it?

You have this from queueing-theory. It is arrival rate divided by service capacity. Here it always means the fleet's ρ unless a sentence says otherwise. 320 requests a second against a fleet that can do 400 is ρ = 0.8.

The individual backends' ρ can be wildly different from the fleet's. In every interesting scenario in this lab, it is. Arjun's counter in stage 4 was past ρ = 1 while the fleet sat at 0.8.

What is "the view", and how old is it (τ)?

The view is what Omar believes about each counter. Its age is how long ago he last looked. Engineers write that age as τ (tau), in milliseconds.

There are three genuinely different situations here. Mixing them up is the single most common source of wrong reasoning about load balancers.

The view What the dispatcher knows Where you actually find it
exact the true, current state of every backend only with one dispatcher that is the sole source of traffic
stale (τ) a snapshot of the truth, refreshed every τ ms any design that gossips or scrapes load statistics
local only the requests this dispatcher has outstanding every real multi-instance load balancer, by default

The third is the normal case and it is the one people forget. Run 20 NGINX instances with least_conn and none of them knows the fleet's least-loaded backend. Each knows only the least-loaded of its own one-twentieth share. Stage 10 measures what that costs.

Which two pairs of words get confused?

Load balancing against sharding, also called partitioning. Both send a request to one of N machines. The difference is whether the choice is free.

  • Balancing: any backend can serve this request, so the dispatcher may choose on the basis of load. Choice is free, so use it.
  • Sharding: only one machine has the data, or the lock, or the session. The destination is determined by the key. Choice is not free, and load is whatever the key distribution makes it.

This is exactly the boundary consistent-hashing was working on, seen from the other side. That lab was about making the sharding function well-behaved when the machine set changes. Turn on sticky sessions and you convert a balancing problem into a sharding problem, inheriting every hot-key problem that lab described. F5 in stage 11 puts numbers on it.

Load balancing against load shedding. From load-shedding, sharpened.

  • Balancing decides which queue a request joins. It assumes the request is going to be served and only asks where. It cannot help you at ρ ≥ 1, because then every queue is growing and there is no good one.
  • Shedding decides whether a request joins any queue at all. It is the only tool that works when the fleet genuinely cannot do the work.

The clean statement: balancing buys you the last 15% of your fleet's usable capacity, and shedding is what happens after that. They are complements. They are usually the same box, which is why they get conflated. Stage 13 shows what goes wrong when they are configured without reference to each other.

Which two terms are deliberately held back until stage 10?

The first is herd, also called a stampede or a thundering herd. The second is power of two choices. Both are introduced at the exact moment the problem that names them appears, because a definition given earlier would not mean anything yet.

8. What picture should you carry in your head?

In one breath: A supermarket that is trying to behave like a bank, using only what it can see from the door. That one sentence predicts almost every result in the rest of this lab.

Two mental models, and they are the same machine seen twice. The first is Asha's shop, which you can picture. The second is the engineer's, which you can compute with. This stage builds both and then maps each part of one onto the other.

The shop and the bank: why is one line better than eight?

Asha's shop has eight checkouts. You must pick a line on arrival and you cannot switch. Some customers have three items and some have three hundred, and you cannot tell which from behind.

The bank across the road has one line and eight tellers. When a teller frees up, the person at the front walks over. Nobody ever waits behind a 300-item trolley while a teller stands idle.

The bank's arrangement is strictly better and it is not close. The gap between the two, on identical hardware and identical traffic, is stage 1's table: 2.7× the mean and 2.6× the p99 at ρ = 0.9.

So why does every web service use the supermarket?

Because the bank's single line requires the tellers to pull work when they are free. A backend server cannot pull. The network only goes one way. The request has to be sent somewhere before anyone knows who will be free next.

A load balancer is a supermarket trying to behave like a bank, using only what it can see from the door.

The rule itself is one line of code. Every algorithm in this lab differs only in that line.

random          →  i = randInt(n)
round robin     →  i = counter++ % n
least conn      →  i = argmin over all n of v[i]
power of two    →  a = randInt(n); b = randInt(n); i = v[a] < v[b] ? a : b

And here is the frame that makes the rest of the lab predictable.

Every dispatch rule is a bet about the world, and its performance is exactly how right that bet is.

  • random bets nothing, and gets nothing.
  • round robin bets all requests cost the same.
  • least connections bets my view of the queues is true.
  • power of two bets almost nothing, and only about two backends at a time.

The order of that list is not increasing quality. It is increasing exposure. Stage 10 collects on each bet in turn.

Where does the shop story lie to you?

Four places, and all four are load-bearing.

1. You can see all eight lines. Omar usually cannot. This is the biggest lie in the story, and it is the whole subject of stage 10. A customer gets a free, instant, perfectly accurate view of every queue. A dispatcher gets a view that is late, or partial, or both. The whole difficulty of load balancing is that acting confidently on a bad view is worse than acting humbly on none.

2. There is only one of you. There are usually many dispatchers. In the shop, when you pick the shortest line you are the only person deciding at that instant. Now imagine two hundred customers walking in together and all picking "the shortest line". They would all pick the same line. That is the herd. It does not exist in the story, and it dominates the engineering.

3. A checkout does not get slower when its queue is long. A server does. Deeper queues mean more memory held, more context switching, worse cache locality, longer GC pauses and more connection state. Real backends have a μ that falls under load. That turns "slightly overloaded" into "collapsing" faster than any model here shows. Both queueing-theory and load-shedding have figures for this. Every number in this lab is therefore optimistic.

4. A checkout is either open or closed. A server has a third state. It can be answering instantly and uselessly, returning 500s in one millisecond. To every load-aware rule in this lab, that looks exactly like the fastest, emptiest, healthiest counter in the shop. Stage 11's F1 puts a number on the consequence, and it is the most surprising number in the lab.

Say it back. Explain to a friend, without the words stale or view, why a doorman who looks at the queues once every quarter-second can do more damage than a doorman who never looks at all.

A good answer sounds like "He looks, sees that counter E is empty, and then sends everybody to E for the next quarter of a second — because as far as he knows, E is still empty. 10. How would you build Omar, one repair at a time?

In one breath: Four versions of the doorman. Each one exists because something broke in the version before it. By the end, the best rule on the page is the one that looks at the least.

We now build the dispatcher for real. Each version is the answer to something that broke in the previous one.

The standing setup for everything below, unless a line says otherwise: eight backends, four workers each, 80 ms mean service time, exponentially distributed. Each backend finishes 50 requests a second and the fleet finishes 400. So ρ = 0.9 means 360 requests a second arriving.

What breaks v2. Three things, in increasing order of severity.

(a) Request sizes vary, and that is the normal case. From stage 4, at c²ₛ = 16 round robin lands on 3,863 ms and random on 3,847 ms. They are indistinguishable. Round robin balances counts, and counts are only a proxy for work when work is uniform.

(b) Backends are not identical. Fleets are heterogeneous more often than anyone admits. Mixed instance types after a capacity top-up. One node on degraded hardware. Round robin sends every one of them exactly one eighth of the traffic. Stage 4's 5×-slow counter produced an unbounded queue on a fleet running at 80%.

(c) The deploy case, which is (b) but happens to everyone, every week. Take the eight-backend fleet at ρ = 0.8 and restart one backend. v3 — what if Omar looks at the queues?

In words: of everything Omar believes about the eight counters, pick the smallest, and send the customer there. argmin is just "the position of the smallest one".

Why does counting outstanding requests work so well when counting dispatched requests worked so badly?

Because outstanding is a feedback signal. A request leaves the count only when the backend finishes it. So a backend that is slow for any reason at all accumulates outstanding requests and stops being chosen. Big requests, a slow disk, a cold cache, a neighbour stealing CPU: all of them show up the same way.

Omar never needs to know why. That is the whole reason to prefer measurement over assumption, and it is a principle far bigger than load balancing.

Break 1 — what if the view is τ milliseconds old?

At τ = 1,000 ms it is 9.6× worse than random, and 25× worse than itself with a perfect view.

The mechanism, concretely. At 14:00:00.000 the snapshot is taken. Counter E happens to be empty. For the next 250 ms, argmin returns E for every single arrival, and there are 90 of them, because the view still says E is empty.

E's real queue goes from 0 to 90 while the other seven drain to nothing. At 14:00:00.250 the new snapshot arrives. E is now catastrophically the worst, and the entire stream swings onto whichever counter is now emptiest. And then it does the same thing again.

The fleet oscillates. Every counter takes a turn being crushed. The average utilisation is still exactly 90%, so Asha's dashboard shows nothing, while the p99 is five times what it should be.

This is a herd, also called a stampede or a thundering herd: many independent deciders, given the same information, making the same choice at the same moment, and thereby destroying the thing they were choosing.

The general law, worth carrying out of this lab. Stale load information is not merely less useful than fresh. It is actively harmful, in a way that gets worse the more decisively you act on it. A rule that commits fully to the best-looking option converts a small delay into a large oscillation. This is a positive feedback loop, and it is the same shape as load-shedding's F3 and the retry storms in distributed-rate-limiting.

v4 — what if Omar looks at only two counters?

The two obvious repairs both fail, and for the same reason.

Refresh faster means every doorman asks every counter more often. The message count is the number of dispatchers times the number of backends times the frequency, written dispatchers × backends × frequency, and it grows with the square of your fleet. Driving τ to 1 ms across 20 dispatchers and 200 backends is 4 million probes a second. Worse, the probes themselves are requests, so they consume the capacity you are trying to measure.

Share one view means a coordination service sitting in the path of every dispatch decision. distributed-rate-limiting measured exactly this trade and the answer is the same: per-request coordination costs a round trip per request. You have added a network hop and a single point of failure, in order to make a routing choice whose entire value is a few hundred milliseconds of tail latency.

The actual repair is neither. It is one of the most surprising results in distributed systems.

a = randInt(8)
b = randInt(8)
i = v[a] < v[b] ? a : b

Pick two counters at random. Send the customer to whichever of those two has the shorter queue. Ignore the other six entirely.

That is the power of two choices (Azar, Broder, Karlin & Upfal 1994; Mitzenmacher's 2001 survey The Power of Two Choices in Randomized Load Balancing is the readable reference).

At ρ = 0.9 with a perfect view:

The step from one choice to two is worth 135 ms. The step from two to all eight is worth 15 ms. The first extra glance does 90% of the work that perfect information does.

Why does looking at two work so well?

Here is the argument in one paragraph you can reconstruct later.

With one random choice, a counter's queue length is decided by luck alone, and luck has a long tail. The worst of n independent random draws grows like log n / log log n. With two choices you only land on a long queue if both your samples were long, which squares a small probability. That squaring compounds at every step, and the worst queue length collapses from log n / log log n to log log n / log d.

That last quantity is doubly logarithmic. It is essentially a constant for any fleet size you will ever operate. Going from d = 2 to d = 3 only changes the denominator from log 2 to log 3, which is exactly why the table above flattens instantly.

You can watch the theory in the numbers. Fleet size varying, ρ = 0.9, one worker per backend so that queue depths are directly comparable:

backends random: worst queue power of two: worst queue least conn: worst queue
4 58.5 18.5 14.8
8 58.0 11.0 7.8
32 82.5 7.5 3.0
128 85.5 6.8 1.0

Random's worst queue grows as the fleet grows. Power of two's shrinks. By 128 backends, random is 12.6× worse.

This is the theoretical result made visible, and it is why the technique is everywhere. NGINX ships it as random two least_conn, and HAProxy as balance random, whose random(<draws>) defaults to two draws. Envoy's default lb_policy is ROUND_ROBIN, and its LEAST_REQUEST is implemented as power of two choices with a configurable choice_count, not as a full scan. The documentation says so, and people who assume otherwise mispredict its behaviour under staleness.

What matters more than the performance?

Power of two is not chosen because it is 89% as good as least connections. It is chosen because it does not herd, and that is a difference in kind rather than degree.

1,000 ms 2,335.7 295.5 7.90×

At τ = 0, least connections wins by 14%. At τ = 500 ms, power of two wins by 6×. The crossover is at about 30 milliseconds of staleness. That is less than one metrics scrape, less than one gossip round, less than one health-check interval. In most systems it is less than a single network round trip.

The reason is structural, not statistical. A herd requires that all doormen, looking at the same stale picture, reach the same conclusion. Taking the smallest of all eight does exactly that: there is one minimum and everybody finds it.

Power of two draws a different random pair for every single request. So Omar and Dev, deciding a microsecond apart, are comparing different counters and cannot agree on a victim. The randomness is not a compromise forced by cost. It is the mechanism that breaks the feedback loop.

This deserves to be the sentence you remember from this lab:

Using less information, less decisively, produced a system that survived bad information. The randomness was the feature.

What breaks v4?

Two things, and both are stage 11.

First, it does not fix the failure magnet. It only dilutes it, to 23.1% instead of least connections' 66.1%, where a fair share would be 12.5%. Choosing a broken counter less often is not the same as not choosing it.

Second, it says nothing about backends of different sizes, sessions that must stay put, backends that have just started, or backends that are about to be shut down. Those are v5.

v5 — what four things does a real balancer add?

Every one of these exists because of something in stage 11. None of them is a dispatch rule. They are all changes to which counters are candidates at all and how much each one counts. In the shop, these are Asha's job rather than Omar's, and that separation is the point.

Weights, for fleets that are not uniform. Give backend i a weight, written w[i], and compare each counter's queue divided by its weight instead of its raw queue. In symbols, compare v[i]/w[i] rather than v[i]. A machine with twice the cores then gets twice the traffic.

This is the same idea as consistent-hashing's weight-as-position-count, and it has the same trap. Weights are static configuration, and static configuration describes the fleet you had when you wrote it. A weight of 2 on a node that is currently degraded is worse than no weight at all. Prefer a rule that measures over a weight that assumes, which is stage 6's principle applied to configuration.

Health checking and outlier ejection, for backends that are broken. This is a separate loop from dispatch. Probe each backend on a schedule and remove failing ones from the candidate set entirely. Asha walks the floor and closes a counter; Omar then chooses only among the counters still open.

This is the only mechanism in the entire lab that addresses the failure magnet, and stage 11's F1 shows that no dispatch rule can substitute for it.

Outlier ejection is Envoy's term for the passive version, and HAProxy and NGINX have equivalents. Instead of probing, it watches the real traffic and ejects a backend whose error rate or latency is an outlier against its peers. It catches things a synthetic probe never will, because it tests the actual code path with actual data.

Slow start, for backends that have just arrived. Ramp a newly-healthy backend from zero to its full weight over some seconds, rather than handing it a full share immediately. Arjun has just clocked on, so send him two customers, not forty. v2's 76-second p99 on a routine restart is what this exists to prevent.

NGINX Plus and HAProxy both call it slow-start. Envoy calls it slow_start_config. Kubernetes has no equivalent, which is why readinessProbe tuning is such a recurring source of deploy-time latency spikes.

Connection draining, for backends that are leaving. Stop sending new requests, but let the in-flight ones finish before the process exits. Close the lane, serve everyone already standing in it, then turn off the light.

The absence of this turns every deploy into a burst of 502s. It is the reason a graceful shutdown handler and a preStop hook are not optional.

Checkpoint — what you can explain now. You can derive all four dispatch rules from the failures that force them. You can say precisely why least connections degrades to worse-than-random under staleness, and why power of two does not. And you can state the crossover conditions, at about 30 ms of staleness or about 16 dispatchers, rather than repeating a preference.

What the next layer adds. Stage 11 is where the direction reverses. Every result so far says load-aware beats load-blind. Two of the eight failures below say the opposite, for reasons that are not a special case.

11. What actually breaks in the real shop?

In one breath: Eight failures, ordered so the most surprising comes first. The first two point in opposite directions, and that contradiction is the whole design lesson of this stage.

F1 — why does the sickest counter attract the most customers?

Counter A loses its database connection pool. Arjun has not gone home and he has not fallen asleep. He now greets each customer, says "sorry, cannot help you", and waves them away in about a millisecond.

He is the fastest counter in the shop. Every queue in front of him empties instantly. To every rule that looks at queue length or at response time, counter A is now the emptiest, fastest, healthiest counter Asha owns.

🔮 Predict — checkpoint 4

Eight backends, ρ = 0.8, one of them returning errors in 1 ms instead of working for 80 ms. Under least connections, what share of the fleet's traffic does the broken backend receive? Fair share is 12.5%.

Then answer the same question for an EWMA-of-response-time rule, the "smart" one that products advertise as adaptive. Commit to both numbers.

At ρ = 0.8, with backend A erroring in 1 ms:

dispatch rule share of all traffic sent to the broken backend
round robin 12.5%
random 12.5%
power of two choices 23.1%
least connections 66.1%
least response time (EWMA latency) 100.0%

The better the load signal, the more completely the fleet commits to the corpse. Least response time sends it literally everything. A backend answering in 1 ms will always beat a backend answering in 80 ms, and it keeps winning for exactly as long as it stays broken.

The mechanism is the same for all of them. Every load-aware rule in this lab optimises a proxy for capacity available: either outstanding requests or observed latency. Both proxies have the same blind spot. They measure how fast the backend gets rid of requests, not whether it did anything useful with them. Failing is the fastest possible way to get rid of a request. A rule that seeks speed will seek failure, because failure is fast.

One guarantee survives. Round robin and random still give it exactly 12.5%, because they are not optimising anything. In this one scenario, and only this one, the dumbest rules are the safest.

And the fix is not a dispatch rule at all. It is health checking and outlier ejection, which are a separate mechanism precisely because they answer a question dispatch cannot. Not "which counter is fastest" but "which counters are allowed to be candidates at all". Asha closes the counter; Omar then chooses among what is left.

This is why every production balancer has two layers. It is also why a load balancer with a beautiful algorithm and a badly configured health check is worse than one running plain round robin.

In production this shows up as a partial outage that is far larger than the broken component. One pod out of eight loses its database credentials after a secret rotation. The error rate should be 12.5%. It is 66%, or 100%, and the graph looks like a total outage caused by a single unhealthy pod. Anyone reasoning "only one of eight pods is bad, so at most one in eight requests can fail" will spend the first twenty minutes of the incident looking in the wrong place.

One configuration trap deserves naming. If your health check is GET /health and it returns 200 from a handler that touches nothing, it will pass on exactly the backend that is failing every real request. Health checks that do not exercise the dependency they certify are the mechanism by which F1 survives the thing designed to prevent it. Envoy's outlier ejection, which watches real traffic rather than a synthetic probe, exists because of this.

F2 — why does the same signal that caused F1 save you here?

Now Arjun does not fail. He gets slow. Five times slow, for any of the ordinary reasons: a degraded EBS volume, a noisy neighbour, a memory leak pushing the process into constant GC, a cache that got evicted.

At ρ = 0.8:

dispatch rule share sent to A fleet mean fleet p99
round robin 12.5% 8,629 ms 330,967 ms → unbounded
random 12.5% 8,692 ms 332,577 ms → unbounded
power of two choices 3.1% 131.5 ms 978.2 ms
least connections 3.0% 102.7 ms 571.0 ms

This is F1 exactly backwards. The load-blind rules, the heroes of F1, send a machine that can do 10 requests a second exactly 40 of them. Its queue grows without bound on a fleet running at 80% of capacity. The load-aware rules notice within milliseconds and back off to a quarter of a fair share.

Why do the two failures point in opposite directions? Both rules answer the question "how fast does this backend clear requests?". That signal correctly identifies a slow backend, and catastrophically misidentifies a broken one, because broken is fast. The signal is not wrong. It is answering a different question from the one you need.

That gives the design principle of the whole stage.

Balancing and health are two different jobs and must be two different mechanisms. Dispatch answers "which of my healthy backends is least busy?" Health checking answers "which of my backends are healthy?" Trying to make one rule do both gives you either F1 or F2, and which one you get depends on whether today's failure happens to be fast or slow.

On a dashboard this appears as the incident where the fleet is at 80% CPU, one node is at 100%, and total fleet throughput has collapsed. The queue on that node is absorbing an eighth of all traffic and returning none of it. The give-away is a per-backend latency graph where one line is three orders of magnitude above the others, next to a per-backend request-rate graph where all eight lines are identical. Identical request rates during an incident are a symptom, not reassurance.

F3 — the herd: what does acting confidently on stale information do?

The mechanics were stage 10's Break 1. The operational shape is what matters here.

You get a herd from any design where dispatchers learn backend load on a schedule rather than continuously. A metrics scrape. A gossip round. A load header on health-check responses. An Envoy load report. You also get it, equivalently, from any design where several dispatchers share a view that updates periodically.

From stage 10, at τ = 250 ms: mean latency 708 ms, against 244 for random dispatch and 94 for the same rule with a live view. Worst queue depth 117 against 9.

Watch per-backend request rate at one-second resolution or finer. At one-minute resolution the herd is completely invisible, because every backend's average rate is exactly fair. They take turns. What you are looking for is eight lines that are individually spiky and collectively flat, with the spikes anti-correlated.

Correctness survives untouched. Every request still reaches a healthy backend and gets a right answer. Throughput is unaffected. Only the tail degrades, which is exactly why this survives so long undetected. Dashboards built on means and one-minute buckets cannot see it at all.

In production it looks like p99 latency several times p50 with no identifiable cause. It looks like a fleet where every backend periodically shows a CPU spike, but never the same one twice. And most characteristically, it looks like an improvement that arrives when somebody changes the algorithm to something simpler. If switching from least-connections to round robin made your tail latency better, you had a herd.

F4 — what does losing one counter cost, exactly?

A node dies, or is cordoned for a kernel patch, or fails a health check. The remaining seven absorb its traffic. Everyone knows this. Almost nobody does the arithmetic.

Nothing about load balancing failed here. This is queueing-theory's cliff, reached by subtraction. The fleet simply no longer has the capacity. The rule is arithmetic, and in words it is this: to survive losing one counter out of eight, run below seven eighths.

To survive losing one of N backends, the fleet must run below (N−1)/N. Eight nodes: below 87.5%. Four nodes: below 75%. Three nodes: below 66%. And because latency is set by spare capacity, "below" needs real margin rather than a rounding error. At ρ = 0.80 the loss is survivable, moving 384 to 427 ms. At 0.875 it is not.

The trap is worst for small fleets. A three-pod deployment must run below 66% to tolerate a single pod restart. Most three-pod deployments are scaled on a CPU target of 70–80%, which means the deployment cannot survive its own rolling update. Since the update takes pods down deliberately, the failure is self-inflicted and it repeats on every release.

What does load balancing contribute here? Only this: it decides how quickly the survivors absorb the load, and whether they absorb it evenly. Compare consistent-hashing's finding, where one position per node meant a dead node's entire share landed on one arbitrary neighbour, at +483%. That is the same failure in a sharded system, and much worse, because there the load cannot be spread at all.

In production this appears as a cascade. One node dies. The survivors slow down. Slower survivors start failing health checks or timing out, so they get removed, and the remaining fleet is smaller still. load-shedding's F3 is this loop at the request level, and it explains why removing the original cause does not end the outage.

F5 — why is session affinity not load balancing?

Somebody needs a user to keep hitting the same backend. An in-memory session, a local cache, a WebSocket, a stateful upload. So they turn on sticky sessions: ip_hash in NGINX, a session cookie, balance source in HAProxy, RING_HASH in Envoy.

In the shop, that is Asha announcing that every customer must always use the counter matching the first letter of their surname. Omar no longer chooses. The surname chooses.

What this actually did was convert a balancing problem into a sharding problem. And a key distribution is whatever your users make it, which is never uniform.

🔮 Predict. Requests are hashed to the eight counters by tenant, and there are 4,096 distinct tenants. That is 512 tenants per counter. Is that enough keys to balance?

Reveal No, and it is not close. With realistic tenant sizes the busiest backend still takes **20.5%** of the traffic against a fair 12.5%, and its queue grows without bound. If you said "4,096 keys over 8 buckets must average out", you assumed the keys were the same size. Make those same 4,096 tenants equal in size and the busiest backend drops to 12.6% and the p99 to 553.8 ms. The number of keys was never the variable. The skew was.

Requests hashed to 8 backends by tenant, ρ = 0.8, where tenant sizes follow Zipf — the ordinary shape of real tenant traffic, in which the largest tenant is many times the median:

distinct tenants busiest backend's share (fair = 12.5%) median p99

Read the last two rows together, because that is the finding. Four thousand tenants is not enough keys to balance, and four thousand equal tenants is plenty. The number of keys is almost irrelevant. The skew is everything.

With realistic skew the busiest backend gets 20.5% of a 320-per-second stream. That is 65 requests a second onto a machine that can do 50, so its queue never stops growing while three other backends idle.

consistent-hashing established the reason and it has not changed. Every hashing scheme agrees that one key has one owner, and that agreement is the feature. You cannot spread a key without giving up the property you enabled stickiness to get.

The stickiness itself works perfectly, by the way. What you lost is the ability to balance, and you lost it in proportion to your traffic skew.

In production this appears as one pod permanently hotter than the others, which no amount of restarting fixes, and which moves to a different pod when the fleet size changes. If your remedy for a hot pod is "restart it", and the heat reappears somewhere else, you have a key-distribution problem rather than a pod problem.

What to do instead: move the state out. A shared session store, or a shared cache, so that any backend can serve any request and the choice becomes free again.

Where that is genuinely impossible, use bounded-load consistent hashing — the technique consistent-hashing flagged as "a load balancer's tool, never a database's". It gives you affinity with a cap. Hash to a preferred backend, but if that backend is already above (1+ε) times the average load, fall through to the next one on the ring. HAProxy ships this as hash-balance-factor. You keep stickiness for most requests and give it up exactly where it would have hurt.

F6 — what if Omar is placing connections, not requests?

This one is nobody's fault and it catches almost everybody.

An L4 load balancer decides when a TCP connection is opened, by hashing the five-tuple of addresses and ports. Once placed, that connection stays where it is for its whole life. A network load balancer, ECMP in a router, iptables in kube-proxy and an AWS NLB all work this way.

That was fine when one connection carried one request. But HTTP/2, gRPC and any connection-pooling client multiplex thousands of requests over a single long-lived connection. The balancer made eight decisions at process start and then never balanced anything again.

In the shop: Omar is not directing customers. He is directing coaches. Each coach parks at one counter and unloads passengers there all day.

You need on the order of a thousand connections before hash placement is safe. Even 256 connections leaves the busiest backend at 57 req/s against a capacity of 50.

The reason is balls-in-bins again. Placing k connections at random into n bins leaves an imbalance that shrinks only as one over the square root of k, written 1/√k. Eight balls into eight bins is spectacularly uneven, with an expected maximum around 3, and every request on a connection inherits its connection's placement. You are not balancing 320 requests a second. You are balancing 8 objects, once, at startup, and living with the result for the process's lifetime.

The balancer is doing exactly what it says on the tin. Connections are spread. Requests are not.

In production this is the single most common "why is one pod at 100% CPU when the others are at 40%" question in any gRPC or HTTP/2 system. The give-away is that the hot pod stays hot for hours, and changes identity only when something reconnects, because reconnection is the only thing that can rebalance.

Four fixes, in order of how much they change:

  1. Balance at L7, per request. Envoy, NGINX, HAProxy in HTTP mode, or a service mesh sidecar all terminate HTTP/2 and dispatch each request independently. This makes the problem vanish, which is the last row of the table. It is the reason service meshes exist for gRPC-heavy systems, and it is worth the extra hop.
  2. Client-side load balancing. Let the client hold a connection to every backend and choose per request. gRPC's round_robin policy, Finagle and Spring Cloud LoadBalancer all do this. The connection count becomes clients × backends, which balances beautifully and scales badly. Five hundred clients and 200 backends is 100,000 connections, and every client must watch every backend.
  3. Subsetting, which is what large deployments actually do. Each client connects to a random subset of, say, 20 backends and balances across those. You get most of the balancing quality with a bounded connection count. Google's Site Reliability Engineering book describes deterministic subsetting. Twitter and Finagle's deterministic aperture is the same idea, with the subsets chosen to tile evenly rather than randomly. This is the standard answer at scale and the name is worth knowing.
  4. Force reconnection periodically, with max_connection_age in gRPC servers or max_requests_per_connection in Envoy. This does not fix the imbalance. It reshuffles it often enough that no single pod stays hot. It is a mitigation rather than a solution, and it is frequently the right one.

F7 — how does a health check lie in both directions?

Health checking is the mechanism F1 and F2 both depend on, and it has two failure modes that point opposite ways.

Too lenient. GET /health returns 200 OK from a handler that allocates a string. It will pass on a backend whose database pool is exhausted, whose disk is full, and whose every real request is failing. That is the exact backend F1 needs removed. A health check that does not exercise the dependency it certifies is decorative.

Too strict. A check with a 100 ms timeout, against a backend whose p99 is 400 ms under load, will fail intermittently. The backend gets ejected. Its load moves to the others. They get slower, and their checks start failing too. This is F4's cascade with the health checker as the amplifier, and it is worse than the original problem, because the machines being removed were working.

Watch the count of backends currently in the pool, as a time series. If it oscillates, you have this. A flat line at N is the only acceptable shape, and most teams never graph it.

Neither failure loses data or corrupts anything. Both convert a small problem into a fleet-wide one.

Four design rules come out of it.

  • Check something real, but check it cheaply. A check that queries the database on every probe from every balancer becomes its own load problem. A common shape is a handler that reports a cached health status, refreshed every few seconds by the application itself.
  • Use asymmetric thresholds. Require several consecutive failures to eject, which is HAProxy's fall and is typically 3, and fewer successes to restore, which is rise and is typically 2. Slow to condemn, quick to forgive.
  • Never eject below a floor. Envoy's outlier_detection has max_ejection_percent, and it exists precisely because a checker that removes everything has converted a latency problem into a total outage. If the whole fleet looks unhealthy, the checker is what is broken.
  • Separate liveness from readiness. Liveness answers "should this process be killed". Readiness answers "should this process get traffic". Wiring a dependency check into liveness means a database blip restarts your entire fleet.

F8 — how does a retry multiply, and how does Omar help it?

A backend gets slow. Clients time out and retry. Omar faithfully dispatches every retry, including to the backend that is already struggling, or worse, spreading the amplified load across a fleet that was already at 80%.

distributed-rate-limiting and load-shedding both put numbers on this loop, and those numbers are theirs rather than this lab's. The relevant point here is what a load balancer contributes to it, which is two things.

It hides the amplification. Offered load has tripled. Every backend's request rate has tripled evenly. Nothing on a per-backend graph distinguishes "three times the customers" from "the same customers asking three times".

It can turn a partial failure into a total one. Retries are dispatched independently of the original. So a request that failed on backend A retries onto backends B and C, spreading the load of a failing region across the healthy one. This is why retrying at the load balancer is more dangerous than retrying at the client. The balancer's retry has no knowledge of how many other retries are in flight, and its position in the path means every client's retries are pooled.

Three things to do, in the order that matters.

  • Budget retries, do not count them. Envoy's retry budget caps retries at a percentage of active requests, defaulting to 20%, rather than "3 attempts per request". A per-request count multiplies fleet load by 3 in exactly the situation where it must not. A budget cannot exceed its percentage no matter how many requests fail.
  • Never retry into an overload. A 503 from a shedder means "there is no capacity", and retrying it immediately is the definition of a retry storm. load-shedding showed Retry-After becoming the synchroniser of the storm when everyone honours the same value. Jitter is not optional.
  • Retry on a different backend, and only once. This is the one thing the balancer is uniquely well placed to do, and it is genuinely valuable. A single immediate retry on a different backend converts a single-backend blip into a small latency bump. Its cousin is hedging: send a second copy after the p95 and take whichever answers first. That is the same idea used proactively, and it is a lab of its own.

12. What are you actually guaranteed?

In one breath: Very little, and most of the things people assume are on the list are not. The one guarantee everybody quotes, exactly equal request counts, is the one stage 4 spent its time attacking.

Load balancing has few guarantees. Being precise about which ones exist matters, because most of the intuitive ones do not.

What is guaranteed?

Every request reaches exactly one backend. The dispatcher is a function, so it returns one answer. That stops being true once retries are involved, which is F8.

No request is lost by the choice itself. In the model of this lab, Omar never drops a customer. He only decides where they wait. Real balancers do drop, through connection limits and queue limits, but that is shedding wearing a balancer's clothes and should be reasoned about as shedding.

Round robin gives exactly equal request counts. To within one, over any window, per dispatcher. This is a real, hard guarantee. Stage 4 is the argument that it is not the guarantee you wanted.

Sticky hashing gives stable placement. The same key reaches the same backend for as long as the backend set does not change, and consistent-hashing is the lesson on what "does not change" costs.

What is not guaranteed but is routinely assumed?

Equal load. Nothing in this lab guarantees equal work per backend. Every load-aware rule is a statistical improvement rather than a bound. Power of two's worst queue at 128 backends was 6.8, against a mean near 1.

Any bound on the worst backend. The one exception is bounded-load consistent hashing, which is the only technique here that enforces a cap. That is precisely what "bounded load" means, and why it has that name.

That the least-loaded backend is chosen. Only true with d = n and an exact view. Both fail in production, and stage 10 is the measurement of what each one costs.

That balancing helps at all.

The algorithm only matters when you are already in trouble. That is both a reason not to agonise over it on a comfortable fleet, and a reason to have the right one configured before the day you need it. Because the day you need it is the day you cannot safely change it.

That it can save you above ρ = 1. It cannot, and this is absolute. Above capacity every queue grows without bound and there is no good one to choose. From F4: eight nodes at ρ = 0.875 losing one gives ρ = 1.0 and a p99 of 2,082 ms with the best rule in the lab. load-shedding is the only tool for that region, and this is the sharp boundary between the two labs.

Which assumptions is every number here standing on?

  1. Backends are independent. If two share a bottleneck, whether the same node, the same database or the same NAT gateway, balancing between them buys nothing. This is the most common reason a real change produces no improvement you can detect.
  2. Any backend can serve any request. The moment that is false you are sharding, which is F5.
  3. Service rate does not fall as the queue grows. False in reality, in the direction that makes everything worse.
  4. The dispatcher itself is not the bottleneck. True for these rules, which are all O(1) or O(d). But a rule that scans 200 backends per request at 50,000 requests a second is 10 million comparisons a second in the request path. That cost is one of the reasons Envoy implements LEAST_REQUEST as power of two rather than a full scan.

What does each rule spend, and what does it buy?

Every dispatch rule spends information to buy tail latency, and it pays for the information in coordination.

Reading down the "fails when" column is the lab. The rules that use more information do not merely stop helping when the information is bad. They actively cause harm, and the harm grows with how decisively the rule acts. That is the single transferable idea here, and it is not really about load balancing at all.

Say it back. Explain to a friend why the rule that looks at all eight counters is the best one in the lab and also the most dangerous one in the lab. Use no numbers.

A good answer sounds like "Looking at everything and picking the very best works perfectly when you can actually see. The moment your picture is a little out of date, or there is more than one of you looking at the same picture, everybody picks the same 'best' one and buries it. And if a counter is broken in the particular way that makes it look empty, looking harder just makes you send more people to it. Glancing at two counters instead of eight is worse when you can see and much better when you cannot."

PRODUCTION MODEL — stages 13 to 17

13. What do you actually operate?

In one breath: Four things matter more than the algorithm, and the algorithm is fifth. If you change one thing after reading this lab, change one of the four.

What did the lesson leave out on purpose?

  • Everything below the request. TCP handshakes, TLS termination, connection reuse, keep-alive, HTTP/2 flow control, NAT against direct server return. All of it matters for throughput, and none of it changes the dispatch decision.
  • Service discovery. This lab assumed Omar already knows which counters exist. Producing that set, through DNS, EDS, Consul or the Kubernetes Endpoints API, is a distributed-systems problem in its own right. gossip-protocols is the lab that covers how such membership propagates.
  • Autoscaling. Adding capacity is the other answer to "the fleet is at 90%", and it has a multi-minute time constant. load-shedding explains why that time constant means it cannot be your only answer.
  • Global traffic management. Everything here is one pool in one place. DNS weighting, anycast and latency-based routing across regions are a different problem with different failure modes.
  • The balancer as a component that can itself fail. A hardware appliance is a single point of failure. A fleet of software balancers is not, but then you have stage 10's multi-dispatcher problem by construction.

Where do these algorithms actually live?

Concrete, so you can find them.

  • NGINX: round-robin as the default, plus least_conn, ip_hash, hash … consistent and random two least_conn. That last one is power of two choices, and the two is not a coincidence.
  • HAProxy: roundrobin, leastconn, source, random, and hash-balance-factor for bounded-load consistent hashing. Its random is power-of-two by default, via random(2).
  • Envoy: ROUND_ROBIN, LEAST_REQUEST, RING_HASH, MAGLEV and RANDOM. LEAST_REQUEST is implemented as power of two choices with a configurable choice_count, not as a full scan. The documentation says so, and people who assume otherwise mispredict its behaviour under staleness. Envoy is also where outlier_detection, slow_start_config and retry budgets live.
  • AWS: ALB is L7 and does round robin or least-outstanding-requests. NLB is L4 and hashes the 5-tuple, so F6 applies to it in full.
  • Kubernetes Service: kube-proxy in iptables mode picks a backend at random, per connection. It is v1 of stage 10, at L4. Every gRPC-in-Kubernetes hot-pod story starts here.
  • gRPC client-side: pick_first, which is the default and means one connection with no balancing at all, and round_robin. That default catches people constantly.
  • Maglev (Google, NSDI 2016) and Ring Hash are consistent-hashing variants, for when you need affinity plus minimal disruption on a membership change. consistent-hashing is the lab for the mechanism.

What should Asha configure first?

In this order, because the order is worth more than the contents.

  1. Health checks that exercise a real dependency, with asymmetric thresholds and a max_ejection_percent floor. F1 and F7. Everything else is optimisation. This is the difference between a 12.5% outage and a 100% one.
  2. L7 or client-side balancing for any HTTP/2 or gRPC traffic. F6. If you change one thing after reading this lab and you run gRPC, change this.
  3. Fleet headroom below (N−1)/N. F4. This is a capacity decision rather than a balancer setting, and it decides whether a node loss is an event or an outage.
  4. Slow start on any fleet where restarts are routine, which is all of them. v2's 76-second p99.
  5. Then pick a dispatch rule. Power of two if you have more than a couple of balancer instances, or any staleness in your view. Least-connections if you have exactly one dispatcher with live state. Round robin if your requests are genuinely uniform in cost. All three are within 1.7× of each other, and the four items above are worth two orders of magnitude more.

What happens when Omar and the doorman who turns people away disagree?

Balancing and shedding are usually the same process, and they interact in a way worth stating explicitly.

A shedder measures this backend's queue delay and rejects when it exceeds a target. A balancer moves work between backends.

If the balancer is doing its job, every backend's queue delay is similar, so the shedder sees a single coherent signal. If the balancer is not doing its job, because of a herd, a hot shard or a hash imbalance, then one backend sheds heavily while seven sit idle. Your error rate is then caused by imbalance rather than by insufficient capacity.

The diagnostic that separates them, and it is worth memorising: if you are shedding while the fleet has spare capacity, you have a balancing problem, not a capacity problem. Adding servers will not help, because the new ones will be as idle as the current idle ones.

Concretely: graph shed rate per backend, never fleet-wide. A fleet-wide shed rate of 5% can be 5% everywhere, which is genuine overload, so add capacity or shed harder. Or it can be 40% on one backend and 0% on seven, which is an imbalance, so fix the routing. Those two have the same number on the dashboard and completely different remedies.

Everything in this lab is invisible on a standard dashboard, which is the main reason it stays broken for so long.

14. Which comfortable beliefs are wrong?

In one breath: Ten sentences that sound obviously true, each followed by the number in this lab that kills it.

Does a load balancer spread load evenly?

It spreads requests. Stage 1 is a fleet with perfectly even request counts, perfectly even CPU, and a breached SLO. Round robin's guarantee is exactly this and it is not enough.

What is true instead: a balancer tries to equalise queues. Equal request counts only equalise queues when all requests cost the same, meaning c²ₛ = 0. Stage 4 shows that is where round robin ties the theoretical optimum, and that c²ₛ = 16 is where it becomes indistinguishable from random.

Is least connections the best algorithm?

With one dispatcher and a live view, yes: 93.9 ms against an ideal of 91.5. With a 250 ms-old view it is 708 ms, which is 2.9× worse than random. With 32 dispatchers it is 1.8× worse than power of two.

Least connections is the best algorithm when its bet holds. Its bet is "my view of every queue is current", and most production deployments violate that by construction.

Does more information about backend load produce better routing?

F1 is the counterexample and the numbers are not subtle. A backend failing in 1 ms receives 12.5% under round robin, 23.1% under power of two, 66.1% under least connections, and 100% under least-response-time.

More information produces better routing when the information means what you think it means. All these signals measure how fast a backend disposes of requests, and failing is the fastest disposal there is. Distinguishing broken from fast is a different mechanism, which is health checking, not a better signal.

Does adding a load balancer add capacity?

Omar performs no work. Every "ideal" column in this lab is a fleet doing the same total work as every other column.

What a balancer does is recover capacity you already own and were wasting on idle backends while other backends had queues. That recovery is worth 0% at ρ = 0.5 and 4.3× at ρ = 0.95, and it is worth exactly nothing above ρ = 1.

Do health checks tell you which servers are healthy?

They tell you which servers pass your specific check. A /health endpoint that touches no dependency passes on exactly the backend F1 needs removed.

A health check is a hypothesis about what "broken" means, and it is only as good as the hypothesis. This is why outlier ejection exists, judging on real traffic rather than a synthetic probe.

Are sticky sessions a small compromise?

F5 says otherwise. With realistically skewed tenants, 4,096 distinct session keys still put 20.5% of traffic on one of eight backends. That is 65 requests a second onto a 50-per-second machine, and its queue grows without bound while others idle.

Enabling stickiness converts balancing into sharding, and it inherits every property of sharding, including that hot keys are unfixable by definition. The number of keys is nearly irrelevant. The skew is everything.

We are only at 80%, so is losing a node fine?

At ρ = 0.875, losing one of eight takes you to exactly ρ = 1.0 and a p99 of 2,082 ms that is still climbing when the run stops.

(N−1)/N is the ceiling, and because latency is governed by spare capacity you need real distance below it. For a three-pod deployment that ceiling is 66%, which is below most autoscaling targets. Many small deployments therefore cannot survive their own rolling update.

Does our L4 load balancer distribute traffic across the fleet?

It distributes connections, once, at connect time. With eight long-lived gRPC connections over eight backends, the busiest backend took 42.2% of traffic, and 62.5% in the worst run.

If requests-per-connection is large, an L4 balancer is balancing a handful of objects at startup and nothing thereafter. Balance at L7, or in the client, or force reconnection.

Is power of two choices a cheap approximation of least connections?

That is how it is usually introduced, and it gets the point backwards. It is better than least connections in most real deployments: 6× better at τ = 500 ms, and 1.8× better at 32 dispatchers.

The randomness is not a cost-saving compromise. It is the mechanism. Drawing a fresh random pair per request means independent dispatchers cannot converge on the same victim, so there is no herd to have. Using less information, less decisively, is what makes it robust.

16. Glossary — what does each word mean exactly?

In one breath: Every term below happened to Asha, Omar or Arjun somewhere above, and each entry says where. Terms are in order of first appearance.

Backend (upstream, real server, origin, target, pod, host) — one independently queueing unit of capacity behind the balancer. One of Asha's eight counters, from stage 2. The definition that matters is has its own queue, rather than is a machine: eight pods sharing one CPU are one queue wearing eight names.

Dispatcher / load balancer — the component that chooses a backend per request. Omar at the door. Performs no work of its own.

Dispatch rule (algorithm, lb_policy, balancing policy) — the function from the view, some randomness and the request, to a backend. All of stage 10.

Shared queue / pooling — the arrangement where all backends draw from one line. The bank across the road from Asha's shop. Unreachable in a network service, and the "ideal" column in every table here. queueing-theory's M/M/k.

ρ (rho), utilisation — arrival rate divided by capacity. In this lab it is the fleet's unless stated. The single most important number: below 0.7 the algorithm is irrelevant, and above 0.9 it is worth several times.

c²ₛ — squared coefficient of variation of service time. How unequal your requests are, or how unequal the trolleys are. 0 means identical, 1 is exponential, 16 is a heavy tail. The number round robin is betting on, from stage 4.

Round robin — take turns. Exactly equal request counts, zero information required, and a bet that all requests cost the same. Ties the theoretical optimum at c²ₛ = 0 and is indistinguishable from random at c²ₛ = 16.

Least connections / least outstanding requests — send to the backend with the fewest requests in flight. This is stage 6's idea and stage 10's v3. Essentially optimal with a live view, capturing 98% of the available gap, and worse than random with a 100 ms-old one.

The view (v), and staleness (τ) — what Omar believes about backend state, and how old that belief is. Three regimes, from stage 7: exact (one dispatcher with live state), stale (a snapshot refreshed every τ), and local (each dispatcher sees only its own requests, which is the normal case).

Herd / stampede — many independent deciders, given the same information, choosing the same target simultaneously and destroying it. What taking the minimum over a stale view produces, from stage 10's Break 1. Invisible on a one-minute dashboard.

Power of two choices (d = 2) — sample two backends at random and use the shorter queue. Stage 10's v4. Captures 89% of what perfect information captures, and is 6× better than least connections at τ = 500 ms, because a fresh random pair per request leaves nothing for dispatchers to agree on. Azar et al. 1994; Mitzenmacher 2001.

Failure magnet — a backend that fails fast looks like the fastest backend, so every load-aware rule sends it more traffic the sicker it gets. Arjun waving customers away in a millisecond, in F1. The figures: 12.5% under round robin, 66.1% under least connections, 100% under least-response-time.

Gray failure — a backend that is degraded rather than down. Arjun working through treacle, in stage 4 and F2. Passes naive health checks, ruins load-blind dispatch, and is handled automatically by load-aware dispatch. The exact mirror image of the failure magnet.

Health check / outlier ejection — the separate mechanism that decides which backends are candidates, as opposed to which candidate is least busy. Asha walking the floor, in v5 and F7. The only defence against the failure magnet. Outlier ejection is the passive form, judging on real traffic rather than a synthetic probe.

Slow start — ramping a newly-healthy backend up to full weight over seconds instead of instantly. Arjun's first two minutes on shift, in v5. Prevents the 76-second p99 that a routine restart produces under round robin.

Connection draining — letting in-flight requests finish on a backend that is being removed. Closing the lane but serving everyone already in it, from v5. The absence of it is why deploys produce 502s.

Session affinity / sticky sessions — pinning a key to a backend. F5. Converts balancing into sharding and inherits the hot-key problem: with realistic tenant skew, 4,096 keys still gave one of eight backends 20.5% of traffic.

Bounded-load consistent hashing — affinity with a cap. Hash to the preferred backend unless it is already above (1+ε)× average, then fall through. HAProxy's hash-balance-factor, from F5. The only technique in this lab that bounds the worst backend rather than improving it statistically.

L4 vs L7 — L4 decides once per TCP connection by hashing the 5-tuple, and L7 decides per HTTP request. F6. With HTTP/2 or gRPC, one connection carries thousands of requests, so L4 balances a handful of objects at startup and nothing after that.

Subsetting / deterministic aperture — each client connects to a random or deterministically-assigned subset of backends rather than all of them. F6's third fix. The standard answer to client-side balancing's clients × backends connection explosion. Google SRE book; Twitter and Finagle.

Retry budget — capping retries as a percentage of active requests, where Envoy's default is 20%, rather than as a count per request. F8. A per-request count multiplies fleet load exactly when it must not.

N+1 / (N−1)/N — the utilisation ceiling for surviving the loss of one backend, from F4. Eight nodes: 87.5%. Three nodes: 66%. Stay below it with real margin, because latency is governed by spare capacity.

Max ÷ mean in-flight — the spread metric nobody graphs and everybody needs, from stage 13. It is 1.0 for a perfect balancer, was 3.4 in F6's eight-connection case, and it degrades before latency does.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 A load balancer exists to stop a request waiting behind a long queue when a short queue exists somewhere else. That is the whole job.

The objection is the point of the lab, not a problem for the answer. Equal request counts are not equal queues, for two independent reasons:

  • Requests are not the same size, so equal counts give unequal work.
  • Arrivals clump, so even identical requests pile unevenly if placement is random.

Round robin fixes the second and does nothing about the first. Stage 1's fleet had 40 requests a second to each of eight servers, identical CPU, zero errors, and a p99 of 561 ms against an SLO of 500 — because a customer does not experience the average.

1.2 "A load balancer performs no work and adds no capacity; it recovers capacity you already own and were wasting on idle servers while other servers had queues."

What it bought: at ρ = 0.9 it is worth 2.6× on p99 (995 ms random vs 384 ms for a shared queue). What it buys at ρ = 0.5: nothing — every rule in the lab is within 5% of every other one there, and within 4% of the theoretical optimum. And above ρ = 1 it buys nothing again, because every queue is growing and there is no good one to pick.

1.3 One big server has one queue. Whoever is free next serves whoever has been waiting longest, so no capacity is ever idle while someone waits. Eight small servers have eight queues, and you have to commit a request to one of them before you know which will free up first. Measured, same hardware and same traffic at ρ = 0.9: one queue gives 91.5 ms mean, eight separate queues with random placement give 244 ms.

We build it the worse way because one big server cannot be made big enough, cannot be patched without downtime, and dies all at once. The eight-queue penalty is the price of horizontal scaling, and the load balancer is the attempt to claw most of it back.

1.4 Round robin bets that all requests cost about the same.

  • Bet right (c²ₛ = 0): p99 of 99.7 ms — exactly the shared-queue ideal of 99.7 ms. It does not approximate the optimum, it achieves it.
  • Bet wrong (c²ₛ = 16): 3,863 ms, against random dispatch's 3,847 ms. Taking turns has become marginally worse than not thinking at all, and both are 2.6× the ideal of 1,460 ms.

1.5 The mechanism is a positive feedback loop, and it has nothing to do with how fast load "really" changes.

At the moment of the snapshot some backend is emptiest. argmin then sends it every single arrival until the next refresh — so the dispatcher's own actions are what make the information wrong, immediately, and in proportion to how much traffic it is carrying. The load did not change on its own; the balancer changed it. Then the snapshot updates, that backend is now the worst, and the entire stream swings onto the next victim.

Measured at ρ = 0.9: a 250 ms-old view makes least-connections 2.9× worse than random. A 15-second view is far past that.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 d = 2 → 4: mean 108.6 → 96.2 ms, p99 413.9 → 392.0. You gain 12 ms of mean, having doubled the state you must read per request. For comparison, d = 1 → 2 gained 135 ms. You pay twice the lookups and, more importantly, you move back toward the behaviour that herds — the wider you sample, the more likely two dispatchers agree on a victim. Do not ship it. The first extra choice is worth 90% of what perfect information is worth.

4.2 8 → 128 backends at ρ = 0.9:

  • random: worst queue 58 → 86. It grows, because the worst of n independent draws grows like log n / log log n, and more backends means more chances for one to be unlucky.
  • power of two: worst queue 11 → 6.8. It shrinks. You land on a long queue only if both samples were long, which squares a small probability; the worst queue is log log n / log d, effectively a constant.

By 128 backends random is 12.6× worse. This is the clearest evidence in the lab that the technique is not a cost-saving approximation.

4.3 Dividing by weight solves heterogeneous capacity — a machine with twice the cores gets twice the traffic, so it stops being the fleet's bottleneck or its idler.

The new failure: a weight is static configuration, and it describes the fleet you had when you wrote it. Plain least-connections measures, so it discovers a degraded machine automatically; weighted least-connections partly overrides that measurement with an assumption. A weight of 2 on a machine that is currently sick tells the balancer to send it twice as much, and it will keep doing so until somebody edits a config. Prefer measurement over configuration wherever you can.

4.4 Switch to round robin. At c²ₛ = 0 it ties the theoretical optimum (99.7 ms) and beats least-connections (114.4) and power of two (131.4), while needing no state, no view, and no coordination — so it also cannot herd and does not degrade when they add a second load balancer.

What they give up: all of the automatic protection. Round robin will hand a 5×-slow backend a full share and produce an unbounded queue on a fleet at 80%, and will hand a cold restart a full share and produce a 76-second p99. They are now entirely dependent on health checks, outlier ejection and slow start, and those must be right before the switch, not after.

4.5 Two backend-side changes, each moving a lever in the model:

  1. Make the service faster (cut mean service time S). This lowers ρ for a fixed arrival rate, and the load sweep shows the gap between rules collapses as ρ falls — at ρ = 0.5 nothing matters. It also lowers the p99 floor, which no dispatch rule can touch.
  2. Make request costs more uniform (cut c²ₛ) — split a heavy endpoint onto its own pool, cap page sizes, move a slow path to async. From the c²ₛ table this is worth going from 3,847 ms to 278 ms under random dispatch. Variability is the lever the balancer is fighting, and removing it at the source is strictly better than compensating for it.

A third, if you count it: add backends. From F4's table, more backends at the same ρ pool better — at n = 128 even least-connections' worst queue is 1.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 Lowest average response time. This is the failure magnet in its purest form, and it is the worst rule in the lab for it.

Smallest scenario: eight backends, one loses its database connection pool and returns HTTP 500 in 1 ms. Its 10-second average response time is 1 ms; every healthy backend's is 80 ms. It wins every comparison, forever.

Measured: 100.0% of fleet traffic to the broken backend. Violated guarantee: none, formally — the rule did exactly what it promised. The design error is that "fastest" was assumed to mean "healthiest", and failing is the fastest thing a server can do.

6.2 Shared Redis counter. Three separate flaws, in increasing severity:

  1. You have put a network round trip in the dispatch path to save a few hundred milliseconds of tail latency, and you now pay it on every request. distributed-rate-limiting priced exactly this trade.
  2. You have created a single point of failure for a component whose entire purpose is surviving the failure of components. When Redis is slow, every dispatch is slow — and a slow store is far more dangerous than a dead one, because it exhausts your request threads while every dashboard stays green.
  3. The view is still stale. Between your GET and the dispatch, other pods have dispatched. You have bought a smaller τ at enormous cost, and τ = 0 is what you needed. With 30 pods you are also generating 30 × λ Redis operations per second.

Smallest failure: Redis takes 200 ms once. All 30 pods block, then all 30 read the same now-stale counters and stampede. You have built a synchronised herd.

6.3 Eject after one failed check. Too strict, and it is F7's second mode.

Smallest scenario: the fleet is at ρ = 0.85, so the backends' p99 is naturally around 430 ms. One health check hits a GC pause and takes longer than its timeout. That backend is ejected. Its traffic moves to the survivors, pushing them from 0.85 to 0.97, whose p99 goes to seconds, so their checks start timing out too. Within two check intervals you have ejected the fleet.

Violated: nothing about correctness — you have converted a latency problem into a total outage. The fixes are asymmetric thresholds (slow to condemn, quick to forgive) and max_ejection_percent.

6.4 Consistent hashing on user ID, "50,000 users so it will be even". The count of keys is nearly irrelevant; the skew is everything.

Smallest scenario: user traffic follows the usual shape where the largest account is many times the median. Measured with Zipf-distributed tenants over 8 backends at ρ = 0.8: 4,096 tenants still put 20.5% of all traffic on one backend — 65 requests a second onto a machine that can do 50 — while the same 4,096 tenants made equal in size produce 12.6% and a perfectly healthy p99 of 554 ms.

Violated: the assumption that many keys implies even load. What they have built is sharding, not balancing, and consistent-hashing already established that a hot key is unfixable by any hashing scheme, because every scheme agrees one key has one owner.

6.5 Retry 3× on a different backend. Fine for an isolated blip; a load multiplier in exactly the situation you cannot afford one.

Smallest scenario: the fleet is at ρ = 0.8 and one dependency slows, so requests start timing out. Each failure becomes 3 requests. Offered load goes from 320 to 960 against a capacity of 400. Now every backend is at ρ = 2.4, everything times out, and every timeout produces 3 more.

Violated: the implicit assumption that retries are rare. A per-request count multiplies fleet load by 3 precisely when it must not. The fix is a retry budget — a cap on retries as a percentage of active requests (Envoy's default is 20%) — which cannot exceed that percentage no matter how many requests fail.

6.6 Four pods, CPU target 75%. The arithmetic is (N−1)/N.

With 4 pods, surviving the loss of one requires ρ ≤ 75% — and that is the break-even point, not a safe one. At exactly ρ = 0.75 with 4 pods, losing one puts the survivors at ρ = 1.0. From F4's table, a fleet at ρ = 1.0 has a p99 of 2,082 ms and rising; it does not have a steady state at all.

And because they scale on 75%, they are at 75% by design most of the time. So the smallest failing scenario is: one pod restarts. Which happens on every deploy.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 The failure magnet, live.

First five minutes, in order of value:

  1. Take the backend out of the pool. Manually, by hand, right now — drain it in the balancer or fail its health check deliberately. This is the whole fix and it takes one command. Error rate goes from 66% to 0%, not to 12.5%, because the remaining seven are healthy.
  2. If you cannot do that, switch the dispatch rule to round robin. Error rate goes from 66% to 12.5% — a 5× improvement from a config reload. Note this is the one and only scenario in the lab where round robin is the right answer, and it works because it optimises nothing.
  3. Check the fleet has headroom for seven-of-eight at current load before doing either. If you were at ρ = 0.875 you are about to hit F4.

Next thirty minutes:

  1. Fix the health check that let this happen — it passed on a backend failing every real request, which means it does not exercise the failing dependency.
  2. Turn on outlier ejection on error rate, with max_ejection_percent set so the mechanism cannot remove the fleet.
  3. Only then think about the code.

Most likely to make things worse: restarting the backend without draining it (you lose in-flight requests and it rejoins at full weight — v2's cold-start problem), and increasing timeouts (F1 is not a timeout problem; it is answering too fast).

7.2 Health check for a Postgres → transform → Kafka service.

What it does: a handler returning a cached status, recomputed by a background task every 5 seconds. The background task does a trivial real query (SELECT 1 on a pooled connection, with a 1-second timeout) and checks the Kafka producer's metadata age. It returns 503 if either has failed for two consecutive attempts.

Why cached: every balancer instance probes every backend; a check that hits Postgres directly on every probe is dispatchers × backends × frequency queries per second against the database — the check becomes its own load problem.

Frequency and thresholds: probe every 2 s; eject after 3 consecutive failures (~6 s to detect); restore after 2 successes. Asymmetric on purpose: slow to condemn, quick to forgive. Cap ejection at 50% of the pool.

What it will not catch: a backend that is slow but working — the gray failure. SELECT 1 returns fine while real queries take 5 seconds. It will also miss a backend that is wrong rather than broken (returning bad data quickly).

The second mechanism: outlier ejection on real traffic — compare each backend's observed p99 and error rate against its peers and eject the statistical outlier. That tests the actual code path with actual data, which no synthetic probe does. Plus load-aware dispatch, which routes around slowness automatically without needing to classify it (F2).

7.3 Removing a backend cleanly.

  1. Mark it not-ready (fail readiness, or set the balancer's drain flag). Skip it, and new requests keep arriving at a process about to exit.
  2. Wait for propagation. Every dispatcher must observe the change; the wait must be at least one health-check interval plus one discovery-propagation delay. Skip it, and the pod stops accepting while some dispatchers still think it is live — a burst of connection refusals. This is the step almost everyone skips, and it is why preStop: sleep 15 exists.
  3. Stop accepting new connections, keep serving existing ones.
  4. Wait for in-flight requests to complete, bounded by a drain timeout that is at least your p99. Skip it, and every in-flight request becomes a 502.
  5. Close idle keep-alive connections, so clients reconnect elsewhere rather than reusing a socket to a dying process. Skip it, and HTTP/2 clients keep multiplexing onto it (F6 in reverse).
  6. Exit.

And on the way back in: slow start, or you get v2's 76-second p99.

7.4 Subsetting for 40 clients × 200 servers.

Subset size: start from what balancing quality needs, not from the connection budget. Power of two on a subset of 20 is close to power of two on 200 — the log log n scaling flattens fast — while 20 × 40 = 800 connections instead of 8,000. Never go below ~5, or a single backend failure removes a large fraction of a client's options.

How chosen: deterministically, not randomly — assign subsets so they tile the backend set evenly. Google's SRE book calls this deterministic subsetting; Finagle's deterministic aperture is the same idea. Random subsets give a binomial spread in how many clients point at each backend, which reintroduces exactly the imbalance you were trying to remove.

On membership change: recompute with a rule that moves as few assignments as possible — this is consistent-hashing's monotonicity requirement, in a new place. A naive recomputation reshuffles every subset at once and produces a fleet-wide connection storm on every deploy.

Failure mode of overlap: if the assignment degenerates so that many clients pick overlapping subsets, some backends are in nobody's subset (idle, wasted) while others are in everybody's (hot). The symptom is a bimodal per-backend load graph that does not change over time. Guard it by asserting that every backend appears in at least k subsets, and alert if that ever drops.

7.5 Rules for retry-on-another-backend.

  1. Budget, do not count. Cap total retries at a percentage of active requests (20% is a reasonable default). Prevents: the 3× fleet-load multiplication of 6.5, which happens exactly when you are already overloaded.
  2. Never retry a 503 from a shedder or a 429. Those mean "there is no capacity", and retrying is the definition of the storm. Prevents: load-shedding's and distributed-rate-limiting's retry storms.
  3. Retry once, on a different backend. Prevents: re-sending into the same failing queue, which is the case where a retry cannot possibly help.
  4. Only retry idempotent requests, or ones carrying an idempotency key. Prevents: double-charging. Your idempotency lab is the prerequisite here, not an optional extra.
  5. Jitter every delay. Prevents: your own Retry-After becoming the synchroniser that converts many independent retries into one simultaneous wave.
  6. Do not retry at more than one layer. Client retries × balancer retries × library retries multiply. Prevents: a 3-deep stack turning one failure into 27 requests.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 The gRPC hot pod.

Diagnosis, from the description alone: F6 — the balancer is placing connections, not requests. Three details give it away: the hot pod is consistently hot (so it is not a transient herd), it changes identity weekly (roughly the pod or client restart cadence, which is the only thing that can re-place a connection), and restarting moves the problem (the connection lands somewhere else). A Kubernetes Service sends gRPC over a small number of long-lived HTTP/2 connections placed once by kube-proxy.

With 6 callers and 12 pods, if each caller holds one connection then 6 of the 12 pods get no traffic at all and the busiest gets whatever share of connections it happened to win.

Confirming measurement: per-pod request rate at 1-second resolution (not CPU), plus the count of established connections per pod. You expect to see a small integer number of connections per pod, several pods at zero, and a request rate that is flat for hours.

Three fixes, increasing in how much they change:

  1. max_connection_age on the server (e.g. 30 minutes, with jitter). Costs: nothing architecturally; a reconnect blip per interval. Buys: the imbalance reshuffles constantly so no pod stays hot. This is a mitigation, not a fix — the fleet is still unbalanced at every instant.
  2. Client-side balancing — gRPC's round_robin policy with a headless Service so the resolver returns all 12 pod IPs. Costs: 6 × 12 = 72 connections (fine at this size), and every client must watch every pod. Buys: real per-request balancing with no extra hop. This is the right answer at this scale.
  3. An L7 proxy or service mesh — Envoy sidecars, or a gRPC-aware ingress. Costs: a hop, memory per pod, and an operational surface you did not have. Buys: per-request balancing plus outlier ejection, retry budgets, slow start and observability, and it scales to the point where option 2's connection count stops being acceptable.

8.2 Choosing a rule with a straight face.

The questions, in order, each with a determinate answer:

  1. How many dispatchers are there, and does each see the whole fleet's traffic? One dispatcher with live state → least-connections is genuinely best (93.9 ms vs 108.6). More than about 16 → power of two wins (159.5 vs 292.4 at 32).
  2. How old is the dispatcher's view of backend load? Live (its own in-flight counts, updated synchronously) → least-connections. Anything on a refresh schedule → the crossover is about 30 ms, so in practice: power of two.
  3. How variable is the cost of a request? Genuinely uniform (c²ₛ ≈ 0) → round robin ties the optimum and beats both load-aware rules. Anything else → a load-aware rule.
  4. Can any backend serve any request? No → you are sharding, and the rule choice does not exist; go and read F5.

Combined: uniform requests → round robin. Variable requests, one live dispatcher → least-connections. Variable requests, many dispatchers or any staleness → power of two. That last case is most production systems, which is why it is most products' recommended setting.

The four things to fix before the rule matters at all:

  1. Health checks that exercise a real dependency, with asymmetric thresholds and an ejection cap (F1, F7).
  2. L7 or client-side balancing for any HTTP/2 or gRPC traffic (F6).
  3. Fleet headroom below (N−1)/N (F4).
  4. Slow start on a fleet where restarts are routine (v2).

Justification for the ordering: the four items above are worth two orders of magnitude (a 12.5% outage vs a 100% one; a 569 ms p99 vs a 338-second one) and the rule choice is worth 1.7×.

8.3 Sticky sessions you cannot remove.

Today: 8 backends, ρ = 0.75, largest tenant at 18% of traffic. Fair share is 12.5%. The backend owning the largest tenant carries at least 18% before any other tenant lands on it — and with ordinary skew in the rest, its realistic share is 22–25%. That is 0.75 × 8 × 0.22 ≈ 1.3× its own capacity. Its queue is growing; the other seven have spare capacity they cannot lend.

When they add a 9th backend: with plain modulo hashing, almost every session moves and every in-memory session is lost at once. With consistent hashing, ~1/9 move. Either way the largest tenant simply relocates — one backend is still over capacity, just a different one. Adding capacity does not fix a hot key; that is the property consistent-hashing established and it has not changed.

Options, ranked:

  1. Bounded-load consistent hashing (HAProxy's hash-balance-factor). Hash to the preferred backend unless it is already above (1+ε)× the fleet average, then fall through. You keep affinity for the overwhelming majority of requests and give it up exactly where it would have hurt. This is the only option that bounds the worst backend, and it needs no application change.
  2. Split the whale. Give the largest tenant its own dedicated pool, sized for it. The remaining 82% of traffic over 8 backends is much closer to even. Costs capacity and a routing rule; buys blast-radius isolation as well.
  3. Sub-key the session. If the session key can carry a suffix (tenant:shard-3), the whale spreads across several backends. Only works if the application does not need all of one tenant's state in one place — often it does not.
  4. Overprovision and wait. Run the whole fleet at ρ = 0.5 so that a 25% share is survivable. Expensive, does nothing about the next larger tenant, but it is the option that requires no change this quarter and it should be stated honestly as the fallback.

8.4 Three pods, 70% CPU target, five deploys a day.

What happens during each deploy. maxUnavailable: 1 means one of three pods is gone, so the fleet is at 2/3 capacity for the duration. If they were at ρ = 0.70 on three pods, they are now at 0.70 × 3/2 = 1.05. That is above capacity: the queue grows for as long as the rolling update takes, on every pod in turn, five times a day. The (N−1)/N ceiling for three pods is 66.7%, and their scaling target is above it. And that ignores the new pod's cold-start period, during which it is effectively worth much less than one pod (v2's measurement: a 5×-slow cold node handed a full share produced a 76-second p99).

A plan, cheapest first:

  1. Set maxSurge: 1, maxUnavailable: 0. Bring the new pod up before removing the old one. Cost: one extra pod for the duration of a deploy, i.e. minutes a day. This alone removes the capacity dip entirely and is the single highest-value change.
  2. Add slow start (or a readiness probe that genuinely waits until the pod is warm). Cost: nothing. Prevents the new pod from being handed a third of the traffic while it is still interpreting bytecode.
  3. Lower the CPU target to 55–60%, or set minReplicas: 4. With four pods the ceiling is 75%, so a 60% target has real margin. Cost: roughly one additional pod's worth of spend. Do this if step 1 is not available.
  4. Do not raise the target or reduce replicas to save money; three pods is already the fragile end of the curve.

What to monitor to know it worked: per-pod request rate and per-pod p99 during a deploy, at 1-second resolution, plus the healthy-pod count. Healthy: the count never drops below 3, and no pod's p99 exceeds the fleet's by more than ~2×. Currently they will see the count hit 2 and one pod's latency spike each time.

8.6 Counterfactual — what if the view were live, free and exact?

What survives unchanged:

  • The pooling result. N queues are still not one queue, and the ideal is still unreachable — a request must be committed to a backend before anyone knows who frees up first. Least-connections with a perfect view still measured 93.9 ms against the shared queue's 91.5.
  • F1, the failure magnet, entirely. This is the important part of the answer. A perfect view of outstanding requests still shows a fast-failing backend as the emptiest one in the fleet; a perfect view of response time still shows it as the fastest. The information was never stale — it was answering a different question than the one you needed. Least-response-time sends it 100% with a live view.
  • F4 ((N−1)/N), F5 (sticky skew), F6 (connection placement), F7 (health checks) and F8 (retries). None of them is an information problem.

What disappears: F3 and the whole of stage 10's second half. No staleness means no herd, so least-connections would simply be the best rule and power of two would be a curiosity — "89% of the benefit for 1/4 of the lookups", interesting only where the fleet is huge enough that an O(n) scan per request is itself the cost.

What you would still need in production: health checking and outlier ejection (F1 is untouched), slow start (a cold backend has low in-flight because it has just arrived, so a perfect view actively directs traffic at it), connection draining, weights or per-request balancing for HTTP/2, and retry budgets.

The lesson to take from that: most of what a real load balancer does is not dispatch. Perfect information would delete one stage of this lab and leave the other seven failures exactly where they are.
