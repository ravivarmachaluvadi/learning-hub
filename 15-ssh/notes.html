Lab 15 · One Machine, Done Right

SSH — how two strangers on a hostile network end up trusting each other

Tell a friend: Alice in Delhi must send one order to Arjun's warehouse in Chennai, and every courier on the road is a stranger. Omar is one of them, and he is happy to open the envelope, or to answer in Arjun's name and never deliver it. Locking the box is easy, and on its own it fixes nothing, because Omar can run the lock trick twice, once with each end, and sit in the middle reading everything. So SSH gives Arjun a permanent name badge that he signs with, and Alice gets exactly one way to judge that badge: she wrote it down the first time, and she holds him to it forever. Same cryptography as TLS, opposite answer on identity — your own memory of last time, instead of a notary's word.

Watch that decision happen in visualization.html, published as The Host Key Decision. What this lab is made of, and where it sits among your other labs, is in section 19 at the end.

BEGINNER MODEL

1. Why does anyone need to solve this at all?

In one breath: You type commands on a computer that is far away. The words travel through machines other people own. Any one of those people can read what you type, change it, or answer you instead of the computer you meant.

You have a computer in a data centre and you are sitting in a café. You need to type commands on it. The words you type, and the results that come back, travel through the café's wifi. Then through the café's internet provider. Then through several networks you have never heard of, and finally the data centre's network.

Every one of those is a machine owned by somebody else.

Before this problem was solved, the standard tool was telnet. It sent everything as ordinary readable text. Your password was ordinary readable text. Anyone running a network anywhere on that path could read it, save it, and use it later. In 1995 a researcher at a Finnish university found exactly that happening on his own network. He wrote SSH in response.

Here is why the stakes are higher than they look. SSH is the tool by which humans and deployment pipelines get administrative control of servers. If it fails, the attacker does not get to read one message. The attacker gets to become you on the machine.

Hold on to that. The failure is not a leak. The failure is a handover.

2. What can go wrong between Alice and Arjun?

In one breath: Alice sends an order to a warehouse she has never visited, through four couriers she has never met. Three different things can go wrong, and each one needs a completely different fix.

Alice is in Delhi. She needs to send instructions to a warehouse in Chennai that only she is allowed to give: ship the gold. The manager there is Arjun. She has never visited the warehouse and has never met him.

She writes a letter and hands it to a courier. The courier passes it to another courier, who passes it to a van driver, who passes it to a local runner. Four strangers hold the letter. One of them is Omar.

Three separate things can go wrong. It is worth keeping them apart, because the rest of this lab is about how differently each one is solved.

  1. Somebody reads it. Omar opens the envelope and learns the instruction.
  2. Somebody changes it. Omar rewrites "ship the gold to warehouse 4" as "ship the gold to my house".
  3. Somebody impersonates the warehouse. Omar never delivers the letter at all. He writes back on warehouse letterhead saying received, send more. Alice is now in a conversation with a stranger and does not know it.

Read that list again and notice the shape. The first two are about the letter. The third is about the person. That is the split the whole lab turns on.

3. Does a locked box fix it?

In one breath: Alice puts the letter in a box with a padlock. That stops Omar reading it and stops him changing it. It does not tell her who is holding the other key.

The obvious fix. Alice puts the letter in a box with a padlock. Couriers carry the box. Nobody can read or change what is inside.

This kills problems 1 and 2 completely. In software it is also a solved matter. Encryption is mature, fast and free.

But it raises an immediate question. A padlock needs a key, and Arjun needs that key in order to open the box. Alice has never met him, so she cannot hand him one. Sending the key inside the box is obviously absurd.

There is a real mathematical answer to this, and you already met it in the tls lab. Engineers call it key exchange. Alice and Arjun can shout numbers at each other in public, in front of everybody, and each end up holding the same secret number that nobody listening can work out.

Eve, sitting by the road with her notebook, hears both numbers and still cannot compute the secret. That is not a trick of obscurity. It is arithmetic.

🔮 Predict: Alice and Arjun do a public key exchange, agree a secret, and encrypt everything from then on. Which of the three problems above are now solved?

Reveal

Problems 1 and 2 are solved — with respect to whoever she exchanged keys with. Problem 3 is completely untouched, and the encryption actively makes it harder to notice, because everything now looks secure. If you said "all three", you are assuming the person on the other end of the exchange is Arjun. Nothing in the exchange said so. Section 4 shows what that costs.

4. What does Omar do about the locked box?

In one breath: He does the clever key exchange twice. Once with Alice while pretending to be Arjun, once with Arjun while pretending to be Alice. Both ends see a perfectly healthy encrypted conversation.

Here is what a courier does about that clever key exchange.

He does it twice.

Omar runs one key exchange with Alice, pretending to be the warehouse. He runs another with Arjun, pretending to be Alice. Now he holds two secrets. One he shares with Alice, one he shares with Arjun. Every message Alice sends, he unlocks, reads, re-locks, and forwards.

Both ends see a perfectly healthy encrypted conversation. There is no error, no warning, and no delay. Experiment 3 does exactly this. In the code the interceptor is called Mallory, which is the traditional name for a courier who edits letters rather than merely reading them.

  client encrypts and sends: 36a622e35c38fd1441ffcc598bbc0cb4d074dc8e...
  Mallory decrypts it as:    "cat /home/ravi/.aws/credentials"   <-- plaintext
  Mallory re-encrypts to the server, which reads: "cat /home/ravi/.aws/credentials"

Stare at the middle line. That is your cloud credentials file, in plain text, on a stranger's screen, during a session your terminal reports as encrypted.

The deeper problem is now visible. Encryption answers "can anyone else read this?". It never answers "who am I talking to?". Those are two separate questions, and only the second one is hard.

5. What do we actually need?

In one breath: Three things, and they are independent. Alice must know which machine answered. Arjun must know which person is asking. And a key stolen next year must not open this year's recordings.

Stated plainly, we need three things. Engineers would call this list the real requirement. Watch how each one lands on a different character.

Need In the story Engineers say
Alice knows which machine she reached, before she sends anything useful "it is Arjun's warehouse, not Omar's shed" host authentication
Arjun knows which person is asking, and the proof works nowhere else "this really is Alice, and her note is useless in another warehouse" user authentication
A key stolen next year cannot open a recording made this year "Eve's notebook stays gibberish forever" forward secrecy

The first one is what this whole lab circles around. The other two turn out to have clean answers once the first is settled.

Notice which of Alice's three worries from section 2 has now been renamed. The courier who answers in Arjun's name is worry 3, and it is the one that took a new mechanism rather than a bigger padlock.

6. What is the key idea?

In one breath: Give every machine a permanent name badge that it uses only to sign, never to lock anything. Then let the client decide whether that badge is the right one by remembering what it saw last time.

Two sentences carry SSH.

Give every server a long-lived key pair that stays with the machine for its whole life. Use it not to encrypt anything, but only to sign a summary of the key exchange that just happened. That signature proves the machine holding the key is the same machine you exchanged secrets with.

In the story: Arjun has a signet ring that never leaves the warehouse. He does not lock the box with it. He presses it into wax on a note that says "the numbers we just shouted at each other were mine". Omar, standing in the middle, shouted different numbers to each side. His wax will not match either.

And then the part that makes SSH different from everything else:

Have the client decide whether that long-lived key is the right one by remembering what it saw last time.

No certificate authority. No third party. Alice's only source of truth is her own past.

That is the sentence to hold against the TLS lab. Nikhil asked Asha the notary whether the seal was genuine, and Asha had vouched for the bank in advance. Alice asks nobody. She opens her own address book and compares.

7. What do engineers call the things Alice just met?

In one breath: Six words carry this entire lab. Every one of them names something that already happened to Alice, Arjun or Omar. Learn these properly and the rest is detail.

Six terms, each earned where the story earned it. The two tables at the end of the section map every word back to the moment it happened.

What is the machine's permanent name badge?

Arjun's signet ring is a host key: a key pair generated once when a machine is built. The private half lives in /etc/ssh/ssh_host_ed25519_key and never leaves the machine. The public half sits beside it in the matching .pub file, and is handed to anyone who connects. The private half is used only to sign.

The word host is doing real work. Both halves of SSH's identity story use keys that look identical, and they answer opposite questions. The host key answers "which machine is this?". Your own user key answers "which person is this?". Confusing those two is the single most common source of SSH confusion.

In TLS this role is played by the server's certificate. But a certificate comes with a vouching signature from someone else. A host key comes with nothing at all. It is naked. Everything in the rest of this lab is a consequence of that one difference.

How do you compare a key by eye?

You cannot. A public key is 68 characters of base64, and nobody compares that correctly by eye at 2am. So SSH prints a short nickname instead, and calls it a fingerprint: the SHA-256 hash of the public key blob, base64-encoded, 43 characters long. Here is one, and it is the badge of the server used all through this lab.

SHA256:xhd1+pkCJTaolmvmKbOu7AEklrDjGapafL4tD5XUav0

The crucial property is that a fingerprint is not a separate identity. It is computed from nothing but the key itself. Comparing fingerprints is therefore exactly as strong as comparing keys, which is why every human procedure in SSH is written in terms of fingerprints. Experiment 1 proves it by building a key in Java, writing it out, and asking the real ssh-keygen binary what it thinks:

  ours          SHA256:vtqVBIOAz5W7fEY6IG5XgFLoRyXoTpZbtqGy348sVgo
  ssh-keygen    SHA256:vtqVBIOAz5W7fEY6IG5XgFLoRyXoTpZbtqGy348sVgo
  MATCH: true

Where does Alice keep what she remembers?

In one small address book on her own desk. Engineers call it known_hosts, and it lives at ~/.ssh/known_hosts on your laptop. One line per host: the host name and port, the key type, and the full public key. Here is one real line, written by scenario 1 of ssh-real.sh:

[127.0.0.1]:2222 ssh-ed25519 AAAAC3NzaC1lZDI1NTE5AAAAIApxgo5ltUSdqOJIG8HCQcERm93FRWOPKsI2CpDkwhWA

The term exists because that file is the entire trust policy of SSH. There is nothing else. No list of authorities, no online check, no second opinion. When people say "SSH is decentralised", this file is what they mean.

It plays the role that TLS's trust store played for Nikhil, with one enormous difference. A trust store is filled in advance, by someone else, with authorities. This address book is filled by you, as you go, with individual machines you have actually spoken to.

What happens the very first time?

Nothing protects it. That is the honest answer, and the term for it is trust on first use, usually shortened to TOFU. In plain words it says: I have no idea who you are, so I will assume you are who you claim, write it down, and hold you to it forever.

Technically it is the policy of accepting an unknown host key without checking anything, recording it, and refusing any future key that differs. The name is deliberately honest about where the protection starts. It starts at the second use. The first use is unprotected, and no amount of cryptography changes that.

This is precisely the problem certificate authorities exist to solve, and precisely the complexity SSH refuses to take on by default. Experiment 4 runs the same three connections twice. Once where the first connection was honest, once where Omar was already on the wire. The second run is the one worth staring at, because SSH behaves identically in both.

Who is on Arjun's guest list?

A file on his side of the wire, called authorized_keys. It lives at ~/.ssh/authorized_keys in the target user's home directory on the server. One public key per line. Being in this file is what "having access" means.

The name marks the direction, and the direction is what people get wrong. Your address book is on your laptop and lists servers. The guest list is on the server and lists people. They are mirror images and they are constantly confused.

Get it wrong and you see Permission denied (publickey). So does a completely different mistake, made entirely on your own laptop, when your private key file has permissions other users could read. Scenarios 5 and 6 of ssh-real.sh produce both messages, and section 11 shows how to tell them apart in under a minute.

What makes Alice's proof useless anywhere else?

A serial number for this one conversation. Engineers call it the session identifier, and it is the exchange hash H from the first key exchange: a SHA-256 over both sides' public values and the shared secret. It is unique per connection, because the throwaway keys are fresh every time.

The term exists because of what the number is used for. When Alice proves who she is, she signs the session identifier along with her username. That binds her proof to this one connection. A hostile warehouse cannot take her signature and walk into a different warehouse with it. Experiment 5 removes the binding and runs the replay:

--- design v1: sign only 'I am ravi, let me in' ---
  prod accepts the replayed signature? true   <-- she is in
--- design v2: sign the session id too (this is what SSH does) ---
  prod accepts the replayed signature? false  <-- refused
  a genuine login to prod still works?  true

8. What two pictures should you keep in your head?

In one breath: The simple picture is recognising a voice on the phone. The engineering picture is three layers stacked in one connection, each refusing to start until the one below it worked. The story lies to you in four places.

What does recognising a voice on the phone get you?

You ring a colleague's desk phone. Someone picks up. You have never met them in person, but you have spoken to them a dozen times, and you recognise the voice. No third party told you it was them. Your own ear did, out of your own memory.

Then, because the request is sensitive, they ask you a question only you could answer. And they ask a different question every time, so a recording of last week's call is worthless to anyone who kept one.

Those two moves are the whole protocol. Recognising the voice is the host key check. The different question every time is the session identifier inside your signature.

What does the engineering picture look like?

🔮 Predict: Omar records one whole SSH session of Alice's, byte for byte, and replays every packet at Arjun's server tomorrow. Does he get a shell?

Reveal

No, and the reason is in the second table. Every connection starts with a fresh key exchange, so tomorrow's session identifier is a different number. Alice's recorded signature covers yesterday's number and verifies against nothing today. If you said "yes, it is the same bytes", you are picturing a password, which is replayable. That is one of the reasons SSH prefers keys.

Where does the phone story lie to you?

Four places, and each one matters.

A voice can be imitated; a key cannot be guessed. Key comparison is exact and binary. Forty-three characters either match or they do not. There is no "sounds about right", and no bad line, and no cold. This makes SSH stronger than the analogy suggests.

You cannot phone the front desk to check a voice, but you can check a fingerprint out of band. Alice can read the fingerprint off the server build ticket, or the cloud console, or a colleague's screen. This is the one move that closes the hole in trust on first use, and the phone story hides it completely.

Recognising a voice is passive; the address book is a file you can edit. You can delete the entry, which throws the protection away. That is exactly what people do when the alarm fires, and scenario 4 of ssh-real.sh walks through it.

The phone call has one conversation; an SSH connection carries many. The single connection multiplexes independent channels: your shell, a file copy, and three port forwards at once, all under one handshake.

INTERMEDIATE MODEL

10. What does the handshake actually do, message by message?

In one breath: Both sides say hello, agree algorithms, throw one public value each across the wire, and derive a shared secret. Then the server signs a summary with its permanent badge, the client checks that badge against its address book, and only then does anyone mention a username.

Here is the whole handshake. Read it once for shape. The three subsections after it explain why three of these steps are not what you would have designed.

sequenceDiagram
    participant C as Your laptop
    participant S as prod-db.example.com
    Note over C,S: nothing is secret or verified yet
    C->>S: TCP connect, then "SSH-2.0-OpenSSH_9.8"
    S->>C: "SSH-2.0-OpenSSH_9.8"
    C->>S: which ciphers, key exchanges, key types I support
    S->>C: same, and we pick the best common one
    C->>S: Q_C — a throwaway public value, made for this connection only
    S->>C: Q_S, its host public key K_S, and sig(H) using the host private key
    Note over C: K = agree(my private, Q_S) · H = hash(Q_C, Q_S, K)
    Note over C: DECISION: is K_S the key known_hosts remembers?
    Note over C,S: session id := H · six keys derived · encryption on
    C->>S: I am "ravi", here is my public key — may I try it?
    S->>C: that key is in authorized_keys, go ahead
    C->>S: sign(session id ‖ "ravi" ‖ my public key ‖ …)
    S->>C: signature valid — you are in
    C->>S: open a channel: "session" → pty → shell

One more surprise. Six keys come out of that one secret, not one.

key 'C'  9b4165ffd0cd85aa378c7dca…   (encryption key, client to server)
key 'D'  51b24ebb3904b67b52004e9d…   (encryption key, server to client)
key 'E'  95288e9101b9bc5480889e46…   (integrity key, client to server)
key 'F'  4eda4ac3e8b9e41c928f1602…   (integrity key, server to client)

Each direction gets its own encryption key and its own integrity key. So a key captured for one purpose is useless for the others. Alice's outbound orders and Arjun's inbound replies are locked with different locks.

Refinement 1 — why does the host key only sign, and never encrypt?

The version you would write first is simpler and looks obviously correct. The server has a long-lived key pair. The client generates a random session key, encrypts it with the server's public key, and sends it. One round trip, and only the server can decrypt it. This was genuinely how SSH-1 worked.

🔮 Predict: Eve records this encrypted traffic today and cannot read it. A year later the server is decommissioned badly and its private host key leaks. What can she read now?

Reveal

Everything. Every session she ever recorded, going back years. The session key was encrypted to the host key, so the host key unlocks it — and the host key is the one key on the machine that never changes. If you said "only sessions from after the leak", you are thinking of a key that rotates. This one does not. Experiment 6 does this for real and prints the recovered command.

=== one year later: the server is compromised and its private host
    key is stolen. Eve opens her archive. ===
design A: unwraps the session key 13ed4c52a0bf7d537aa3084f...
          and reads: "psql -h prod -c 'select * from payments limit 1'"
design B: the host key never touched the session key. The two
          ephemeral private halves were discarded at the end of the
          connection and exist nowhere. Eve holds 76 bytes of noise.

So SSH does it the other way. The session key is agreed by a fresh, throwaway key exchange. The host key is used only to sign the transcript. It proves identity and unlocks nothing. That is why Arjun's signet ring can sit in the warehouse for a decade without becoming a liability for last year's letters. You met this property as forward secrecy in the TLS lab. The mechanism here is identical.

Refinement 2 — why does your signature cover the session identifier?

Again the simple version first. Alice signs "I am ravi, here is my public key". The server checks the signature against its guest list. Clean.

🔮 Predict: Alice logs into a shared build box that Ben administers. What can that box's root user do with the signature she just sent it?

Reveal

Take it, connect to the production database server, and present her signature as their own. It is a valid signature by Alice's key over the words "I am ravi" — and those words are true no matter which server reads them. If you said "nothing, the key never moved", you are right that the key never moved, and it did not need to. The proof moved.

So the signed blob starts with the session identifier. That value came out of this connection's key exchange, and an attacker cannot reproduce it on a different connection. Experiment 5, quoted back in section 7, runs both designs. The replay succeeds against v1 and fails against v2, while an honest login still works.

This is why it is safe to ssh from your laptop into a machine you do not fully trust. The machine learns nothing it can reuse.

Refinement 3 — why is there a prompt at all?

The third simple version is the one that feels airtight. The server signs the transcript, the client checks the signature, done. It is cryptographically valid.

Experiment 3 shows what that buys you:

  So Mallory signs with a host key of her OWN. That signature is
  mathematically valid:
    verify(Mallory host key, H_as_client_saw_it) = true   <- valid!

The problem is not the signature. It is that no signature can tell you which key was supposed to sign. Something outside the connection has to answer that question. TLS's answer was a certificate authority you trusted in advance. SSH's default answer is your own address book — and on the very first connection, that file is empty, so it asks you.

Trust on first use therefore protects connection two onwards. Connection one is protected only if a human compares the fingerprint against something that did not come down the wire. Section 15 shows how production environments remove the prompt entirely.

ENGINEERING MODEL

11. What are the eight ways it fails, and how do you tell them apart?

In one breath: Three failures are about Arjun's badge, two produce the identical error message from opposite ends of the wire, and three are about Alice's own key leaking. Two of the eight are silent, and those are the dangerous ones.

You can stage every one of these. ./ssh-real.sh runs them against a real sshd, and the visualization lets you watch the first three. Asha is on call this week, so each story is told as what lands on her screen.

F1 — what if the badge is not the one Alice remembers?

Scenario 3 of ssh-real.sh restarts the server with a different host key. Asha connects and gets the loudest banner in all of SSH: REMOTE HOST IDENTIFICATION HAS CHANGED.

What happened underneath is one comparison failing. The key the server offered is not the key in known_hosts. Three quite different things produce that. Somebody is intercepting. Or the server was rebuilt. Or Asha is hitting a different machine behind the same load-balanced name.

The client aborts immediately, before sending her username or offering her key. Her credentials are untouched; nothing usable reached the other end. What she loses is availability. She cannot log in until a human decides what happened.

In real life this is overwhelmingly a rebuild, which is exactly why the alarm gets ignored. Meera rebuilt that box on Tuesday. The correct move is still to fetch the expected fingerprint from somewhere that is not the wire, and compare it before touching known_hosts. Three places usually have it: the cloud console's instance screen, the build pipeline log, and a colleague who already knows.

F2 — does the alarm mean Asha is under attack?

Set the visualization's wire to the real server and the memory to the attacker's key. Identical banner, opposite cause.

This is the one to think about slowly. The alarm says the key changed. It does not and cannot say that anyone is attacking. Asha, reasoning "the warning appeared, so I am being attacked", has the logic backwards. Her colleague, reasoning "it is always a rebuild", will eventually be wrong once. Both are guessing where the protocol handed them a question.

F3 — what if the very first connection was the intercepted one?

In the visualization, set someone intercepting plus nothing remembered plus type yes blindly. Alice is now fully compromised and her screen is perfectly clean.

There is no response to give, because SSH performed correctly. It had nothing to check against. What was lost is everything, silently.

And it gets worse in a way that catches people. Alice's laptop has now pinned Omar's key. When Omar goes away, the honest server looks wrong, and she gets the F1 banner about the machine that was never the problem. This is precisely why SSH certificates exist, and section 15 is about them.

🔮 Predict: The next two failures produce the identical client message: Permission denied (publickey). One is caused entirely on your laptop, the other entirely on the server. Before reading on — what single piece of evidence would let you tell them apart in under a minute?

Reveal

A key fingerprint, seen in two places. Run ssh-keygen -lf ~/.ssh/id_ed25519.pub to get yours, then look for it in the server's log. If it appears there, your key travelled and the server rejected it → authorized_keys. If it does not, your client never sent it → a local problem, usually permissions. With no server access, ssh -v and the line Offering public key answers the same question from your side. If you said "read the error text", the error text is byte-identical in both cases; that is the whole trap.

F4 — why does Permission denied sometimes mean Alice's own laptop?

Scenario 5 of ssh-real.sh runs chmod 644 on the private key file. Here is what Asha sees:

Permissions 0644 for '…/id_ravi' are too open.
This private key will be ignored.
ravi@127.0.0.1: Permission denied (publickey,keyboard-interactive).

The client is refusing to use a key that other users on your own machine could read. It never offered it. So the server never saw a key at all and answered the only way it could.

The trap is in the last line. It blames authentication, and people go and edit authorized_keys on the server. The cause is three lines higher, on the laptop. The server was never involved.

There is a twin of this bug on the server side, called StrictModes. sshd refuses to read authorized_keys when the home directory or the .ssh folder is group-writable, and it logs that it did. Same class of mistake, other end of the wire.

F5 — and why does the same message sometimes mean Arjun's guest list?

Scenario 6 empties authorized_keys. The client message is identical to F4, word for word. The difference lives in the server log:

Failed publickey for ravi from 127.0.0.1 port 59417 ssh2: ED25519 SHA256:vAX2lrlLwqgjAWsFb/TmngpKvc8eh8hpBUannuOmD/U

Compare that fingerprint with ssh-keygen -lf ~/.ssh/id_ed25519.pub. If it appears in the server log at all, the key reached the server and the server said no. The problem is authorized_keys. If nothing appears, the key never left the laptop, and the problem is local. Without access to the server log, run ssh -v and look for Offering public key.

That one diagnostic separates the two most common SSH failures on earth, and it takes about forty seconds.

F6 — what does a stolen private key cost?

Copy id_ravi to another machine and use it. It works instantly. A private key with no passphrase is a bearer token: whoever holds it is Alice.

There is no response, because there is nothing to detect. The login is genuine by every measure SSH has. Nothing survives.

This is why passphrases exist, and why they are made bearable by ssh-agent. An agent is a process that holds your unlocked key in memory and signs on request, so you type the passphrase once per day rather than once per connection. It is also why hardware-backed keys (ed25519-sk, Secure Enclave) are strictly better, since the key physically cannot be copied. And it is the strongest argument for short-lived certificates over long-lived keys.

F7 — what does Alice hand over with ssh -A?

She runs ssh -A into Ben's shared build box, because it is convenient. That flag exposes a unix socket on Ben's box that signs anything on request, for as long as her session lasts.

🔮 Predict: Alice's private key never leaves her laptop during that session. So what exactly does the box's root user get?

Reveal

Everything the key grants, for the duration. The agent's whole protocol is "sign this blob", and it has no way to ask what the blob is for. Root on that box can ask it to sign a login to production, and it will. If you said "nothing, the key stayed home", you are protecting the wrong asset. The key is intact. The authority was lent out.

The fix is ProxyJump, written ssh -J bastion target. A bastion, or jump host, is the one machine reachable from outside, through which all access to a private network is funnelled. ProxyJump tunnels a new, separate connection through it. The bastion carries Alice's encrypted traffic without ever being able to use her key.

F8 — what leaks even though everything is encrypted?

Experiment 7 answers this, and the answer surprises people. Ciphertext lengths vary with command length. Interactive shells send one packet per keystroke.

    "ls"                                   -> ciphertext  30 bytes
    "sudo systemctl restart payments-api"  -> ciphertext  63 bytes

Eve, who never speaks and only keeps copies, learns the shape of everything Alice types. Command lengths, password lengths, and typing rhythm all survive encryption. Song, Wagner and Tian published keystroke-timing attacks on exactly this at USENIX in 2001.

The content itself is safe. It is the metadata that leaks. In production this is a real reason to prefer key authentication over typing passwords into an interactive session, where each keystroke becomes its own packet.

12. What does SSH actually promise Alice, and what does it not?

In one breath: If her address book holds the right key, everything after the handshake is private, unforgeable and useless to replay. SSH promises nothing at all about whether that key was right in the first place.

Every guarantee below sits on one precondition: known_hosts holds the right key for this server. Read that sentence twice. It is the hinge of the whole lab.

What is guaranteed, once the address book is right?

Guarantee In Alice's words What it means for your code
Privacy and integrity "nobody reads or edits my orders" everything after the handshake is encrypted, and modified packets are detected rather than silently mangled
Peer identity "the warehouse holds the ring I remember" the peer holds the private half of the host key you already trusted
Forward secrecy "Eve's old notebook stays gibberish" today's stolen host key does not decrypt yesterday's recorded session
Non-replayable proof "my signed note works in one warehouse only" your authentication signature is worthless on any other connection
Nothing sent too early "she gives her name last, not first" username, key and signature are all withheld until the machine is identified

What is explicitly not guaranteed?

The first row is the important one. Everything else in SSH is arithmetic. This one is trust, and trust is a human decision that happened once, possibly years ago, in about one second.

Not promised Why it bites Where you saw it
That the key in known_hosts is the right one the entire chain rests on it, and by default it rests on somebody typing yes section 7, and F3
Anything about the first connection unless a human compared the fingerprint against something off the wire F3
Metadata privacy sizes and timings leak, whatever the cipher F8
Safety against a compromised server if the machine is owned, SSH delivered you correctly to a hostile host refinement 2 keeps your key; your session is gone
That an alarm means an attack F1 and F2 produce the same screen from opposite causes F1, F2

🔮 Predict: Alice logs into a production box that has already been compromised. Which of the five guarantees above still held?

Reveal

All five. That is the uncomfortable part. SSH promised to deliver her to the machine whose key she remembered, privately and unforgeably, and it did exactly that. The machine itself being hostile was never on the list. If you said "none of them", you are asking SSH to judge the far end's behaviour. It only judges the far end's identity.

What was traded away to get this?

Two things, and both are about size rather than cryptography.

Scale. With TLS you trust about 150 authorities and can then check millions of servers. With SSH you must acquire trust in every individual machine, one at a time. For Alice's three servers this is genuinely simpler than TLS. For three thousand ephemeral containers it collapses, which is what section 15 is about.

Revocation. Removing a line from authorized_keys on 400 machines is 400 operations. Nothing tells you when one of them was missed.

Those three keys are the whole cast of scenarios 1 to 6. Arjun's warehouse is the first, Omar's shed is the second, and Alice's own key is the third. The impostor key is what scenario 3 restarts the server with, and it is why F1 fires.

Then the crypto itself. Six files, and none of them is long.

Wire.java        SSH's on-the-wire encoding, key blobs, fingerprints, signing
Kex.java         key exchange, the exchange hash, key derivation
Crypto.java      AES-GCM seal/open — the record layer, in two methods
KnownHosts.java  the entire trust policy, in about ten lines
Auth.java        the blob a client signs to prove who it is
SshLab.java      the seven experiments

The load-bearing lines, in order of importance.

Auth.blob is the one field that stops replay. This single line is refinement 2, in code:

byte[] head = sessionId == null ? new byte[0] : Wire.string(sessionId);

Passing null builds the broken v1 design. Everything else in the method is bookkeeping. This line is the security property.

Kex.exchangeHash is why Omar cannot hide. Both public halves go into the hash:

return Wire.sha256(Wire.cat(Wire.string(qc), Wire.string(qs), Wire.string(k)));

Omar must present his own public half to Alice. So the number Alice computes differs from the number Arjun computes, and Arjun's signature cannot verify against Alice's version.

KnownHosts.check is the whole trust decision. Three branches: unknown (ask a human, check nothing), match (proceed silently), mismatch (refuse). There is no fourth branch, and there is no third party anywhere in the file. Ten lines hold everything TLS spends a certificate chain on.

Kex.ephemeral is forward secrecy in one word. A fresh key pair per connection, discarded at the end. If these were long-lived, experiment 6's design B would fail exactly like design A.

In one breath: The cryptography does not change at all. What changes is where trust comes from. Priya cannot remember three thousand machines, so she stops remembering and starts vouching, with an authority she runs herself.

What replaces trust on first use?

Trust on first use does not survive autoscaling. Priya's servers are created and destroyed hourly and named by IP address. So every connection is a first connection. Every engineer is trained to type yes reflexively. The protection is gone in practice while looking present on paper.

The fix is to bring back the idea you already know from the TLS lab: delegate trust in advance. The difference is that the authority is one Priya runs herself, not one of the 150 that shipped with the laptop.

A user certificate also carries principals, which are the usernames it may log in as. It carries a validity window. And it carries extensions saying whether port forwarding or agent forwarding are permitted at all. That is policy authorized_keys cannot express well.

Which settings actually change your day?

Almost everything painful about SSH is fixed by ~/.ssh/config, and most engineers never write one. Here is a config worth stealing:

Host prod-*
  User deploy
  IdentityFile ~/.ssh/id_ed25519_prod
  IdentitiesOnly yes          # stop offering every key you own to every server
  ProxyJump bastion.example.com
  ControlMaster auto          # reuse one connection for many sessions
  ControlPath ~/.ssh/cm-%r@%h:%p
  ControlPersist 10m

ControlMaster matters more than it looks. It removes the handshake from every subsequent connection to the same host, and you already know from the networking lab that the handshake is the expensive part.

Hardening comes next, in rough order of value. PasswordAuthentication no removes brute-forcing entirely and is worth more than everything below it. Then PermitRootLogin no, then AllowGroups ssh-users, then MaxAuthTries 3. Finally, for automation accounts that must never get a shell, forced commands in authorized_keys:

command="/usr/local/bin/backup-only",no-port-forwarding,no-agent-forwarding ssh-ed25519 …

Nothing. It asked the network "what key should I trust?" and wrote down the answer. That is the F3 weakness, automated. It runs fresh on every single build, so it never even gets the second-connection protection. Whoever controls the pipeline's network path controls the answer. If you said "it pins the key", it pins whatever answered this minute. The fix is to commit the known-good fingerprint into the pipeline config, where a change to it is a reviewable diff.

That trap is worth naming out loud, because it is everywhere. ssh-keyscan is not a check. It asks the network what to trust, which is the thing trust on first use was already weak about, now automated and repeated. Pin the published fingerprint in your pipeline config instead.

SSH — Solutions

Try the exercises first. Rungs 7–8 are worked discussions, not single answers.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Encryption puts your message in a locked box that only the person you exchanged keys with can open. It says nothing about who that person is. If a stranger intercepted the setup and did the key exchange himself, you now have a beautifully locked box that only he can open — and it looks exactly like success. The hard problem is not hiding the message; it is knowing who is at the other end.

1.2 On the first connection your laptop has never seen this server, so it has nothing to compare against and no third party to ask. It shows you a fingerprint and asks you to decide. From the second connection onwards it has a recorded answer, so it can check automatically and refuse anything different. The protection therefore begins at connection two.

1.3

known_hosts authorized_keys
lives on your laptop (client) the server, in the target user's home
lists machines and their host keys people and their public keys
answers "am I talking to the right machine?" "is this person allowed in as this user?"

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 (a) printed at the top: server host key fingerprint : SHA256:…. (b) the single line [127.0.0.1]:2222 ssh-ed25519 AAAAC3Nza…. (c) the script runs ssh-keygen -lf lab/known_hosts, which prints the same SHA256:… value — the fingerprint is computed from the key, so matching fingerprints means matching keys.

2.2 It dies at step 5, the host key check — before authentication begins. At that point the client has sent only the SSH version string and its throwaway public value Q_C. It has not sent your username, offered your public key, or signed anything. The attacker gains nothing reusable.

2.3 Separate keys per direction and per purpose limit what one compromised key can do. If both directions shared one key, an attacker who obtained it could not merely read — they could forge messages as the server to the client. Separating encryption from integrity keys means a flaw that exposes one does not automatically grant the other. This is a standard principle: one key, one job.

2.4 Changing every run: the generated key fingerprints, Q_C, Q_S, K, all derived keys, session ids, and all ciphertexts. Stable: the structure — blob length 51 bytes, key lengths 32 bytes, the six derived key roles, and the verdicts (true/false) of every check. Per-connection values are the ephemeral ones; a real server's host key would be the stable one, but this lab generates a fresh one each run, so nothing here is per-machine. That is worth noticing: it is the difference between a lab and a real deployment.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 No, no, and no. Host key verification happens in the transport layer, before the authentication layer starts. The client aborts with Host key verification failed having sent only the version string and its ephemeral public value. This is the strongest single property in SSH's design: identity of the machine is settled before anything about the user is disclosed.

3.2 You get the full REMOTE HOST IDENTIFICATION HAS CHANGED banner — pointing at the genuine server. Your laptop pinned the attacker's key as the truth, so the honest machine is now the one that looks wrong. Experiment 4 story B prints exactly this. The lesson: TOFU is faithful to whatever you told it first, and it cannot distinguish a lie recorded early from a change later.

3.3 In real SSH: no. Your signature covers this connection's session identifier, which the operator cannot reproduce on a connection to prod.example.com. In the version without it: yes — the signature is over "I am ravi", which is equally true anywhere, so it replays. Experiment 5 shows true then false.

3.4 With host-key encryption of the session key: she reads everything, retroactively, for as long as she has been recording. With real SSH: nothing new — the session keys came from ephemeral values that were destroyed at logout, and the host key was only ever used to sign. She can impersonate the server in future connections, which is serious, but the archive stays shut.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 The honest user still logs in perfectly — so liveness is unaffected. What is lost is safety: the replay now succeeds, so a signature captured by any server you touch is usable against any other server that trusts your key. Note the shape of this: the system looks completely healthy in testing. Only an adversary notices the difference, which is why this class of bug survives code review.

4.2 The interceptor still fails. Measured over 200 simulated attempts:

  H = hash(Q_C, Q_S, K)  -> attacker survived 0 times
  H = hash(Q_C, K)       -> attacker survived 0 times
  H = hash(K)            -> attacker survived 0 times

The reason is K. An interceptor must run two separate key exchanges, so the secret she shares with you is necessarily different from the one she shares with the server. Any hash that includes K therefore differs at the two ends, and the real server's signature cannot verify against the client's hash. The public values are not what catches her.

So what are the other components for? Downgrade protection. The real exchange hash also covers both sides' version strings and their full KEXINIT algorithm lists. Without that, an interceptor who cannot break the exchange could still edit the negotiation on the way past — stripping strong ciphers from the list so both ends settle on the weakest option they both support, then attacking that. Hashing the negotiation means any such edit changes H, and the signature fails. The signature protects the terms of the conversation, not only its participants.

4.3 The fingerprint-comparison version is correct. ssh-keygen -R deletes your only evidence and re-runs TOFU, which is exactly the state an attacker wants you in. It is rarely what happens because the alarm is nearly always a legitimate rebuild, so the reflex is reinforced dozens of times before the one occasion it matters — and because obtaining the true fingerprint requires leaving the terminal and finding an authoritative source, which is slow at the precise moment you are blocked. That asymmetry, not ignorance, is why the protection fails in practice — and it is the argument for host certificates (stage 15), which remove the alarm from routine rebuilds entirely.

4.4 The message becomes Permission denied (publickey,password,keyboard-interactive). That parenthesised list is the set of authentication methods the server is willing to accept — it is a useful diagnostic in itself. If publickey is absent from it, the server has disabled key authentication and no amount of fixing your key will help.

Rung 5 — Implement

5.1 Add a revoked set checked before the match branch:

if (revoked.contains(fp))
    return "!! this host key has been REVOKED. Do not connect. !!";

It matters operationally because the two states demand opposite responses. A mismatch means "something changed, go and find out what" — often benign. A revoked key means "this key is known to be compromised; a server presenting it is either the attacker or still running the bad key" — never benign, and never to be resolved by deleting a line. OpenSSH supports this as the @revoked marker in known_hosts and, for certificates, RevokedKeys.

5.2 Split on spaces, base64-decode field 2, then walk the blob: read a 4-byte length, read that many bytes ("ssh-ed25519"), then the same again for the 32 raw key bytes, and hand those to Wire.parseEd25519. The test that it is right is that your fingerprint matches ssh-keygen -lf on a key file the real ssh-keygen produced — the same cross-check experiment 1 uses in the other direction.

5.3 The session identifier must stay fixed at the first H because it is what your authentication signature was bound to. If it changed on rekey, either every existing signature would become invalid, or — far worse — the binding would become re-negotiable, which reopens the replay hole from experiment 5. RFC 4253 is explicit: the session identifier is set once, by the first exchange, and never changes for the life of the connection. Encryption keys, by contrast, are replaced every rekey; that is the entire point.

Rung 6 — Debug

6.1 Check 2 fails: a stranger who generated a key five seconds ago is admitted as ravi. What she had to possess: nothing. No stolen key, no intercepted traffic — just the ability to make a key pair, which anyone can do.

The line is:

return Wire.verify(offeredKey, expected, signature);

It answers "did the holder of this key sign this message?" — and the answer is honestly yes, because she signed it with the key she just made. The question it should answer is "is this key allowed in?". authorizedKeys is consulted only via containsKey(user), which checks the username exists, never the key.

Using experiment 3's sentence: the signature is fine; the key is wrong. The code verifies possession flawlessly and never checks authorization — exactly the man-in-the-middle failure, moved from the transport layer to the auth layer.

The fix:

PublicKey allowed = authorizedKeys.get(user);
if (allowed == null || !allowed.equals(offeredKey)) return false;

On a real server the data structure you must consult is ~/.ssh/authorized_keys in the target user's home directory.

Would the session id have helped? No — and this is the point of the question. The session identifier stops a valid proof being reused on a different connection. It says nothing about whether the key behind that proof was ever authorized. They are two independent checks: is this proof genuine and for this connection (signature + session id) and is this key permitted (authorized_keys). This code does the first perfectly and skips the second entirely.

6.2 (i) Where did you get the expected fingerprint for the rebuilt box, and did it come from somewhere other than the SSH connection itself? (ii) Did everyone else get the same new fingerprint you did — or did some colleagues see a different one? (iii) Was a rebuild actually scheduled at that time, and does the build log show a new host key being generated? Any "no" or "don't know" means it was not verified, only silenced.

6.3 StrictHostKeyChecking=no accepts unknown host keys silently and allows connections to hosts whose key has changed. That second half is the dangerous one, and it is current behaviour, not a legacy quirk — ssh_config(5) on this machine says of no: "ssh will automatically add new host keys to the user known hosts files and allow connections to hosts with changed hostkeys to proceed, subject to some restrictions." It gives up host verification completely — the CI job will happily talk to anyone who can answer on that address. Do instead: commit the expected host key into the repo and point UserKnownHostsFile at it, so the pipeline verifies against a reviewed constant. If you want the "unknown is fine, changed is fatal" behaviour, accept-new is the correct value — never no.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 Deploy an SSH host certificate authority. Each server, at build time, gets its freshly generated host key signed by your CA, producing a host certificate naming it. Every laptop's known_hosts gets one line:

@cert-authority *.prod.example.com ssh-ed25519 AAAA…   # the CA's public key

What is trusted changes from "this specific key, per machine, recorded by me at some point" to "any host key the CA has vouched for". Rebuilds then verify silently and correctly on the first connection. Crucially the protection is not weakened but strengthened: today an engineer approves an unverified key twice a month; afterwards a prompt never appears at all, so if one ever does, it is real. The new dependency is the CA private key, which becomes the most sensitive secret you own and belongs in an HSM or a hardware token.

7.2 Compromised: everything that key could reach — 300 servers, with whatever privileges the account holds on each, and any further access reachable from those hosts. Assume it is being used now.

First hour: remove the key from the central source that generates authorized_keys (configuration management, not by hand); push that removal to all 300 hosts and record which succeeded; disable the engineer's account in the identity provider; check auth.log across the fleet for Accepted publickey lines carrying that fingerprint since the theft; look for newly added authorized_keys entries, cron jobs and systemd units on any host that shows one.

Full remediation: days. The hard part is not the removal, it is proving completeness — hosts that were down, hosts outside configuration management, keys copied into a container image, keys added to a service account by hand. There is no mechanism that tells you when you missed one.

The design where this is a non-event: short-lived user certificates. The engineer's key alone is useless without a valid certificate, and certificates expire in hours. Revoke at the CA, or simply disable the SSO account, and access ends fleet-wide within one certificate lifetime. No file on any host is edited.

Residual risk even then: any session already open stays open until killed (certificate expiry does not terminate a live connection); anything the attacker established during the valid window — an added authorized_keys entry, a reverse tunnel, a modified image — survives the certificate expiring. Short credentials shrink the window; they do not undo what happened inside it.

7.3 A forced command with every capability stripped:

command="/usr/local/bin/deploy",restrict ssh-ed25519 AAAA… ci@buildserver

command= means the server runs that program regardless of what the client asked for, so an interactive shell is impossible and the client's requested command is available only in $SSH_ORIGINAL_COMMAND for the script to inspect. restrict is the modern all-in option: it denies port forwarding, agent forwarding, X11 forwarding, PTY allocation and user rc execution, and is safer than listing no-port-forwarding,no-agent-forwarding,… by hand because it stays correct when OpenSSH adds new capabilities. Add from="10.0.5.0/24" to bind the key to your build network as well.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 Sketch of a defensible answer.

Replaces known_hosts: a host CA. Every instance, in its bootstrap, gets its host key signed by the CA with a short-lived host certificate. Laptops trust one @cert-authority line per domain. No prompts, ever; autoscaling stops mattering.

Replaces authorized_keys: user certificates carrying principals. sshd_config gets TrustedUserCAKeys and AuthorizedPrincipalsFile, mapping a principal such as payments-oncall to the local account it may assume. Access becomes group membership, not a file edit.

Issuance: an engineer authenticates to the identity provider (SSO + MFA), receives a certificate valid ~8 hours with principals derived from their current groups. Removal within 5 minutes is achieved by deactivating the account so no new certificates issue, plus a revocation list distributed to hosts (RevokedKeys) for the case where you cannot wait for expiry.

Automated systems: never long-lived keys checked into anything. Workloads obtain certificates from the same CA using their platform identity (instance identity document, Kubernetes service account, OIDC federation), with lifetimes in minutes, principals restricted to a purpose-built account, and forced commands as in 7.3.

Attribution: the certificate's key id carries the human's identity and is logged by sshd on every login, so a shared deploy account still produces per-person audit lines. Log centrally; alert on any Accepted publickey without a certificate (i.e. a raw key still working somewhere).

Blast radius: hosts do not hold credentials for other hosts — a compromised host yields no reusable key, because it never had one. Agent forwarding is denied by certificate extension, so a hostile host cannot borrow an engineer's identity; ProxyJump is used instead.

When the CA is down: nobody can obtain new certificates, but existing ones keep working until they expire — so the outage becomes visible gradually rather than instantly, which is the right failure shape. You still need a documented break-glass path: a small number of emergency keys in authorized_keys on critical hosts, held in a vault, alerting loudly on use. Design it before you need it, and test it on a schedule; an untested break-glass path is decoration.

8.2 Plain TOFU is correct when the number of hosts is small, stable, and long-lived, and the number of engineers is small enough that everyone can be told a fingerprint out of band — realistically, up to a few dozen hosts and a handful of people. Below that scale a CA is more moving parts than it saves, and the CA key becomes a new single point of catastrophe you now have to protect.

The signal that you have outgrown it is behavioural, not numerical: when engineers start clearing host-key warnings without investigating, the protection has already stopped working — the file still exists, the checks still run, and the humans they depend on have been trained out of the loop. Secondary signals: hosts outliving their host keys, ssh-keyscan appearing in scripts, or offboarding requiring an edit on more machines than one person can verify in an afternoon.

8.3 What gets better: first-contact security, without you running any infrastructure. Any server could be verified on the very first connection, so F3 disappears globally rather than per-organisation.

What gets worse: every internal machine would need a publicly-issued certificate, which means a public CA must be told the names of your private hosts, and names like db-04.internal cannot be issued publicly at all. Renewal becomes a fleet-wide operational dependency with a hard expiry — the classic PKI outage, now able to lock you out of the very machines you need in order to fix it. You would also inherit the entire public CA trust surface: roughly 150 organisations, any one of which mis-issuing gets you a valid certificate for your own production host.

Why SSH does not work this way: SSH's job is administrative access to machines you already own, usually named privately, often unreachable from the internet. HTTPS's job is a stranger's browser reaching a public name it has never seen. Public CAs solve the second problem, and SSH does not have it — so it declines the dependency and keeps trust local, at the cost of the first-connection hole. The organisations for whom that hole matters run their own CA, which is the same design with the trust anchor moved in-house.
