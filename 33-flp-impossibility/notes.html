Lab 33 · Why Distribution Is Hard

FLP Impossibility — why no protocol can promise to finish

Tell a friend: Three lighthouse keepers, Ada, Bo and Cy, must tonight either all light their beacons or all leave them dark, because a half-lit coast wrecks ships. They can only write letters, Omar the ferryman decides how long each crossing takes, nobody owns a clock, and any one keeper might quietly fall ill. In 1985 three researchers proved that no rulebook can survive a world like that. Nothing which always reacts the same way can promise everybody agrees, and that the answer is one somebody wanted, and that the thing ever finishes. Asha's payments cluster is those three islands with better lighting. That is why nobody can tell her an upper bound on how long her next leader election will take.

BEGINNER MODEL — stages 1 to 9

1. Why does this lab exist at all?

In one breath: Asha's five machines have to agree on one thing and then actually get on with it. This lab is about the second half of that sentence, which nobody ever promises her.

Asha runs a payments service across five machines. They have to agree on one thing: is this transfer committed, or not? One answer, the same on every machine. And the machines have to actually reach it. Not sit there thinking about it while the customer refreshes the page.

Asha has already built systems that do this. She has a Raft cluster. It works. So here is the question nobody asked in those labs, the one her colleague Meera asks over coffee:

Is there a protocol that always finishes?

Not "usually finishes". Not "finishes when the network is fine". Always, the way a sorting algorithm always terminates.

In 1985 three researchers proved the answer is no. The proof is so robust that thirty-odd years of hardware improvement has not dented it. It is not about slow networks. It is not about bad code. It holds on a perfect network that loses nothing, with fast machines, and with as many spare replicas as you like.

What goes wrong if Asha does not know this. She spends a career hunting a bug that is a theorem. Her cluster stalls for forty seconds during a leader election. She writes a postmortem calling it a defect. She tunes a timeout down. Next month it stalls again for a different reason, and she tunes another one.

Knowing this result tells her which knobs exist, which knobs are lies, and how to describe the risk honestly to people who need to plan around it.

2. Three lighthouses and a ferryman — what must they agree on?

In one breath: Three keepers on three islands must all light their beacons or all leave them dark. They can only write letters, and a ferryman who takes as long as he likes carries every one.

Three keepers live on three islands. Ada on North Rock. Bo on Middle Sands. Cy on South Cape. Tonight they must agree on one thing.

Light the beacons, or leave them dark.

Either is a defensible choice. What is not defensible is a half-lit coast. A ship that sees one beacon and not the others reads the gap as clear water, and puts itself on the rocks. So whatever they do, all three must do the same thing. And they must actually get around to doing it before dawn.

Here are the rules of their world. Every one of them matters later.

  1. They can only write letters. No island can see another. There is no shouting, no flag signal, no shared view of anything.
  2. A ferryman carries every letter, and never loses one. His name is Omar. Not one letter is ever destroyed, misdelivered, or forged. Every letter written is eventually handed over.
  3. The sea decides how long he takes. A crossing might be an hour. It might be three weeks. There is no upper limit, and nobody is told which it will be. Letters can also overtake each other, because Omar is not obliged to deliver in the order they were written.
  4. Nobody owns a working clock. No keeper can measure elapsed time in any way another keeper would agree with.
  5. Any one keeper might fall ill and simply stop. Mid-sentence, without warning, without a farewell note. At most one, though. The other two will definitely stay healthy.

Rule 5 is not pessimism. It is the whole reason a protocol is needed at all. If nobody could ever stop, "wait until everyone has spoken" would work fine.

🔮 Predict: One of those five rules is the one that makes this problem unsolvable. If you could repeal exactly one, which would you repeal?

Reveal Most people pick rule 5 — "a keeper might fall ill" — because failure feels like the enemy. It is the wrong answer. Repeal rule 5 and the problem is still hard, but repeal rule 3 — "the sea decides how long he takes" — and it becomes easy: with a known upper bound on crossing time, silence past that bound is proof of illness rather than a guess, and majority voting works exactly as stage 3 hopes. (Repealing rule 5 alone also works, for a duller reason: if nobody can ever stop, you simply wait for everyone.)

Hold on to this: the enemy is not failure. The enemy is the missing deadline. Everything real systems do about this — election timeouts, leases, heartbeat intervals — is an attempt to put rule 3 back, and stage 13 is about the bill.

3. What does any sensible person try first?

In one breath: Everyone tells everyone what they want, everyone counts, and the majority wins. It is obviously right, and it works perfectly, as long as everybody answers.

Ada writes to Bo and Cy: "I say dark. What do you say?" She waits for both replies, counts, and takes the majority. Bo and Cy do the same. Three keepers, three views, everyone counts the same votes, everyone lands on the same answer.

This is obviously right, and it obviously works. As long as everybody answers.

4. Why can Ada never tell "slow" from "dead"?

In one breath: An empty mailbox looks exactly the same whether Bo is dead or whether Bo's letter is still at sea. The only difference between those two worlds is how long the future takes, and the future has not happened yet.

Ada waits. Nothing comes from Bo.

She has exactly two explanations, and she cannot tell them apart.

  • Bo is alive. He wrote his reply immediately, and Omar's boat is stuck in a storm.
  • Bo is dead. No reply will ever exist.

Ada's evidence in both cases is identical: an empty mailbox. There is no letter she can write. There is no question she can ask. There is no instrument she can build that separates the two. The only difference between the two worlds is how long the future is going to take, and the future has not happened yet.

Open tab 1 of the visualization, "Slow, or dead?" Drag Ada's patience. The page shows both worlds and what Ada sees in each. You are looking for a slider setting that is right in both worlds. There isn't one.

So Ada must pick a deadline. And every deadline is a guess. Read the middle column as "what Ada does", and the right column as "what it costs her".

Ada's deadline What she does What it costs
Too short writes off a living keeper and carries on without him Bo, alive and well, is also carrying on. Two keepers now run the coast, and their letters cross in the post. This is precisely the split-brain lab, and it is where wrong answers come from.
Too long waits, and waits if Bo really is dead, Ada waits past dawn and the coast stays unlit. Nothing wrong happened, and nothing happened at all.
Infinite waits forever correct in the storm case, catastrophic in the dead case

🔮 Predict: Ada has more paper, more ink, five spare islands, and an unlimited budget. Can she buy her way out of this?

Reveal No. More islands mean more mailboxes that might be empty for either reason. The ambiguity is not caused by a shortage of anything — it is caused by the absence of an upper bound on delivery time. You cannot buy an upper bound with equipment; you can only assume one, and then live with being wrong when the assumption fails. That is exactly what real systems do, and stage 13 is about paying for it.

The naive solution didn't merely fail. It revealed that the thing it needed, "know who is dead", is not obtainable.

5. What exactly are we asking for?

In one breath: Everybody writes the same answer. The answer is one somebody actually wanted. And everybody healthy writes something. Those three together are called consensus.

Strip the story away for one moment. Each keeper starts with a preference, light or dark. Each must eventually write one final, unchangeable answer on a slate. Three properties, in plain words.

Property Plain words Why you'd want it
Agreement No two keepers write different answers. A half-lit coast wrecks ships.
Validity The answer is one somebody actually wanted, not invented. A protocol that always answers "dark" satisfies agreement and is useless.
Termination Every healthy keeper eventually writes something. An answer after dawn is not an answer.

Together these three are called consensus.

Agreement and Validity are safety properties. They are promises that nothing bad ever happens. Termination is a liveness property. It is a promise that something good eventually happens. You met that distinction in the paxos lab. It is about to become the most important distinction in this one.

And the failure model, stated precisely: at most one keeper may crash. Stop working permanently, at any moment, with no announcement. Not lie. Not forge letters. Not come back. Just stop.

This is the target. All three properties. Always. With one possible crash. With no clock.

And it is unachievable. That is the theorem.

🔮 Predict: Every real consensus system you have ever used ships without one of these three. Which one did they give up, and did they tell you?

Reveal They gave up Termination — and mostly they did tell you, in a sentence you skipped. etcd, ZooKeeper and Spanner all keep Agreement and Validity unconditionally, and offer finishing only while the network behaves. If you said Validity, you were thinking of systems that invent a default answer on timeout; those exist, but they are not consensus, they are giving up. If you said Agreement, you were thinking of eventual consistency, which is a different product with a different promise (your cap-theorem and pacelc labs). Stage 12.5 states the trade in one line.

6. Why is breaking one protocol worth nothing?

In one breath: Show that a protocol fails and somebody says "then use a better one". So the proof stops talking about protocols altogether and talks about situations instead.

Here is the trap a smart person falls into first. It is worth naming, because it is where most people's understanding stops.

Asha's colleague Ben tries it. The wrong move: find a protocol and break it. Show that majority voting fails. Show that Paxos can livelock. Show that Raft can split-vote. All three are true, and all three are worthless as an argument. For every broken protocol somebody will say "then use a better one", and they might be right. Breaking a thousand protocols says nothing about the thousand-and-first.

The right move: stop talking about protocols, and start talking about situations. Fischer, Lynch and Paterson's insight is to reason about the state of the whole world. Everything every keeper knows, plus every letter currently at sea. And then to ask one question of it:

From here, which final answers are still possible?

Some situations have both answers still live. Others have already been forced one way, even if nobody on the islands knows it yet. The entire proof is one observation about how these two kinds of situation relate:

From any situation where both answers are still possible, there is always a next event that leaves both answers still possible.

If that is true, Omar never has to let anyone finish. He steps from undecided situation to undecided situation forever. And he does it without losing a single letter and without ignoring a single keeper.

This works against any protocol, because the argument never mentions what the protocol does. That is what makes it a theorem rather than a bug report. Ben's objection is answered before he makes it.

7. Which words do you need, now that you have earned them?

In one breath: Nine words carry this lab. You have already felt all nine of them happen to Ada, Bo and Cy. Now they get their real names.

Each word below is a thing that has already happened in the story. Read the paragraph, then the tables at the end of the section, which map every word back to the moment you met it.

What does "asynchronous" actually rule out?

An asynchronous system is a world with no upper limit on how long anything takes. That is Omar exactly. A crossing may take an hour or three weeks, and no keeper is told which.

Said precisely: there is no bound on message delivery delay, no bound on the relative speed of processes, and no synchronised clocks.

The word exists to separate this world from a synchronous one, where known bounds exist. In a synchronous world, a timeout that expires is proof of a crash. In an asynchronous world, a timeout that expires is proof of nothing at all. That is the whole of stage 4 in one sentence.

Be careful about what this claims. It is not a claim that real networks are this bad. It is a claim about what you may safely assume. Asha's production network becomes asynchronous every time a garbage-collection pause, a saturated link, or a busy disk makes a bound she believed in stop holding.

Why does FLP assume the gentlest failure it can?

A crash failure is a machine stopping, forever, without warning. Bo dies in the night. Half a letter on his desk, and nothing more.

Said precisely: a process takes only finitely many steps in an infinite run.

The word exists to fence off much nastier failure models. A crashed process does not send wrong data. It does not forge messages. It does not come back with corrupted state. FLP assumes the mildest possible failure, and that is exactly what makes the impossibility so striking.

You have seen this in the wild. The gossip-protocols lab called the failure detector a fallible component, and ran a 100-node cluster where every node wrote off every other node with zero actual crashes. That was this problem, happening to real machines.

What is the smallest thing that can happen, and where does a timeout hide?

A step is the smallest thing that can happen: one keeper reads her mailbox once and reacts.

Said precisely, a step of process p is three things in a row. First, receive returns either a message m addressed to p or nothing. Second, p updates its state as a function of its old state and what it received. Third, p sends a finite set of messages. In symbols the first part is written receive(p). The pair (p, m), meaning this process receiving this message, is called an event.

Now the part that matters most. The "or nothing" branch is called a null step, and it is how a timeout lives inside the model. There is no clock in this world, so a timeout cannot be "when 5 seconds pass". It can only be "a step in which I looked, there was nothing there, and I decided to act anyway".

Timeouts are not outside the theorem. They are a case of it.

In the visualization, the button "Let Ada give up waiting" is a null step. Notice who decides when it happens: you, playing Omar. In a world with no clock, nothing else can. The raft lab's election timeout is exactly this, a null step that promotes a follower to candidate.

🔮 Predict: Asha argues that her election timeout is a real clock, so it sits outside this clockless model. Is she right?

Reveal No, and the reason is worth holding on to. Her timeout is a real clock reading, but the model does not care what triggered the step — it only cares that a process acted on having received nothing. Any rule of the form "if I have heard nothing, do X" is a null step, however you implement the waiting. If you said "yes, a clock puts her outside FLP", you have found the right idea in the wrong place: a clock helps only when it comes with a bound she can trust, and that is escape 1 in stage 12.4, not an exemption from the theorem.

What is the whole world called, and who is allowed to see it?

A configuration is a complete snapshot of the world. Every keeper's internal state, plus the multiset of every letter that has been sent and not yet delivered. The visualization calls it a situation.

Here is one, written out in full: Ada has promised attempt 4 and accepted (1, dark); Bo has promised 7 and accepted nothing; Cy is idle; four letters are at sea. That whole sentence is one configuration.

The word exists because the proof needs an object it can label. You cannot say "this protocol is undecided". You can say "this configuration is undecided".

And notice the cruelty in it. A configuration is a global object that no participant can ever see. Ada sees a projection of it. The theorem reasons about the whole thing.

When is Omar playing fair?

A schedule is the order Omar chooses: a sequence of events applicable one after another from a configuration. A run is that sequence actually executed.

A run is admissible when two things hold. At most one process is faulty. And every message sent to a non-faulty process is eventually delivered.

The word admissible exists to close the cheap objection. An adversary who destroys mail or ignores a keeper proves nothing, because of course that breaks things. Admissibility is the fairness contract. The FLP adversary honours it completely.

This is the sharpest line between FLP and cap-theorem. CAP's adversary takes the messages away. FLP's adversary delivers every one of them, and only chooses the order.

What would it take for Ada to be unpredictable?

Deterministic means the same machine in the same state, given the same input, always does the same thing. Ada, holding two agreements and a preference for dark, always proposes dark. She never sometimes proposes light.

Said precisely: each process's transition function is a function. No coin flips. No randomness. No reading the wall clock.

This word is load-bearing, and it is one of the four you are allowed to attack. A protocol that flips a coin is outside FLP's scope entirely.

Keep one warning in your pocket. The raft lab's randomised election timeouts look like an attack on this word. Stage 12.4 shows they are mostly not, and puts a number on it.

Which endings are still on the table? (bivalent and univalent)

This is the word the proof is built out of, so it gets the most room. The plain question it answers is: "which endings are still possible from here?"

A configuration is 0-valent if every reachable decision from it is 0. It is 1-valent if every reachable decision is 1. It is bivalent if both are still reachable. 0-valent and 1-valent together are called univalent, which means the ending is settled even if nobody knows it yet.

The word exists to separate two things that sound the same and are not:

  • "Nobody has decided yet."
  • "The outcome is not yet determined."

A configuration can have zero decisions written down and still be univalent. The outcome is already sealed, and the keepers simply have not caught up. Bivalent is the strictly stronger statement: the future is genuinely open.

Which promise can only ever be broken by a run that never ends?

You have safety and liveness from paxos. Safety means nothing bad ever happens, which here is Agreement and Validity. Liveness means something good eventually happens, which here is Termination.

The thing worth re-noticing is how you catch each one breaking. Safety can be checked by looking at any finite prefix of a run. Liveness can only ever be violated by an infinite run.

That is why the theorem's counterexample is an infinite run. And it is why "it hasn't finished yet" is never proof that it never will.

What world does Asha's cluster actually live in?

Partial synchrony is the honest middle: the network behaves most of the time, and you don't know when.

Said precisely, and this is Dwork, Lynch and Stockmeyer, 1988: bounds on delay and process speed exist, but are unknown. Or they are known, and only start holding after some unknown moment. That moment is called the Global Stabilization Time, usually written GST.

The word exists because "synchronous" is a lie about real networks, and "asynchronous" is too pessimistic to build on. Partial synchrony is what every production consensus system actually assumes, Asha's included.

8. Which two pictures should you carry, and where does the friendly one lie?

In one breath: Picture one is a patient ferryman who never cheats. Picture two is a walker on a graph of situations, who always has a bivalent step available. They are the same picture, and one of them is easier to remember.

What does the friendly picture get right?

Three keepers, letters, and a ferryman who is not malicious but is unlucky in exactly the worst way. Omar never destroys mail. He never refuses a crossing. He only ever chooses which letter to hand over next. That alone is enough to keep Ada, Bo and Cy talking forever.

What does the engineer draw instead?

The world is a directed graph. Nodes are configurations. Edges are events. Each node carries a hidden label: 0-valent, 1-valent, or bivalent.

The adversary is a walker. He starts on a bivalent node. At every move he has some edge leading to another bivalent node. A decided configuration is never bivalent. So the walker never steps onto one.

Where does the story lie to you?

Read these five before you repeat the lighthouse story to anyone.

Lie 1. Omar is not real, and your network is not malicious. He is a proof device. His job is to demonstrate that a bad run exists, not to predict what Asha's Tuesday looks like. In practice the "adversary" is a garbage collector, a saturated network card, or a noisy neighbour on a shared host. Indifferent, not hostile. Indifferent is enough, because the theorem only needs the run to be possible.

Lie 2. The story makes crashing feel central. It isn't. In the run the proof builds, nobody actually crashes. Ada, Bo and Cy all work the whole time. What matters is that the protocol must be willing to carry on without any one of them, and that willingness is the door Omar walks through. This is the point most summaries of FLP get wrong.

Lie 3. "Three keepers" suggests small numbers matter. They don't. The result holds for any number of processes, and only needs one possible crash. Adding replicas does not help even slightly. Dev suggests it in stage 11, and it does not work.

Lie 4. The story has a dawn deadline. The model has no time at all. "Before dawn" is a synchrony assumption smuggled in by the narrative. In the real model there is no dawn, no clock, and no meaning to "too late". There is only "eventually" and "never".

Lie 5. A ferryman "choosing" implies he sees the future. The FLP adversary is adaptive, in that it decides each next event knowing the current configuration. But it does not need to read the protocol's source code at runtime. The proof shows a suitable choice always exists. It does not require anyone to compute it.

INTERMEDIATE MODEL — stage 10

10. How does Omar stall a real protocol, and why can nobody stop him?

In one breath: First watch him beat one specific protocol by never letting two islands accept the same attempt. Then watch the 1985 proof do the same thing to every protocol at once, without ever naming one.

10.1 What protocol are the keepers running?

To watch a stall you need something to stall. Ada, Bo and Cy run single-decree Paxos with attempt numbers handed out in rotation. That is the paxos lab's protocol with one change: attempt numbers are assigned round-robin instead of being picked freely. Ada owns 1, 4, 7 and so on. Bo owns 2, 5, 8. Cy owns 3, 6, 9.

Here it is as five things a keeper does. You know it already, so this is a refresher.

  1. A keeper who has been waiting decides to try. She picks her next attempt number and writes a request for a turn to all three islands, including herself.
  2. A keeper receiving that request checks the number. If it is higher than any attempt she has promised, she promises it, and replies with an agreement that reports the last value she accepted, if any. If it is not higher, she says nothing at all.
  3. With agreements from a majority, which here means 2 of 3, the proposer picks the value. If any agreement reported an earlier acceptance, she is forced to re-propose that value. Otherwise she proposes her own preference. She writes a proposal to all.
  4. A keeper receiving a proposal accepts it if its attempt number is at least her promised attempt, and replies with an acknowledgement. Otherwise, silence.
  5. With acknowledgements from a majority, the proposer decides the value and writes the verdict to the others.

Five kinds of letter go in Omar's satchel. Read the middle column as what the letter actually says out loud, and the right column as the shorthand you will see in the traces.

The letter What it says in words Written as
a request for a turn "I am starting attempt n. Promise me you will honour nothing lower, and tell me what you have already written down." PREPARE(n)
an agreement "Attempt n is yours. Here is the last thing I accepted, if there was one." PROMISE(n, …)
a proposal "Under attempt n, write down this value." ACCEPT(n, v)
an acknowledgement "Done. I have written it down under attempt n." ACCEPTED(n)
the verdict "It is settled. The answer is this value." DECIDE(v)

Two things are worth noticing, because both matter later.

Refusal and death look the same. In steps 2 and 4 a keeper who refuses just doesn't reply. That is a simplification in this lab, because real Paxos usually sends a negative acknowledgement. It happens to make the lesson louder. From the proposer's side, "she refused me" and "she is dead" produce the identical observation. An empty mailbox, again.

Safety here is already proved, and does not depend on timing. The paxos lab established the quorum-overlap argument: any two majorities share a member, and that member's memory forces every later attempt to re-propose an already-chosen value. Nothing in that argument mentions delays. This lab ran the check again anyway, and the right-hand column is why it is worth reporting.

The safety soak Count What it means
random delivery orders driven through the protocol 500 enough shuffling to find an ordering bug if one existed
of those, orders run with a keeper crashed 1 in 3 crash and delay together, not one at a time
Agreement violations found 0 safety is not what breaks here

10.2 What does a fair crossing look like, step by step?

10.3 Why doesn't Omar simply hide the dangerous letter?

Look at step 11 above. Ada has one acknowledgement and needs two. One letter stands between this world and a settled answer.

🔮 Predict: Omar wants to stop this. His first idea is simply not to deliver that letter. Does it work?

Reveal No — it only postpones. The fairness contract says every letter must be delivered eventually. He can hold it for a thousand steps, but the moment he hands it over the matter is settled, and he must hand it over. He has bought time, not a result. To win he needs the letter to be harmless when it arrives, not merely late.

That is the whole refinement, and it is the sentence to carry into the proof. Omar does not need to withhold the dangerous letter. He needs to arrange, before delivering it, that the keeper receiving it will refuse it. Then he can hand it over with a clear conscience, fairness intact, and nothing happens.

He has exactly one tool for that. Attempt numbers. A keeper who has promised a higher attempt refuses a lower one. So:

Omar's rule. Never let two islands accept the same attempt. Whenever a proposal is about to reach a second island, first deliver that island a request for a turn at a higher attempt. If no such letter exists, let some other keeper give up waiting, which manufactures one.

Every attempt therefore collects at most one acknowledgement, never a majority, and nobody ever decides.

10.5 That beat one protocol. How do you beat every protocol at once?

Everything above beats this protocol. Ben's answer is the reasonable one: "then fix the protocol." The 1985 proof closes that door for good. It rests on three facts, and it mentions no protocol at all.

Read the three facts as three questions Omar might ask on the quayside.

  • Is there any morning where the ending is genuinely still open? Fact A.
  • Does it matter which island I visit first? Fact B.
  • Can I always make a dangerous letter boring before I deliver it? Fact C.

Yes, no, and yes. Those three answers are the whole theorem.

Fact A — is any starting morning genuinely undecided?

Suppose Meera claims the opposite: that every morning is already settled before a single letter is written. Try to build her world and watch it break.

Line up the possible mornings so that each one differs from the next in exactly one keeper's wish. Start with all three wanting dark. End with all three wanting light. Change one mind at a time.

 D D D        L D D        L L D        L L L
 only DARK      ?            ?         only LIGHT

Validity nails down the two ends. If everyone wants dark, dark is the only answer allowed. If everyone wants light, light is. So walking from the left end to the right end, you begin at a dark-only morning and finish at a light-only one. Somewhere along that line, two neighbours must have different endings. Call them C₀ (dark-only) and C₁ (light-only). They differ only in what one keeper p wants.

Now use the one thing every protocol here must survive: a keeper falling ill.

Take the morning C₀, and consider the run in which p never gets out of bed. Not one step. That run is legal, because the protocol must cope with one crash. The other two keepers must still finish. By assumption they finish dark.

Now run the exact same story from C₁. In C₁, p takes no steps either. And p's wish is the only difference between the two mornings. The other two never heard it. They cannot tell which morning they are in. So they behave identically, letter for letter, and they finish dark in C₁ too.

But C₁ was supposed to be a morning that can only end light. Contradiction. So Meera's world does not exist. Some starting point is bivalent. ∎

That is the proof. Written the way papers write it, it goes in four moves. Line up the initial configurations in a chain, each differing from the next in one input. Use Validity to fix the valency of the two endpoints. Take adjacent C₀ and C₁ of opposite valency, differing at process p. Apply a p-free run to both: indistinguishability at the surviving processes forces the same decision, which contradicts opposite valency.

🔮 Predict: That argument used a crash. In the infinite run the theorem eventually builds, how many keepers actually fall ill?

Reveal None. The crash appears only inside the argument, as a hypothetical run used to prove two starting points are indistinguishable. The run the theorem constructs has every keeper healthy and working the whole time. What the theorem needs is not a crash — it is that the protocol must be willing to proceed without any one keeper. That willingness is the vulnerability. A protocol that refused to tolerate any crash could wait for everyone and would be immune to FLP — and would also stop dead the first time a machine rebooted.

Fact B — does it matter which island Omar visits first?

Omar has two letters in his satchel. One is for Ada. One is for Bo. He can deliver either first. Does the choice change anything?

No, and the reason is almost embarrassingly simple. Ada opening her post cannot change one word in Bo's cottage. Nothing in this world touches a keeper except a letter placed in that keeper's own hand. Ada's step changes Ada's notebook and drops new letters into the sea. Bo's step changes Bo's notebook and drops new letters into the sea. Neither reaches across.

So the two visits never interfere. Deliver Ada's letter then Bo's, or Bo's then Ada's, and both cottages end up identical, with the same letters still at sea. Order is invisible.

That paragraph is the proof. Written the way papers write it: let event e happen at keeper p, and event e′ at a different keeper q. Then applying e then e′ yields exactly the configuration you get by applying e′ then e. The two events commute.

Fact C — can a dangerous letter always be made harmless first?

This is the heart of it, and it is the one worth reading slowly.

In plain words: you can always let the annoying event happen. Just not yet, and not before you have arranged for it to be harmless. That is exactly Omar's refinement from stage 10.3, promoted from a trick against one protocol to a fact about all of them.

Stated properly: let C be a situation where both endings are still open, and let e be any single pending event in it. Then there is a schedule from C, ending with e, whose final situation still has both endings open.

Try to imagine it failing, and watch it break. Suppose Omar is stuck. There is some pending letter e such that no matter what he arranges first, the moment e lands the ending is sealed.

Give the two piles names. Let 𝒞 be every situation Omar can reach from C without delivering e. Let 𝒟 be what you get by delivering e to each of them. Omar being stuck means every situation in 𝒟 is univalent.

Step 1: 𝒟 contains both a dark-only and a light-only situation. C is bivalent, so some run from C ends in dark. Either that run never used e, in which case delivering e at the end gives a dark-only member of 𝒟. Or it used e somewhere, and the situation right after e is itself a dark-only member of 𝒟. Either way, 𝒟 has a dark-only member. The same argument gives a light-only one.

Step 2: two of them are only one step apart. Everything in 𝒞 is reachable from C, so you can walk from one witness to the other. Somewhere on that walk the label flips across a single edge. That gives you C₀ and C₁ in 𝒞 with C₁ = e′(C₀) for one event e′, where delivering e to C₀ gives a dark-only D₀, and delivering e to C₁ gives a light-only D₁.

Now there are exactly two cases, and Omar's two facts handle one each.

🔮 Predict: Case one is when e′ lands on a different keeper than e, and Fact B settles it in a line. Case two is when both land on the same keeper, so swapping is not available. Which tool is left?

Reveal Crash tolerance — the same tool Fact A used, and the only other thing the proof is allowed to assume. If both events land on keeper p, you silence p entirely and let the other two finish, which they must. If you said "a third fact" or "something about attempt numbers", notice that the proof has never mentioned attempt numbers and never will: it does not know what protocol it is arguing about.

Case one: e′ happens at a different keeper than e. Then by Fact B they commute, so

D₁ = e(e′(C₀)) = e′(e(C₀)) = e′(D₀)

D₁ is reachable from D₀ in a single step. But D₀ is dark-only, so everything reachable from it is dark-only. And D₁ is light-only. Contradiction.

Case two: e′ happens at the same keeper p as e. Now use crash tolerance, exactly as in Fact A. From C₀, let p go completely silent. The other keepers must still finish, so there is a finite run σ from C₀ containing no steps by p, which reaches a situation A where somebody has decided. Because σ contains no steps by p, it commutes with both e and e′. So:

e(A)        = e(σ(C₀))        = σ(e(C₀))        = σ(D₀)   → dark-only
e(e′(A))    = e(e′(σ(C₀)))    = σ(e(e′(C₀)))    = σ(D₁)   → light-only

From A you can reach a dark-only situation and a light-only situation. So A is bivalent. But A is a situation in which somebody has already decided, which makes it univalent. Contradiction.

Both cases are impossible, so the assumption was wrong. A bivalent next move always exists. ∎

The theorem — what do the three facts add up to?

Start at a bivalent initial situation, which Fact A says exists. Then repeat forever:

Take the keeper who has gone longest without a step, and the oldest letter addressed to them. If their mailbox is empty, take a null step instead. Call that event e. By Fact C, there is a schedule ending in e that leaves the world still bivalent. Run it.

Two things are now true at the same time, and their coexistence is the whole result.

  • The run is admissible. Every letter eventually becomes the oldest one addressed to the longest-idle keeper, so every letter is delivered. Every keeper takes infinitely many steps. Nobody is starved. No letter is lost. No keeper crashes.
  • Every situation in the run is bivalent, and a situation in which somebody has decided is never bivalent. So nobody ever decides.

Termination is violated, in an admissible run, by a protocol that was assumed to guarantee it. Therefore no such protocol exists. ∎

Fischer, Lynch & Paterson, "Impossibility of Distributed Consensus with One Faulty Process", Journal of the ACM 32(2), 1985. It won the Dijkstra Prize in 2001.

11. Where does the theorem show up on a Tuesday?

In one breath: The theorem has no failure modes of its own. It has sightings: moments when a real cluster briefly re-enters tab 2 and stops finishing things while every dashboard stays green.

A protocol has bugs. A theorem does not. What this theorem has are sightings, which are situations where a real system briefly re-enters the world of tab 2 and stops finishing things. Here are the eight worth recognising on sight. Asha and her team have hit every one.

Sighting 1 · Duelling proposers, also called election livelock. Two of Asha's nodes have retry timers that keep firing just before the other one completes. Every attempt reaches one acceptance and then dies to a higher attempt. The attempt counter climbs without limit. This is exactly the trace in stage 10.4, and there is no response available from inside the protocol, because this is the FLP run. Raft's randomised election timeouts and Paxos's stable-leader optimisation exist to make it self-correcting, not to prevent it.

Sighting 2 · The garbage collector as the adversary. Arjun's leader node takes a stop-the-world pause longer than the failure-detection timeout. The leader is frozen. Followers see silence and start an election. Then the old leader wakes up believing it is still leader. A frozen process and a dead process emit identical evidence, which is stage 4 happening in a machine room. The old leader discovers a higher term and steps down, so Agreement survives. This is why broadcastTime ≪ electionTimeout ≪ MTBF is a rule and not a suggestion.

Sighting 3 · The failure detector that is confidently wrong. Meera's cluster hits packet loss that outlasts her timeout. Healthy nodes are evicted. Quorum is lost. The cluster stops. Her failure detector is a timeout, and a timeout is a guess. The cure is a suspicion mechanism with refutation, like the incarnation numbers in gossip-protocols. The design rule underneath it: separate suspicion from eviction, make suspicion appealable, and never let a failure detector remove a majority.

Sighting 4 · The grey failure, a leader that is alive but useless. Asha's leader sits on a host with a dying disk and a noisy neighbour. It is fast enough to heartbeat and too slow to commit. Every health check is green. Every dashboard is green. Priya's write latency is infinite. The failure detector answers "is it responding?", but liveness needs "is it making progress?", and those are different questions. There is usually no automatic response. This is the classic pager-at-3am shape, and the single most common way the theorem bites people who have never heard of it.

Sighting 5 · Symmetric timeouts and split votes forever. Ben gives every node the same election timeout, because it is tidier. Identical timers make everyone a candidate simultaneously, every time, forever. The raft lab has this on record. Randomisation does not prevent split votes. It makes each one self-correcting, which is a much cheaper thing to buy.

Sighting 6 · Adding replicas, and it not helping. The cluster is stalling, so Dev suggests scaling from 3 nodes to 5, and then to 7. It does not help at all. The theorem needs one possible crash and holds for any number of processes. More replicas raise the crash count you can survive, and enlarge the quorum you must assemble. They do nothing whatsoever about the ambiguity between slow and dead. The fix is always to attack the timing assumption, never to add votes.

Sighting 7 · The partition that heals, and the cluster that doesn't. The network splits, and then it is repaired. gossip-protocols recorded a healed partition where beliefs had already converged on "everyone is dead", so nobody would talk to anybody. Liveness is not automatically restored by removing the fault, because the fault left durable state behind. Every recovery path needs an explicit way back, whether that is refutation, re-seeding, or an operator-triggered reset. And it must be tested, because it runs approximately never.

Sighting 8 · Nobody is even trying to be adversarial. Deliver letters in a uniformly random order. No malice, no strategy, no crash required. A healthy fraction of all schedules stall anyway. This is why "we've never seen it" is not evidence. You have seen it. You called it a slow period.

12. What exactly is claimed, and what is not?

In one breath: Six words in the theorem are load-bearing. Weaken the right one and consensus becomes solvable. Weaken the wrong one and you get a different impossible problem.

12.1 Which words in the theorem are load-bearing?

In an asynchronous system with reliable message delivery, where at most one process may crash, there is no deterministic protocol that guarantees Agreement, Validity, and Termination.

Every bold word carries weight, and knowing which is which is the practical payoff of this whole lab. Read the right-hand column as what happens to the theorem when you pull that word out.

Word If you weaken it Result
asynchronous assume a delay bound Δ, even an unknown one consensus becomes solvable — this is what your cluster does
reliable allow message loss you get a different, also-impossible problem (Two Generals) — weakening this makes things worse, not better
one crash tolerate zero crashes trivially solvable: wait for everyone
deterministic let processes flip coins solvable with probability 1 (Ben-Or)
Agreement allow disagreement you have eventual consistency, not consensus
Termination only promise it when the network behaves this is what every real system actually ships

12.2 What are the seven things FLP does not say?

Arjun has heard all seven of these said out loud in design reviews. Each one is wrong, and each one is wrong in an instructive way.

  1. It does not say consensus is impossible. etcd, ZooKeeper, Spanner and Kafka do it continuously. It says you cannot put a guarantee on finishing.
  2. It is not about slow networks. The messages are reliable and the machines are fast. It is about the absence of a known bound, not the size of the delay.
  3. It is not about crashes. In the run it constructs, nobody crashes. Only the obligation to tolerate a crash is used.
  4. It is not fixed by more replicas. One possible crash suffices, at any cluster size. This is Dev's suggestion from sighting 6.
  5. It does not say your system will hang. It says a hanging run exists. In practice you get liveness with overwhelming probability, and you pay for that probability with timing assumptions you must maintain.
  6. It is not CAP. See the next subsection, because this is the most common confusion of all.
  7. It does not apply to synchronous systems. With known bounds on delay and processing speed, a timeout that expires is proof, and consensus is straightforwardly solvable.

12.3 Which three results do people mix up?

Three impossibility results, three different adversaries. The difference is entirely in what the adversary is allowed to do to a letter.

What the adversary does What is impossible Fix in practice
FLP (1985) delays and reorders messages; loses nothing guaranteeing termination assume partial synchrony, or randomise
CAP (2000/2002) cuts the network in two; messages don't arrive at all staying available and consistent during that cut choose per-operation; see your pacelc lab
Two Generals destroys messages ever achieving certainty of agreement over a lossy channel acknowledge + retry + idempotence; see your idempotency lab

The one-line separators, worth memorising:

  • CAP takes the messages away. FLP leaves every message intact and only takes away the clock, and that alone is enough.
  • Two Generals loses letters. FLP loses the clock.
  • CAP is a statement about safety versus availability during a partition. FLP is a statement about liveness in the complete absence of a partition. FLP is the older, deeper, and quieter result.

One more distinction. FLP assumes non-Byzantine failures, meaning processes stop and never lie. Byzantine agreement is a strictly harder problem, and has its own impossibility results, such as needing n > 3f. FLP's power comes from assuming the gentlest possible failure and still finding the wall.

Escape 1 — partial synchrony, which breaks "asynchronous". Assume a bound Δ on delivery, even an unknown one, or one that only starts holding after some unknown moment. Escape 3 — randomised decisions, which breaks "deterministic" properly. Ben-Or (1983) and Rabin (1983): processes flip coins over the value, not the timing. This terminates with probability 1 and needs no timing assumption at all. The FLP run still exists. It just has probability zero. This one is not simulated in this lab, because it needs a different protocol. And note what "probability 1" does and does not mean. It is not "guaranteed". It is "the exceptions have measure zero". That is a genuinely weaker promise, even though it is the one you would happily ship.

Escape 4 — failure detectors, which break "no information about failures". Give processes an oracle that offers hints, possibly wrong ones, about who has crashed. Chandra & Toueg (1996) showed consensus becomes solvable with surprisingly weak oracles. Chandra, Hadzilacos & Toueg showed ◇W is the weakest oracle that can do it, given a majority of correct processes. ◇W eventually stops suspecting some correct process, and eventually suspects every crashed one. In this lab's simulator, an oracle naming one keeper who is never wrongly doubted beats Omar in 13 steps, on attempt 1, with no timing assumption at all. And here is the sting. You cannot build that oracle in a truly asynchronous world. If you could, you would have refuted FLP. In practice you build it out of timeouts, which is escape 1 wearing a hat.

12.5 What was traded away?

Every real system makes the same trade, and it is worth being able to state it in one breath:

Keep safety unconditionally. Buy liveness with a timing assumption you are responsible for maintaining.

Concretely, in Asha's Raft or Paxos cluster: Agreement and Validity hold under any pattern of delay, reordering, duplication and crash. No assumption required. Termination holds only while the network and the machines behave roughly as she assumed. Her election timeout is the price tag on that assumption, and her monitoring is how she finds out she was wrong.

13.5 What did this lab deliberately leave out?

Named, so you know what you have not yet learned.

  • Persistence. The keepers never forget. Real acceptors must flush their promise and acceptance to disk before replying. The paxos lab shows what amnesia does: two different values chosen, with every node obeying the protocol.
  • Replicated logs. This is single-decree, meaning one value, once. Real systems agree on an unbounded sequence, which brings log matching, compaction and snapshots (raft).
  • Membership changes. Adding and removing nodes safely is its own hard problem.
  • Message loss and duplication. Omar is reliable on purpose, and that is what makes the result strong. Real networks lose and duplicate, which is strictly worse, and is why idempotency exists.
  • Network partitions. Also on purpose, and also strictly worse. That is cap-theorem and split-brain.
  • Byzantine failures. Processes here stop; they never lie. PBFT and friends are a different and harder world.
  • Ben-Or's randomised protocol. Described in stage 12.4 and not simulated, because it needs a different protocol from the one in the visualization. The lab says so rather than pretending.
  • More than three processes, batching, pipelining, flow control, and real wall-clock time. Steps here are abstract. A "step" is not a millisecond.

16. GLOSSARY — what does each word mean, precisely?

In one breath: Seven small tables instead of one long one, grouped by the question each set of words answers.

What are we asking the keepers for?

Term One line
Agreement No two processes decide different values. A safety property.
Validity The decided value is one some process actually proposed — not invented. A safety property.
Termination Every non-crashed process eventually decides. A liveness property.
Consensus Agreement + Validity + Termination together.
Safety A promise that nothing bad ever happens; violated by a finite prefix of a run.
Liveness A promise that something good eventually happens; only ever violated by an infinite run.

What kind of world are they living in?

Term One line
Asynchronous system No bound on message delay, no bound on relative process speed, no synchronised clocks.
Synchronous system Known bounds exist, so an expired timeout is proof of a crash.
Partial synchrony Bounds on delay and speed exist but are unknown, or only begin to hold after some unknown moment. (Dwork, Lynch & Stockmeyer, 1988.)
GST (Global Stabilization Time) The unknown moment after which the timing bounds start holding.

How is a keeper allowed to fail, and who notices?

Term One line
Crash failure A process stops permanently, without warning, and never lies or comes back.
Non-Byzantine The failure model in which processes may stop but never behave maliciously.
Failure detector A component that offers hints — possibly wrong — about which processes have crashed.
◇W "Eventually weak": eventually stops suspecting some correct process and eventually suspects every crashed one. The weakest failure detector that solves consensus with a correct majority. (Chandra, Hadzilacos & Toueg, 1996.)
Grey failure A component alive enough to pass health checks but too degraded to make progress.

What are the proof's moving parts?

Term One line
Step One process reading its mailbox once and reacting; the atom of execution.
Event A step described as the pair (process, message received).
Null step A step in which receive returns nothing — the only thing a timeout can be in a clockless model.
Configuration A complete snapshot: every process's state plus every message in flight.

What does the adversary control?

Term One line
Schedule A sequence of events applied one after another from a configuration.
Run A schedule actually executed.
Admissible run A run in which at most one process is faulty and every message to a correct process is eventually delivered — the adversary's fairness contract.
Deterministic Same state plus same input always produces the same behaviour; no coin flips, no clock reads.

Which endings can still happen?

Term One line
Valency Which decisions are still reachable from a configuration.
Bivalent Both 0 and 1 are still reachable — the future is genuinely open.
Univalent Only one value is still reachable; the outcome is sealed even if nobody knows it. 0-valent / 1-valent name which.

What does the protocol, and the production system, use?

Term One line
Attempt number This lab's word for a Paxos proposal number / Raft term; a totally ordered ballot identifier.
Quorum A majority; any two quorums share at least one member. (From quorums-and-replication.)
Livelock Continuous activity that never makes progress — the everyday face of an FLP run.
Pre-vote A Raft extension in which a candidate checks it could win before raising the term, preventing a returning partitioned node from disrupting a healthy cluster.
Check-quorum A leader that cannot reach a majority steps down instead of continuing to act as leader.

FLP Impossibility — Solutions

Attempt the exercises first. Every number quoted here was produced by the simulation engine in visualization.html; you can reproduce all of them.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 (model answer, 108 words) Ada writes to Bo and waits. Nothing comes back. There are exactly two reasons that could be true: Bo is dead, or Bo's letter is on a slow boat. Ada's evidence is the same in both cases — an empty mailbox — and no letter she can write will tell them apart, because the only difference is how long the future will take. So she has to guess. Guess too short and she writes off a living colleague, who is also carrying on, and now two people are running the coast. Guess too long and she waits past dawn. A timeout does not remove the guess; it just picks a number for it.

1.2 FLP does not say agreement is impossible — it says no protocol can guarantee that it always finishes, in a world where messages can take unbounded time. etcd works because it does not live in that world: it assumes message delays are usually bounded, and turns that assumption into an election timeout. When the assumption briefly fails — a long GC pause, a saturated link — etcd does exactly what the theorem predicts and stops making progress for a while. It never gives a wrong answer, though; safety never depended on timing.

1.3 "Nobody has decided yet" is about what is written on the slates. "The outcome is not yet decided" is about what is still reachable. They come apart: a situation can have three blank slates and still be univalent — every possible continuation ends in DARK, and the keepers simply have not caught up yet. The proof needs the second, stronger statement (bivalent), because its whole strategy is "stay somewhere both endings are still live". If it only tracked blank slates, it would have no way to rule out walking into a situation whose ending was already sealed. You can see the difference in tab 3: all three want DARK has three blank slates and is univalent; Ada DARK, Bo LIGHT, Cy LIGHT has three blank slates and is bivalent.

1.4 Zero. In the run the proof constructs, all three keepers work the entire time and every letter is delivered. This surprises people because the theorem is usually paraphrased as "with one faulty process", which sounds like a process must fail. What the proof actually uses is the obligation to tolerate a failure: because the protocol must be able to finish without any one keeper, there always exists a run in which the others carry on without hearing from that keeper — and that run is indistinguishable from one where the keeper was simply slow. A protocol that refused to tolerate any crash could just wait for everyone, would be immune to FLP, and would deadlock the first time a machine rebooted.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 The very first event of any run must be a null step — a step in which a process receives nothing. It cannot be a message delivery, because no message exists until somebody sends one, and a process only sends messages as part of taking a step. This is the model's answer to "what starts the machine?", and it is why a timeout is not something bolted on to consensus — it is the only possible first move.

2.2 A representative instance, from the trace in README §10.4: (a) either remaining ACCEPT(1, DARK) letter — the one to Bo or the one to Cy — would have produced a second acceptance and settled the matter; (b) the ferryman instead delivered nothing to them, and let Bo give up waiting, which started attempt 2 and sent PREPARE(2) to everyone; (c) once Bo and Cy had promised attempt 2, the ACCEPT(1, DARK) letters were delivered normally and refused. The dangerous letter was not withheld — it was made harmless first, then delivered.

2.3 For the ferryman to be cheating, either letters lost would have to be non-zero, or longest a letter has waited / longest any keeper sat idle would have to grow without bound. Neither happens: measured over 5000 steps, letters lost stays 0, longest letter delay stays at 7 steps, and longest keeper idle stays at 4 steps. That lets you make the claim that matters: the run is admissible — it satisfies the fairness contract completely — and still nobody decides. If the numbers grew, the demonstration would prove nothing, because an adversary who starves people obviously breaks things.

2.4 The half-dark, half-lit lamp is bivalent — both endings still reachable. In a univalent situation where nobody has decided, the lamp would look exactly the same, and that is the point: valency is a property of the whole configuration, not of anything a single keeper can see. No island — and no picture of an island — can display it. The page can only compute it globally, by searching schedules, which is what tab 3 does.

2.5 The attempt number is the interesting one. The step count only says time passed. The attempt number climbing (measured: 682 after 5000 steps) says the keepers are working hard — starting attempt after attempt, each one reaching one acceptance and dying. It is the difference between a system that is idle and a system that is busy achieving nothing, and only the second one is a livelock.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 Yes, the ferryman still wins. Measured: all three wanting LIGHT, the stalling ferryman held it open for 1000 steps, reaching attempt 137, longest letter delay 7 steps. Under the ordinary ferry the same start settles on LIGHT at step 15.

This is the most instructive result in the exercise set, because that starting point is univalent — LIGHT is the only reachable answer. Univalence says which value, if one is ever chosen. It says nothing about whether one is ever chosen. Determined and terminating are different properties, and FLP attacks the second.

3.2 (a) Ordinary ferry with Bo ill: the remaining two settle on DARK at step 30 on attempt 1 — slower than the 15 steps with three healthy keepers, because Ada waits for letters that will never arrive and has to be satisfied by a bare majority. (b) Stalling ferryman with Bo ill: still nothing decided after 1000 steps, attempt 189. Two healthy keepers are quite enough to duel with each other. (With Ada ill instead, the ordinary ferry settles on LIGHT at step 30, on attempt 2 — Bo's attempt. Note the answer changed, and that is legal: Validity only requires the answer be somebody's preference.)

3.3 Δ = 2 settles at step 19; Δ = 25 settles at step 63. Both use exactly one attempt. The lesson: once the ferryman cannot delay a letter past the deadline, he cannot manufacture a rival attempt in time, and the very first attempt runs to completion. The cost of a loose bound is pure latency, not extra attempts — a system with a generous election timeout is not less correct, it is just slower to recover.

3.4 The ferryman loses, in 13 steps, using 1–3 attempts (specifically: attempt 1 if Ada is the named leader, 2 if Bo, 3 if Cy — each keeper's first owned attempt number). This is the striking one: no timing assumption at all was needed. Take away the ferryman's ability to manufacture a rival attempt and he has nothing left. The catch is in README §12.4 — such an oracle cannot be built in a truly asynchronous world, because building one would refute FLP.

3.5 Ada proposes her own preference, because neither agreement she received reported an earlier acceptance. This is safe, and the rule that makes it safe is quorum overlap, not luck: Bo's (4, LIGHT) was accepted by one keeper, so LIGHT was never chosen — choosing requires a majority. Any value that was chosen would have been accepted by a majority, and Ada's majority of agreements must intersect it, so at least one of her agreements would have reported it and forced her hand. A single acceptance is a vote, not a decision; the protocol is allowed to abandon it. (This is your paxos lab's P2c invariant, and this lab re-measured it: 0 agreement violations in 500 random schedules, one third of them with a crashed keeper.)

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 You attacked deterministic — and it did not help. Measured: with randomised waiting times and no delivery deadline, the ferryman held it open in 60 runs out of 60. The reason is that the adversary is patient and adaptive: when a keeper's random pause is in effect, he simply delivers other letters until it expires, then uses the keeper exactly as before. Randomising when somebody retries does nothing against an opponent allowed to wait arbitrarily long.

Adding Δ = 4 changes everything: 60 out of 60 runs settle, averaging 23.0 steps — the same as Δ = 4 with no randomisation at all. So in this three-keeper setup the randomisation contributed nothing measurable once Δ was present. What it buys in a real system is protection against collisions: many nodes timing out simultaneously and cancelling each other. Your raft lab measured that directly — identical timeouts gave term 40 in 6000 ticks and no leader ever, while randomised ones elected a leader in 200/200 cold starts. Randomised timeouts cure collisions; they do not cure asynchrony.

4.2 It does not defeat the ferryman. He controls the delivery of the refusal letter exactly as he controls every other letter, so he can hold it as long as he likes. What improves is expected liveness under partial synchrony: a superseded proposer learns quickly that it is wasting its time and can back off instead of sitting in a doomed attempt, which reduces how often two proposers duel. What is completely unaffected: safety (it was never at risk) and the impossibility itself. Real Paxos does send negative acknowledgements for exactly this reason — it is a practical optimisation, not a fix.

4.3 Essentially no change. The ferryman needs only that some higher attempt number is always obtainable, and both schemes give him that. Free choice arguably makes his job marginally easier — any keeper can outbid immediately rather than waiting for their slot — but "marginally easier than always winning" is not a meaningful improvement. Rotation exists for a different reason: it makes "who is the natural leader right now" computable from the attempt number alone, which is what Multi-Paxos and Raft build on.

4.4 The plan requires the keepers to agree on what day it is, and there is no clock. Agreeing on the current day is itself a consensus problem — the same problem, with a different name — so the plan assumes the thing it was meant to provide. This is the general shape of most proposed escapes: they smuggle in a shared time reference. The honest version of this plan is "assume a bound Δ and derive turns from local timers", which is escape 1, with all its conditions.

Rung 5 — Trace

5.1 Executed by the simulator, step by step:

1  Ada gives up waiting        → PREPARE(1) to Ada, Bo, Cy
2  PREPARE(1) → Bo             Bo promises 1, replies PROMISE(1, accepted:none)
3  PREPARE(1) → Cy             Cy promises 1, replies PROMISE(1, accepted:none)
4  PROMISE(1) → Ada            Ada has 1 of 2 agreements
5  PROMISE(1) → Ada            Ada has 2 → proposes DARK, sends ACCEPT(1,DARK) ×3
6  ACCEPT(1,DARK) → Bo         Bo accepts (1,DARK), replies ACCEPTED(1)
7  Cy gives up waiting         → PREPARE(3) to all   (3 is Cy's next owned number)
8  PREPARE(3) → Bo             Bo promises 3, reports it accepted (1,DARK)
9  ACCEPT(1,DARK) → Cy         Cy ACCEPTS it

At step 6 the letter carries DARK, because neither agreement Ada received reported an earlier acceptance, so she was free to propose her own preference.

At step 9 Cy accepts — and this is the subtle part. Cy started attempt 3 at step 7, but Cy's own PREPARE(3) to itself is still at sea, so Cy's promised attempt is still 1 and the late ACCEPT(1, DARK) is perfectly acceptable to it. A keeper's own letters to itself go through the same post as everyone else's, and the ferryman may delay them too.

Final state: Ada{promised 0, accepted none, running attempt 1, 0 acceptances collected} — her own PREPARE(1) to herself was never delivered, and both ACCEPTED(1) replies are still at sea; Bo{promised 3, accepted (1,DARK)}; Cy{promised 1, accepted (1,DARK), running attempt 3}.

What Ada believes about attempt 1: nothing has come back, so she cannot distinguish "two keepers accepted and the replies are in transit" (which is what actually happened, and means DARK is now chosen) from "everyone refused me". Two keepers accepting is a majority — the value is chosen at step 9 — and nobody in the system knows it yet. That gap between chosen and known is where every consensus bug lives.

5.2 The ferryman's three moves, taken from the engine:

1  let BO give up waiting     → Bo starts attempt 5, PREPARE(5) to all three
   why: the two ACCEPT(4,DARK) letters at sea are lethal — attempt 4 already
   has one acceptance (Ada's own), so either letter would be the second.
   He needs a higher attempt in existence before he delivers them.
2  deliver PREPARE(5) → Bo    Bo promises 5
3  deliver PREPARE(5) → Cy    Cy promises 5
   why: these are the two islands the lethal letters are addressed to.

Afterwards: Ada{promised 4, accepted (4,DARK)}, Bo{promised 5}, Cy{promised 5}, and at sea: both ACCEPT(4,DARK) letters (now certain to be refused), plus PREPARE(5)→Ada and two PROMISE(5) replies to Bo. The ferryman can now deliver the two dangerous letters at his leisure, fairness intact, knowing they do nothing. Attempt 5 will die the same way.

5.3 Both orders give the identical result. PROMISE(1) Bo→Ada only touches Ada's state; ACCEPT(1,DARK) Ada→Cy only touches Cy's state. Neither can observe the other, because the only thing that can change a keeper is a letter arriving at that keeper. Whichever order you use, you end with Ada holding one agreement for attempt 1, Cy having accepted (1, DARK) and sent ACCEPTED(1), and Bo untouched.

For a pair where the answer is no, take two events at the same island — for example PROMISE(1) Bo→Ada and PROMISE(1) Cy→Ada. The second one arrives at an Ada who has already been changed by the first: after one promise she is still collecting; after the second she reaches a majority and sends out ACCEPT — and which promise arrived second determines which one she was holding when she crossed the threshold. The engine's audit finds exactly this: 133 of 133 swaps at different islands identical, 5 of 48 at the same island different.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 "Wait three round trips before retrying." Two errors. First, "three round trips" is not measurable without a clock or a delay bound — the thing the model denies you. Second, even granting a way to count them, the ferryman decides when every step happens, so he simply arranges for the wait to expire at the moment that hurts. This is exactly the randomised-backoff escape, measured: 60 runs out of 60, the ferryman still won. Violated guarantee: Termination. What the rule genuinely buys is a lower probability of collision under partial synchrony — worth having, but not a fix.

6.2 "30 seconds is 300× our p99, so it is safe." The error is reasoning from a percentile to a bound. p99 says nothing about the tail beyond it, and the tail is where this problem lives: your split-brain lab measured connection p99 going from 1,011 µs to 191,661 µs — a 190× collapse — on a healthy server, caused by garbage collection, which is inside the machine and entirely unaffected by network quality. A 400 ms pause can become a 40 s pause on a large heap under pressure. Two guarantees are at stake, not one: Termination if the timeout is too long, and — much worse — Agreement, if "declare failed and proceed" is not paired with fencing, because the declared-dead participant is still running and its in-flight work will land later. See split-brain for the fencing machinery.

6.3 "Randomised election timeouts, so FLP does not apply." Randomising when a node retries is not the same as randomising what it decides. The transition function is still deterministic given its inputs; only a timer draw changed, and the adversary is adaptive and patient enough to wait it out. Measured in this lab: 60 runs out of 60 held open with randomised waits and no delivery bound. The escape that genuinely breaks "deterministic" is Ben-Or's, where processes flip coins over the value — and it buys termination with probability 1, which is still not a guarantee.

6.4 "Seven replicas, so more resistant." FLP needs one possible crash and holds for any number of processes. Going 3 → 7 raises the number of crashes you survive, which is a real and different benefit — but it does nothing about the slow-versus-dead ambiguity, and it makes each round require agreement from 4 nodes instead of 2, so the slowest member of a larger quorum sets your latency. On liveness, larger clusters are usually slightly worse, not better.

6.5 "Single rack, sub-millisecond RTT, effectively synchronous." You have an empirical distribution, not a guaranteed bound — and the theorem's asynchrony is about the absence of a guarantee. Worse, the dominant sources of long pauses are not on the wire at all: stop-the-world GC, page-cache stalls, kernel scheduler preemption, a hypervisor stealing CPU, an fsync behind a full disk queue. A dedicated switch fixes none of those. The honest version of this claim is "our Δ is small and we monitor it", which is exactly right and is escape 1 stated properly.

Rung 7 — Handle failure (worked discussion, not a key)

7.1 Forty elections an hour, no crashes.

Measure first: GC pause p99/p999 on every member; fsync p99 on the consensus data directory; peer-to-peer RTT p99; and the ratio election timeout ÷ observed broadcast time. You are looking for which of these is closest to the timeout.

Three likely causes and their fixes:

Cause Fix Word attacked
GC pauses exceeding the election timeout raise the election timeout; tune/replace the collector; shrink the heap asynchronous — you are restoring the Δ you assumed
a disk whose fsync tail exceeds the heartbeat interval faster/dedicated disk, or separate the consensus volume asynchronous
a partitioned or flapping member repeatedly bumping the term on return enable pre-vote and check-quorum neither — this attacks disruption, not the theorem

What would not help: adding replicas (§11 sighting 6 — raises crash tolerance, does nothing for liveness, enlarges the quorum you must assemble); lowering the election timeout (makes false positives more frequent, which is the disease); restarting nodes (see 7.4).

7.2 Making a slow keeper harmless when it wakes.

The design must add a monotonically increasing epoch attached to every action — your raft term, your paxos attempt number, your distributed-locking fencing token — and, critically, the resource must check it. The keeper does not need to know it was slow; the resource refuses work stamped with an epoch older than the highest it has seen. Your split-brain lab's headline applies directly: if the resource does not check the token, the first two mechanisms give zero protection. Note what this does and does not achieve: it makes the stale keeper's work harmless, which is a safety property. It does not stop the stall, and nothing can.

7.3 Detecting grey failure.

Measure time since the commit index last advanced, per group, and compare it against whether client writes are arriving. A leader that is heartbeating but has not advanced the commit index while writes are queued is the signature. Threshold: something like 5–10× your normal commit latency, tuned so it never fires during a routine leader change. Automatic action: have the leader step down voluntarily (most Raft implementations support a leadership transfer), which is far safer than killing the process. The risk is a feedback loop — if the underlying cause is cluster-wide (a slow disk model, a network event), automatic step-downs will just move the problem around and add elections to an already struggling cluster. So rate-limit it, and never let it fire on more than one member at a time.

7.4 "No leader for 60 seconds → restart nodes one at a time."

When it is right: almost never as a first action, but it can clear a genuinely wedged process — a deadlock, a leaked file descriptor, a pathological GC state.

When it is actively harmful: if the cause is load or slow disks, restarting removes a member from an already-degraded quorum, forces log catch-up on restart (more IO, on the disk that was already the problem), and can turn a temporary stall into an outage. If two people run the runbook at once on a three-node cluster, you lose quorum entirely.

Replace it with: (1) check whether this is a stall or a genuine failure — time since last commit, GC p99, disk p99, peer RTT; (2) if the assumption is merely strained, raise the election timeout at runtime if your system supports it, and shed load; (3) only restart a member you have positively identified as wedged, never on a timer, never more than one at a time, and never without confirming the remaining members form a quorum.

Rung 8 — Design (worked discussion)

8.1 Config store across three datacenters, 50 ms apart.

What survives from this lab: everything about safety. Quorum overlap, attempt/term ordering, and the requirement that a value accepted by a majority can never be lost are all independent of geography. Also surviving: the fact that you cannot guarantee termination, now with a much larger Δ.

What must change: the timing constants, by roughly an order of magnitude. With 50 ms inter-DC RTT, a heartbeat interval of 100 ms and an election timeout under 500 ms is asking for permanent elections. Something like heartbeat 250 ms, election timeout 2.5–5 s, keeping broadcastTime ≪ electionTimeout ≪ MTBF with real margin. Also: leader placement now matters enormously, because every write costs a cross-DC round trip to the nearest quorum member.

The Δ you assume, and how you defend it: Δ is the inter-DC p999 RTT plus the worst intra-host pause you tolerate (GC, fsync). Defend it by measuring both continuously and alarming on the ratio to your election timeout, not on absolute values — because the absolutes change every time you move hosts or resize heaps.

What to monitor, and the single metric to keep: keep time since the commit index last advanced. It catches grey failure, leader loss, quorum loss and disk stalls in one number, and it is the one that maps directly to customer impact. Everything else — election rate, GC p99, fsync p99, peer RTT — is diagnosis after that alarm fires.

Documented behaviour when the assumption breaks: writes fail with a retryable error and a clear reason; reads continue from a local replica with an explicit staleness bound, if your consistency model allows it; there is a tested, rehearsed procedure for a DC loss. Write it down before you need it.

What to tell the product team: durability and correctness are unconditional; write availability is not, and there is no upper bound on config-write latency during a leader change. Give them a realistic p99, an explicit statement that the tail is unbounded, and a design that does not put config writes on the critical path of a customer request.

Three nodes or five: five, spread 2/2/1 across three DCs so that losing any one DC leaves a quorum. This has nothing to do with FLP — it is purely about crash tolerance and failure-domain independence. FLP is unaffected by the count.

8.2 The reply about a 500 ms hard bound. (model answer, ~190 words)

We can't promise a hard upper bound on confirmation time, and I want to be straight about why, because it isn't something we can engineer around with more servers.

Confirming a transfer means several machines have to agree it happened. When one machine doesn't hear from another, there's no way for it to tell whether that machine has died or is just slow — the evidence is identical. So it waits a while and then assumes the worst. That waiting period is a guess, and while it plays out, confirmation is delayed. This is a proven limit, not a gap in our implementation; it applies to every system that works this way, including the ones our competitors use.

What we can promise: we never confirm a transfer that didn't happen, and we never lose a confirmed one. Those hold no matter what the network does.

What I'd offer instead of a hard bound: a 500 ms target at p99.9, published and monitored; a clear retryable error rather than a hang when we miss it; and a UI that shows "processing" rather than blocking. If the 500 ms is a regulatory requirement, let's talk — that changes the design, not the timeout.

8.3 A single leader with no election, promoted by a human.

What it buys with respect to FLP: quite a lot, honestly. It replaces the automatic failure detector — the component that cannot be correct — with a human operator, who can look at dashboards, phone the datacenter, and apply judgement that no timeout can encode. You have not defeated the theorem; you have moved the unavoidable guess to somebody who is much better at guessing, and who can be held accountable for it. In FLP terms it is a very slow, very accurate oracle (escape 4).

What it costs: your recovery time is now measured in minutes-to-hours instead of seconds, and it depends on somebody being awake. You have added a dependency on a human to your availability story, and humans are unavailable in correlated ways (holidays, incidents, 3am). You also need a safe promotion procedure — fencing the old leader — or a human error turns directly into split brain, and humans under pressure make exactly that error.

When it is the right call: when a wrong automatic failover is far more expensive than a slow manual one. Primary/standby databases holding money, systems where promotion has side effects that cannot be undone, and small deployments where nobody is going to operate a consensus cluster correctly anyway. It is also the right first version of a system: manual failover that works beats automatic failover that has never been tested. The wrong version is automatic failover with no fencing — that is strictly worse than both.
