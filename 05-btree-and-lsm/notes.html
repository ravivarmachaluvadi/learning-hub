1. WHY — one level down is where the answers live

Every store you cataloged in the last lab keeps its data in one of two structures: PostgreSQL, MySQL, SQLite, Oracle, and nearly every secondary index anywhere sit on B-trees; Cassandra, RocksDB, LevelDB, HBase, ScyllaDB — and, as embedded engines, half the "new" databases of the last decade — sit on LSM-trees. That means the mysterious production symptoms of both worlds — "why did our p99 spike after we switched to UUID keys?", "what is this compaction backlog alert?", "why do deletes make Cassandra slower?", "why does Postgres need VACUUM?" — are not database trivia. They are the mechanics of these two structures leaking upward. Understand the two machines and those symptoms become predictable; tune a database and you are, almost always, turning knobs on one of these two trees.

2. PROBLEM — Kabir and the invoices

Meera's bookshop (from the last lab) has grown enough to hire Kabir, who runs the back office. His job, reduced to its essence, is two operations on paper invoices, all day:

  1. File each invoice as it arrives — dozens per hour, in no particular order (invoice #4831, then #212, then #9905...).
  2. Find any invoice the moment someone asks — "pull up #3117" — and sometimes a range: "all invoices from #3000 to #3200."

Two innocent-looking requirements. Everything in this lab — both trees, every knob, every production incident — falls out of the tension between them.

3. NAIVE SOLUTION — the pile, then the sorted notebook

Try 1: the pile. Kabir drops each invoice on top of a pile. Filing is instant — the fastest possible write. But "find #3117" means digging through the whole pile, every time. With 10,000 invoices, every question is a 10,000-paper excavation. (You know this structure: it's the append-only log from the last lab — trick 7. Perfect for "what happened, in order"; hopeless for "find one thing.")

Try 2: keep everything sorted. Kabir binds the invoices into one giant sorted binder. Now "find #3117" is delightful: open the middle, too high or too low, halve again — binary search, ~14 peeks for 10,000 invoices instead of 10,000. Ranges are even better: find #3000, then read forward until #3200. Sorted order is the superpower for finding things.

4. FAILURE — sorted order and arriving order are enemies

Now invoice #4832 arrives, and it belongs in the middle of the binder. To insert one page in its sorted place, Kabir must shift every page after it — thousands of pages moved to file one invoice. The binder made finds cheap by making files ruinous.

Look at the shape of the dilemma, because it is the entire subject:

  • Writes arrive in random order and want to be cheap.
  • Reads want the data in sorted order.
  • Keeping data sorted at all times means paying a re-sorting cost on every single write.

And one more constraint, from the machine itself (you met it in the last lab): storage devices don't read and write single bytes — they move fixed-size chunks (pages, ~4–16 KB), and they are dramatically faster at sequential chunks than at scattered ones (your Machine B: seeks vs sequential blocks). Any real solution must respect chunk-sized, preferably sequential, I/O.

🔮 Predict: you're Kabir. You refuse to shift thousands of pages per invoice, and you refuse to dig through an unsorted pile. What furniture do you buy? (There are two famous answers. Think of at least one before revealing.)

Reveal Answer one: a filing cabinet — chop the sorted sequence into many small folders, each with slack space, so an insert only ever rearranges ONE folder. Answer two: an inbox tray — let invoices pile up unsorted but SMALL, sort the small pile at day's end into a neat stack, and merge stacks occasionally. The first is the B-tree. The second is the LSM-tree. Both are ways to keep things "sorted enough" without re-sorting the world per write — and the entire rest of this document is just these two ideas growing up.

5. THE REAL REQUIREMENT

In plain words, a storage engine must give Kabir:

Reads that behave as if the data were fully sorted, writes that cost a small bounded amount of work each, and I/O that arrives in page-sized — ideally sequential — chunks. (Plus, after a crash, nothing acknowledged is lost.)

No structure gets all of it for free. The B-tree and the LSM-tree are the two canonical ways to distribute the cost of sortedness: the B-tree pays a little on every write, at write time, in place; the LSM pays later, in bulk, in the background. Hold that sentence — it generates everything else.

6. KEY IDEA

Two inventions, one dilemma:

B-tree: cut the sorted data into thousands of small pages, each with slack space, and build a tree of signposts above them. An insert rearranges exactly one page — and when a page overflows, it splits in two, pushing one signpost up. Sortedness is repaired locally, immediately.

LSM-tree: stop repairing the sorted files altogether — they are immutable. Collect incoming writes in a small sorted buffer in memory; when it fills, write it out as a brand-new sorted file sequentially; and occasionally merge files in the background. Sortedness is repaired globally, later, in bulk.

7. TERMINOLOGY — names for what Kabir just invented

The load-bearing vocabulary. (From the last lab you already carry: page, compaction, tombstone, write-ahead log, B-tree/LSM as one-liners — those get upgraded here, not re-introduced.)

Node / page (upgraded). Plain: one folder in the cabinet. Precise: the fixed-size unit (say 8 KB) a B-tree reads or writes at once; holds sorted keys plus either pointers to child pages (an internal node) or the actual data (a leaf). Why the term: it aligns the tree's shape with the device's native chunk — one node = one disk fetch. Example: an 8 KB page holding ~200 keys of ~40 bytes each.

Fanout (a.k.a. branching factor / order). Plain: how many folders a signpost page can point at. Precise: children per internal node — hundreds in practice, because a node is a whole page full of routing values (each one a separator key: "below this value, go left"). Why it exists: fanout is what makes B-trees shallow: fanout 200 reaches 200³ = 8 million keys in 3 levels, ~1.6 billion in 4. A find costs one page read per level — so "3–4 page reads for a billion keys" is fanout doing its job. Connects to: exp1 of the last lab (the 5,344× lookup was a 3-level walk).

Split. Plain: a folder overflows, so you split its contents into two folders and add one line to the signpost above. Precise: when an insert lands in a full page, the page divides at its median; the median (or a separator) is promoted into the parent — which may itself overflow and split, possibly all the way up. When the root splits, the tree gains a level: B-trees grow from the top, which is why they stay balanced with no rebalancing pass. Example: in §10.

Memtable. Plain: the inbox tray — plus a clever detail: Kabir keeps the tray itself sorted as papers land (it's small; that's cheap). Precise: an in-memory sorted structure (skiplist/red-black tree) absorbing all writes; reads check it first; at a size threshold (say 64 MB) it becomes immutable and is written out. Why: it converts random-order writes into a batch that can be emitted sequentially — the whole LSM trick in one object.

SSTable (sorted string table) / sorted run. Plain: one day's sorted stack, stapled — never edited again. Precise: an immutable file of sorted key-value pairs, written once sequentially, read many times; a "newer" table's value for a key shadows any older table's. Why immutability matters: immutable files need no locks to read, can be scanned sequentially, and make crash recovery trivial (a half-written new file is just discarded).

Flush. Plain: day's end — sort the tray, staple it, shelve it. Precise: writing the frozen memtable to disk as a new SSTable, sequentially. This is the ONLY way user data reaches disk in an LSM (aside from the log below).

Compaction (upgraded). Plain: merging several stapled stacks into one, discarding superseded invoices. Precise: a background k-way merge of SSTables into fewer, larger ones, dropping shadowed values and (eventually) tombstones. It is deferred sorting — the work the B-tree does per-write, done here in bulk. The policy deciding what to merge when (size-tiered vs leveled, §10.6) is the LSM's most consequential knob.

Write-ahead log — WAL (upgraded). Plain: Kabir's carbon-copy book: scribble every invoice into it the moment it arrives, before filing — if fire takes the tray, the book replays it. Precise: an append-only file each write goes to (sequentially, cheaply) before the engine acknowledges; on restart, unflushed writes are replayed from it. Both engines carry one; the LSM's protects the memtable (RAM would otherwise lose acknowledged writes on crash), the B-tree's protects half-updated pages.

Amplification (read / write / space). Plain: how much extra work or room the engine spends per unit of useful work. Precise: write amp = bytes actually written to disk ÷ bytes of user data (a B-tree rewrites an 8 KB page for a 100-byte row: amp ≈ 80; an LSM rewrites data at every compaction: amp 5–30); read amp = places checked per lookup (B-tree: height, ~3–4; LSM: memtable + up to N tables); space amp = disk used ÷ live data (old versions not yet compacted; pages deliberately left with slack). Why the triad exists: it's the scorecard the two engines trade on — you never reduce all three; you choose which one bleeds. This lab's experiments measure all three on your machine.

Minor terms, one line each: B+ tree — the ubiquitous B-tree variant where all data lives in leaves and leaves are chained left-to-right (what databases actually use; what we build); separator key — the signpost value an internal node uses to route ("< 40 go left, ≥ 40 go right"); bloom filter and sparse index — two small LSM read accelerators, earned properly in §10.5; fill factor — how full pages are kept on purpose; write stall — an LSM refusing writes because compaction fell behind (§11).

Checkpoint — BEGINNER → INTERMEDIATE. You can explain the dilemma (sorted reads vs random writes vs chunked I/O) and both answers in furniture terms, and you know the cost scorecard's names. Next: the actual machinery, key by key — where splits cascade and why deletes are writes.

8. MENTAL MODELS

Simple model — Kabir's two offices. Office A (B-tree): a filing cabinet of small folders, each deliberately left one-third empty; a signpost card on each drawer; a master signpost on the wall. Every invoice goes straight into its one true folder; a full folder is split into two and the drawer card gains a line. Everything is always in its place; nothing is ever done "later." Office B (LSM): a sorted inbox tray on the desk, a carbon-copy book beside it, and shelves of stapled sorted stacks, newest on top. Nothing on the shelves is ever edited; finding means tray first, then stacks newest-first; night shifts merge stacks. Everything is done "later," in bulk.

Engineering model — two ways to spend the sorting budget:

B-tree LSM-tree
Disk layout mutable pages, updated in place immutable sorted files, append-only
Write path walk to leaf, edit 1 page (maybe split) append WAL + insert memtable; flush later
Read path root → internal → leaf (height page reads) memtable, then SSTables newest→oldest
Sortedness repaired locally, at write time globally, at compaction time
I/O personality small random reads/writes big sequential writes, scattered reads
Delete remove from page (space reclaimed later) write a tombstone (§10.4)
Crash safety WAL for page updates WAL for the memtable
Bleeds on the scorecard write amp (page-per-row), space slack read amp (many files), compaction I/O

The mapping: folder = page · drawer/wall signposts = internal nodes/root · folder split = page split · inbox tray = memtable · carbon-copy book = WAL · stapled stack = SSTable · night merge = compaction · "one-third empty folders" = fill factor · "invoice marked CANCELLED on a new sheet" = tombstone.

Where the analogy breaks. (1) Kabir splits a folder in seconds; a database splits a page while hundreds of threads are reading it — real engines need latches/lock-free schemes we deliberately omit (§15). (2) The night-shift merge sounds free; on a machine, compaction competes with live traffic for the same disk — it is the LSM's defining operational burden, not a janitorial detail (§11). (3) Paper folders hold ~20 invoices; real pages hold hundreds of keys, which is why real trees are 3–4 levels, not 6–10 — intuition from small drawings understates fanout's power. (4) No filing cabinet grows a new top; B-trees do — the root split is where the analogy simply stops. Watch it happen in the visualization instead.

10. ACTUAL MECHANISM

We build each machine the way a smart engineer would: the obvious version first, then watch it break, then the refinement. Every "weird" detail of the finished engines is the scar of one of these breaks. Tiny pages (max 4 keys) throughout, so the examples fit on screen — real pages hold hundreds; the logic is identical (that, and everything else we simplify, is listed honestly in §15).

10.1 B-tree v1 — sorted pages and a signpost page

Chop Kabir's sorted binder into pages of ≤ 4 keys, and keep one signpost page listing each page's first key:

signpost: [3→P1, 15→P2, 28→P3, 44→P4, 61→P5, 80→P6]
P1:[3,9,12] P2:[15,20,25] P3:[28,31,37] P4:[44,52] P5:[61,70] P6:[80,91,95]

Search 31: read the signpost (1 page read) — 31 ≥ 28 and < 44, so P3; read P3 (1 page read); binary-search inside; found. Two page reads, any key. Insert 33: route to P3, slide it in: [28,31,33,37] — one page touched, because pages carry slack space. The binder's shift-everything disaster is gone.

But two seeds of trouble: P3 is now full, and with a million pages the signpost page itself is a million entries — it no longer fits in one page. The second problem answers itself with a beautiful move: a signpost page is just a sorted page of keys — so give the signposts their own signposts. Pages of signposts, recursively, until one page rules them all: the root. Internal nodes and leaves, exactly the mermaid shape above. Search = one page read per level, and fanout keeps levels tiny (fanout 200 → 4 levels covers ~1.6 B keys).

10.2 The split — how a B-tree absorbs overflow and stays balanced

Now the first seed. Insert 35 into full P3:[28,31,33,37]:

  1. P3 splits at its median into P3a:[28,31] and P3b:[33,35,37].
  2. The separator key 33 is promoted into the parent: it now routes "< 33 → P3a, ≥ 33 → P3b". One page became two; the parent grew by one key.
  3. If the parent was itself full, it splits the same way, promoting a separator further up — a split cascade.
  4. If the cascade reaches a full root, the root splits and a new root is created above it with a single separator. The tree grows from the top — this is why every leaf is always at exactly the same depth, i.e. why B-trees are perfectly balanced without any rebalancing procedure. (Watch this in Machine 1; it is genuinely satisfying.)

🔮 Predict: Kabir inserts keys in strictly increasing order — 1, 2, 3, 4, ... 1000 (an auto-increment primary key). Which page does every insert hit, and are splits more or less frequent than with random keys?

Reveal Every insert lands in the rightmost leaf — the only page that ever splits. Same number of splits overall (the data must still fit), but every touched page is the same hot page (cached, no random I/O), and real engines special-case rightmost splits to leave the left page ~100% full instead of half — so sequential inserts also pack pages tighter. Random keys (UUIDv4) hit a different random leaf each time: cold pages, scattered writes, half-full pages everywhere. You measured the seek half of this in the last lab's Machine B; experiments/Exp2KeyOrder.java measures the splits-and-fill half in this one.

Two finishing touches turn this into the B+ tree every database actually ships: keep all values in the leaves (internal nodes hold only separator keys — so they pack enormous fanout), and chain the leaves left-to-right. The chain makes range scans trivial: walk down to the first leaf of the range (3–4 page reads), then follow sibling links sequentially — no tree re-walking. "Orders #3000–#3200" is one descent plus a linked-list stroll.

The B-tree's bill. Every write rewrites its whole page (a 100-byte row in an 8 KB page = write amp ~80). Every page keeps slack (engines aim for ~70% full — that's fill factor; the slack is the product: it's what makes the next insert cheap) = space amp ~1.4. In exchange: reads are a fixed, tiny height page reads — the read-optimized half of the fork you already knew. Now the write-optimized half.

10.3 LSM v1 — memtable + flush... and the read bill arrives

Office B. Writes append one line to the WAL (sequential, cheap, crash insurance) and land in the memtable, kept sorted in RAM. At the threshold — ours flushes at 6 entries, RocksDB at ~64 MB — the memtable is written out, already sorted, as an immutable SSTable, in one sequential pass. Writes are gorgeous: append + RAM insert, deferred bulk flush. Machine B of the last lab, now with named parts.

Read get(52) must respect one law — newest wins: check the memtable first, then SSTables newest → oldest, stop at the first hit. And here is v1's bill: after a week, 400 SSTables. A read checks the memtable, then potentially all 400 files. Read amplification isn't a footnote — untreated, it's fatal. Three refinements (10.4–10.6) are all treatments.

10.4 Deletes — why removing data means writing data

Delete invoice 20. In the B-tree: walk to the leaf, remove it (the page just gets emptier — real engines reclaim/merge underfull pages lazily; so does ours; §15). But in the LSM, key 20 might live in SSTable 3 — which is immutable. You cannot remove what you cannot touch. The only thing an LSM can ever do is write something newer: so it writes a tombstone — a special "20 is deleted" record — to the memtable. Newest-wins does the rest: any read finds the tombstone before the old value and reports "not found." The old value physically dies later, when compaction merges the two files. This is the last lab's one-line "deletes write tombstones", now with its why: tombstones are the price of immutability — and their production sting (§11) is that a delete-heavy workload makes files bigger and reads slower until compaction catches up.

10.5 Two read accelerators — paying tiny RAM for huge I/O

Problem 1: absent keys. get(99999) — never written. No file contains it, so no file check can stop early: the read probes every SSTable. Absent-key reads are the worst reads — and common (caches asking "is X here?"). Fix: a bloom filter — a small bitmap per SSTable, built at flush from the table's keys, answering "possibly here" or "definitely not here" with a tunable false-positive rate (~1% at ~10 bits/key). "Definitely not" skips the file without touching disk: absent-key reads collapse from N file probes to ~zero (experiment 3 measures it). One-way certainty is the whole trick — it can never wrongly skip, only occasionally probe in vain.

Problem 2: finding a key inside one big file. A 2 GB SSTable is sorted, but binary-searching raw disk is many scattered reads. Fix: a sparse index — at flush, remember every ~64th key's file offset in RAM; a lookup finds the surrounding pair, seeks once, reads one block. Sorted files + tiny in-RAM maps = one seek per file probed.

10.6 Compaction policy — the LSM's steering wheel

Refinement three attacks the file count itself. Merging is easy (all files are sorted: k-way merge, drop shadowed values and expired tombstones — our src/Lsm.java does exactly this). The consequential question is which files, when — the policy:

  • Size-tiered (STCS) — merge ~4 similar-sized tables into one bigger one, tier by tier (our toy; Cassandra's default). Write-cheap: each key is rewritten only ~log(N) times as it graduates through tiers. But reads may still probe a table per tier, and space spikes: shadowed copies survive across tiers, and a big merge needs room for input and output (the "needs 2× disk" alert of §11).
  • Leveled (LCS) — organize tables into levels L1, L2, L3... where each level is ~10× bigger and holds non-overlapping key ranges; new data trickles down level by level, merging into the ranges it overlaps (RocksDB's default). Reads touch ≤ 1 table per level (~3–4 total, almost B-tree-like!) and space amp stays ~1.1 — but each key is rewritten at every level: write amp ~10× the tier policy.

Read the pair against the scorecard: STCS bleeds reads and space; LCS bleeds writes. Choosing a compaction policy is choosing which amplification bleeds — the fork inside the fork. (Time-series stores add a third policy — time-windowed — merging only same-time-window files, because time-keyed data never shadows across windows; your last lab's ⑧ in new clothes.)

🔮 Predict: an LSM ingests at full speed all day. Compaction is throttled to be "nice" to foreground traffic and quietly falls behind — file count climbs. What breaks first: correctness, reads, or writes?

Reveal Reads first — every extra file is another probe on every get; p99 read latency climbs smoothly. Correctness never breaks (newest-wins holds regardless of file count). Writes break last but hardest: at a file-count threshold the engine stalls writes on purpose (RocksDB write stalls, Cassandra backpressure) because accepting more ingest would dig the hole deeper. "My writes suddenly hang" on an LSM almost always decodes to "compaction lost the race hours ago" — §11's F-L1.

10.7 Crash safety — the WAL closes both loops

The B-tree edits pages in place — a crash mid-split could leave the tree inconsistent; the WAL (plus recovery replay) makes page updates redoable. The LSM holds acknowledged writes in RAM — a crash would eat them; the WAL replays them into a fresh memtable at startup (our crash_recovery_replays_wal test does exactly this: build, "crash", recover, verify). Once a memtable is safely flushed as an SSTable, its WAL segment is deleted — the log stays short. Immutable SSTables themselves need no recovery: a half-written one is simply discarded.

You can walk both write paths and both read paths with real keys, explain splits, tombstones, bloom filters, sparse indexes, and the two compaction policies — each as the fix to a failure you watched. 11. FAILURE SCENARIOS — the mechanics leaking into production

Four per engine. Each: how to create it → where to watch it → what happens and why → what production does about it. Every one is a §10 mechanism wearing a pager.

F-B1 — the random-key insert storm (the UUID incident). Create: switch a table's primary key from auto-increment to random UUIDv4 under heavy insert load. Watch: experiments/Exp2KeyOrder.java, or Machine 1 with "random" order. What/why: every insert now lands in a different leaf — cold pages dragged into cache, scattered page writes, splits sprayed across the whole tree leaving every page ~half-full (the tree is bigger and its cache hit rate worse for the same data). Sequential keys hit one hot rightmost leaf. Production: the classic "we switched to UUIDs and p99 doubled"; fixes are time-ordered keys (UUIDv7/ULID) or accepting the bill. Guarantee intact: correctness, balance. Lost: the write-cost assumption your capacity plan was built on.

F-B2 — bloat: the tree that grows while the data doesn't. Create: heavy updates/deletes on a B-tree table for weeks (bonus in Postgres: MVCC keeps old row versions until VACUUM). Watch: run Exp2KeyOrder.java and compare live keys vs pages held. What/why: deletes empty pages but pages are rarely merged back; splits never un-split; slack plus dead entries accumulate — space amp climbs and scans read mostly air. Production: Postgres autovacuum (tuned, monitored — "dead tuples" and bloat queries), REINDEX/pg_repack as the deep clean; InnoDB purge threads. The mirror image: this is the B-tree's version of the LSM's shadowed data — both engines carry garbage; they differ in who collects it and when.

F-B3 — the crash mid-split (torn write). Create: kill the process between "leaf split" and "parent updated." Watch: conceptually in Machine 1 (a split is visibly multi-page); in code, crash_recovery_replays_wal shows the WAL discipline. What/why: a multi-page change half-applied = a tree pointing at pages that aren't what the parent believes — without protection, corruption. Production: WAL + replay makes the split redoable; Postgres additionally writes full page images after each checkpoint (torn-page defense). The rule the WAL enforces everywhere: acknowledge nothing the log can't replay.

F-L1 — compaction falls behind (the write stall). Create: sustained ingest above compaction throughput (throttle compaction, as §10.6's prediction did). Watch: Exp1Amplification.java prints the file count growing; Machine 2's shelf visibly deepens if you never compact. What/why: reads slow first (a probe per extra file), then the engine stalls writes at a threshold to protect itself. Production: RocksDB pending_compaction_bytes / stall counters, Cassandra sstables per read p99 — the on-call graphs for this failure; remedies are rate-limiting ingest, more compaction parallelism, or faster disks. The deep lesson: an LSM's write speed is a loan from the future; compaction is the repayment schedule.

F-L2 — the tombstone storm (the queue anti-pattern, decoded). Create: use an LSM table as a queue — insert, read, delete, thousands/sec, then range-scan for the next work item. Watch: tombstones_slow_range_reads (tests double as this experiment). What/why: every consumed item leaves a tombstone; a range scan must read through the tombstones to find live entries — soon each poll wades through millions of dead records; and tombstones can't be dropped at the first compaction (an older copy of the key might still exist in a not-yet-merged file — drop the tombstone early and the old value resurrects; this is why Cassandra keeps them for gc_grace_seconds, default 10 days). Production: Cassandra logs tombstone warnings and aborts pathological reads; the real fix is the last lab's rule — queues are jobs, not facts; use a queue.

F-L3 — the 2 a.m. disk-full during compaction. Create: run size-tiered compaction with the disk at 60% full and a big tier merge due. Watch: Exp1Amplification.java prints space amp before/after compaction. What/why: a merge writes its output before deleting its inputs (crash safety demands it), so peak usage ≈ inputs + output — a large STCS merge can transiently need ~2× the data size. Production: capacity rule of thumb — keep STCS disks ≤ 50–60% full; or run leveled compaction and pay in write amp instead. The scorecard again: you chose where to bleed.

F-L4 — reads for keys that don't exist. Create: point a cache-miss-heavy workload ("is X here?" mostly answering no) at an LSM with bloom filters disabled or under-sized. Watch: Exp3Bloom.java — the same 2,000 absent-key reads with blooms on and off. What/why: absence can't stop early; without blooms every file is probed. Production: bloom bits-per-key is a first-class tuning knob (RocksDB ~10 bits/key ≈ 1% false positives); monitoring watches "bloom useful" ratios.

12. CORRECTNESS — invariants, guarantees, and the bill

B-tree invariants (all enforced in src/BTree.java, named in comments):

  1. Sortedness — keys sorted within every page; separators route correctly (everything in child i is < separator i).
  2. Uniform depth — every leaf at the same depth, forever, because the tree only grows by root split. Balance is not maintained; it is structural.
  3. Bounded occupancy — pages hold ≤ max keys (and, in real engines, ≥ ~half after merges); hence height ≤ log_fanout(N) and every operation is O(height) page I/Os.

LSM invariants (enforced in src/Lsm.java):

  1. Newest wins — the read path's order (memtable, then tables newest→oldest, first hit wins) defines the current value; tombstones are just values that mean "gone."
  2. Immutability — flushed files never change; all mutation is new files.
  3. WAL-before-ack — no write is acknowledged before it is in the log; recovery = replay.
  4. Compaction is logically invisible — merging preserves exactly the newest-wins answer for every key (it only makes the physical set smaller). A compaction that changes any read's answer is a bug, full stop.

What is guaranteed, what is not, what it costs:

Guarantees Does NOT guarantee The bill
B-tree O(height) point & range reads, always; predictable latency; data always in sorted place cheap random-key writes; full pages write amp (page per row), space slack, random write I/O
LSM cheap durable writes at any rate (until the loan is called); sequential disk usage; immutable files flat read cost (grows with file count); prompt space reclaim; cheap deletes read amp, compaction I/O forever, tombstone debt, space spikes

And the honest summary of the whole fork: neither tree is faster — they move the same sorting work to different moments. The B-tree sorts at write time so reads are flat; the LSM sorts at compaction time so writes are flat. Pick per workload — which you already knew from the landscape lab; now you know the machinery that makes it true.

Checkpoint — ENGINEERING model complete. Invariants, failure modes, and the cost model are yours. What remains: touch the machines (§13–14) and map the toys to InnoDB/RocksDB/Cassandra knobs (§15).

Commonly confused, one sentence each: B-tree vs B+ tree — in the B+ variant (what databases ship) internal nodes hold only separators and all data sits in chained leaves; "B-tree" in conversation almost always means B+ tree. LSM vs append-only log — the log stores history in arrival order; the LSM uses append-only files as a technique to store current state in sorted order. Compaction vs VACUUM — the same garbage-collection job on opposite trees: compaction merges immutable files dropping shadowed data; VACUUM reclaims dead row versions inside mutable pages. Memtable flush vs checkpoint — a flush writes new data to its permanent sorted home (LSM); a checkpoint writes already-applied dirty pages back so the WAL can be trimmed (B-tree world). Bloom filter vs index — an index says where a key IS; a bloom filter can only swear where it ISN'T.

Rung 1

1.1 One big sorted file makes finding wonderful (binary search, and ranges are just "read forward") but inserting ruinous: a new key belongs in the middle, so everything after it must shift — thousands of pages moved to file one invoice. Writes arrive in random order; reads want sorted order; keeping data sorted at all times means paying a re-sort on every write. And disks move fixed-size chunks, fastest when they're sequential, so the fix has to respect chunks too.

1.2 Both answer "how do we stay sorted enough without re-sorting the world on every write?" The B-tree repairs sortedness at write time, locally: data lives in small pages with slack, so one insert rearranges one page, and a full page splits in two. The LSM refuses to repair anything at write time — it buffers writes in memory, dumps them as immutable sorted files, and merges those files later, in bulk, in the background.

1.3 The correction: neither is faster; they move the same sorting work to different moments, and each pays in a different currency. The B-tree pays write amplification (a whole page rewritten per row — 119.2× in exp1) to buy flat, predictable reads (exactly height page reads, always). The LSM pays read amplification and perpetual background compaction I/O to buy cheap sequential writes. "Modern databases use LSM" is also just false: Postgres, MySQL, SQLite, Oracle and nearly every secondary index on earth are B-trees.

1.4 Once a file has been written out, it is never changed again — so there is physically nothing to erase; the only move available is to write something newer that hides the old value, and reads honor "newest first." That newer record is a tombstone: a marker meaning "this key is deleted." The old value dies later, when a merge rewrites both records into one file.

Rung 2

2.1 The single top box is the root; middle rows are signpost pages; the bottom row holds the data. Numbers inside a signpost are directions, not data — separator keys saying "keys below this value go left." The same value may appear both as a separator above and as real data in a leaf.

2.2 Identical cost: a miss walks the same root→leaf path and reads the same number of pages as a hit. The tree can only conclude "absent" by reaching the one leaf where the key would live. (Contrast the LSM, where absence is the worst case — nothing can stop early, which is why bloom filters exist.)

2.3 The memtable is empty and the WAL counter is back to 0: the flush made that data durable in an immutable SSTable, so the log entries protecting it are no longer needed and were truncated. The WAL only ever holds writes that exist nowhere but RAM.

2.4 Both copies are visible on the shelf — the newer one in the file nearer the top. The read wins for the topmost, because the shelf is ordered newest-first and the read stops at the first hit; the "last read checked" counter confirms how few places it needed.

Rung 3

3.1 Measured: height 2 first at insert 5 (the root leaf overflows and splits), height 3 at insert 13, height 4 at insert 37. Note the growth: each level takes ~3× more keys than the last — that's fanout, and it's why real trees with fanout ~200 are 3–4 levels for billions of rows.

3.2 Measured order (smallest → largest): LSM read amp 2.0, B-tree read amp 6.0, LSM write amp 2.5, B-tree write amp 119.2. The headline: the two engines' write costs differ by ~48×, their read costs by ~3× — and the LSM's read number is only that good because bloom filters turn most "places considered" into zero-I/O skips (0.9 real probes/get).

Rung 5

5.1

public static int predictedHeight(int nKeys, int maxKeys) {
    int fanout = maxKeys + 1;       // a page holding maxKeys keys has maxKeys+1 children
    int height = 1;                 // height 1 = a single leaf page
    long capacity = maxKeys;
    while (capacity < nKeys) {
        height++;
        capacity *= fanout;         // each new level multiplies the leaf count
    }
    return height;
}

The insight is the loop's *=: capacity grows multiplicatively per level, so height grows logarithmically — predictedHeight(1_000_000, 100) is 3. That is the entire reason a billion-row index is 4 page reads deep.

5.2

public static List<Entry> newestWinsMerge(List<Table> tables) {
    // TreeMap keeps the output sorted by key for free
    TreeMap<String, Integer> winningSeq = new TreeMap<>();
    TreeMap<String, String> winningValue = new TreeMap<>();
    for (Table table : tables) {
        for (Entry e : table.entries()) {
            // the SEQ decides which table is newer — never the list position
            Integer best = winningSeq.get(e.key());
            if (best == null || table.seq() > best) {
                winningSeq.put(e.key(), table.seq());
                winningValue.put(e.key(), e.value());
            }
        }
    }
    List<Entry> out = new ArrayList<>();
    for (var e : winningValue.entrySet()) {
        if (!Lsm.TOMBSTONE.equals(e.getValue())) out.add(new Entry(e.getKey(), e.getValue()));
    }
    return out;
}

The trap the tests set: an implementation that just iterates the list and lets later entries overwrite earlier ones passes four tests and fails newest_table_wins_regardless_of_list_position. (That trap is not hypothetical — it caught the first draft of this very solution.)

Rung 6

6.1 The expression is the loop that keeps the entry with the largest negPrio():

for (int x = i + 1; x < j; x++) {
    if (merged.get(x).negPrio() > winner.negPrio()) winner = merged.get(x);
}

Entries are tagged Tagged(key, -seq, value), so negPrio() is the negated sequence number: taking the largest -seq selects the smallest seq — the oldest table. The correct code just takes the first element of the group (merged.get(i)), because the k-way merge already ordered same-key entries newest-first.

6.2 compaction_preserves_every_answer_and_reclaims_space in tests/LsmTest.java — the test that asserts every get() answers identically before and after compaction. Point fresh() at BrokenLsm and the suite reports 5 passed, 1 failed, that one. Nothing else notices: writes, reads, flushes, file counts, and disk shrinkage all look perfectly healthy.

6.3 LSM invariant 4, compaction is logically invisible (§12) — and, through it, invariant 1 (newest wins), since the merge output no longer reflects the newest write.

6.4 Measured output of ExpDebugBroken: every deleted key comes back (k00: null -> v0) and every overwritten key reverts to its original value (k01: v31 -> v1) — all 10 answers change. The user sees deleted data resurrect and recent updates silently roll back — overnight, with no error anywhere. It's among the worst possible bugs because (a) it's silent — no crash, no log line, no failing health check; (b) it destroys data that was correctly written and acknowledged; (c) it's retroactive, so backups taken after the compaction contain the corruption; and (d) for a deletion-for-compliance workflow, resurrecting the record is a legal problem, not just a technical one.

Rung 7

7.1 Neither loss nor corruption. Verified by experiment: after flushing 4 keys and then replaying the stale WAL, all four keys read back correctly and the fifth (post-flush) write survives too. The replayed operations simply re-insert values that already exist in the SSTable; because the memtable is newer, its identical value wins, and the next flush writes a duplicate that compaction later collapses. The property that makes this safe is idempotence: put(k, v) applied twice equals put(k, v) applied once — the same property that makes at-least-once delivery survivable in the transactional-outbox lab. This is why WAL recovery is allowed to be sloppy about replaying too much, but never too little. (Deletes are idempotent for the same reason: a tombstone written twice is one tombstone.)

7.2 Merge two sibling leaves when their combined key count fits in one page (real engines use a threshold like "below half full") — but only siblings sharing a parent, since merging non-siblings would break the separator structure. The parent must then drop the separator that used to route between them, which can leave the parent underfull, cascading upward; if the root ends up with one child, the root is removed and the tree shrinks a level (the exact mirror of the root split). The new failure mode: splitting only ever adds structure, so a reader walking down always finds a valid page; merging deletes pages while readers may still be descending toward them, so merge requires coordination (latching, deferred freeing, or epoch-based reclamation) that splitting doesn't. That difficulty is precisely why real engines defer or skip merging and let VACUUM-style processes clean up instead — the bloat in Exp2KeyOrder is a deliberate tradeoff, not an oversight.

7.3

  • Throttle ingest — fixes the root cause immediately (compaction wins the race), costs producer latency or backpressure upstream, and could break tonight by pushing the queue backup into an upstream system that has no backpressure handling (a Kafka consumer lag alert, a full outbox table).
  • Raise compaction concurrency — fixes the backlog fastest, costs disk bandwidth and CPU shared with foreground reads, and could break tonight by making p99 reads worse before better, or by exhausting the disk headroom you're already short of (71% full, and STCS merges need room for input + output — F-L3).
  • Switch STCS → leveled — fixes read amp and space amp durably, costs a large one-time rewrite of the entire dataset (the most dangerous option tonight: it multiplies write amp exactly when the system is already behind) and roughly 10× ongoing write amp. The order a careful on-call engineer picks: throttle first (stop digging), then concurrency (dig out), schedule the policy change for a calm window with disk headroom secured. Never the policy switch first.

Rung 8

8.1 One strong answer (grade on reasoning, not agreement):

  • Engine: LSM. 500k writes/sec of never-updated data is the write-firehose workload; a B-tree's per-write page rewrite is exactly wrong here.
  • Key design: partition/primary key (channel_id, timestamp) — channel as the prefix so one channel's messages are contiguous, timestamp descending as the clustering order so "last 50 in channel X" is a single short sequential read from the start of the range, and the time range query is one contiguous scan. Time-bucket the key (channel_id, day) if channels can grow unbounded, or a hot channel becomes an unbounded partition.
  • Compaction: time-windowed (TWCS). Messages are immutable and time-keyed, so files from different windows never shadow each other — merging them would be pure wasted I/O. TWCS also makes retention nearly free: an expired window is a whole file to drop, not millions of tombstones.
  • Bloom filters: ~10 bits/key, but note they matter less here than usual — queries are ranges within a known channel, not point lookups of possibly-absent keys. Don't over-provision RAM for them.
  • Retention/deletes: compliance deletes are rare and individual — those get tombstones. Bulk expiry must NOT be done with deletes (that's the tombstone storm, F-L2); use TTL/window dropping.
  • Deliberately accepted bad number: space amplification (immutable files keep superseded data until a merge) and read amp for old messages, which may sit in several time windows. Both are cheap in exchange for the ingest rate, and neither hurts the dominant query, which reads the newest window.

8.2 Mechanism: UUIDv4 primary keys are uniformly random, so every insert targets a random leaf. In InnoDB the primary key is a clustered index — the row itself lives inside that B-tree — so each insert dirties a random 16 KB page rather than appending to a hot right edge. The working set of recently-written pages explodes (exp2: 8 cold pages vs 914), so the buffer pool thrashes, page writes scatter, and splits leave pages ~half full, inflating the tree and worsening cache hit ratio further — a feedback loop that matches all three symptoms (write latency, cache hit ratio, and the fact that tripling volume quadrupled latency: superlinear). Evidence to gather: buffer pool hit ratio over time; pages dirtied per insert; index size vs data size (bloat); average leaf fill factor; whether p99 correlates with checkpoint flushes; and the decisive one — compare insert latency for a table with sequential keys under the same load. Three fixes: (1) UUIDv7/ULID (time-ordered) primary keys — restores the hot right edge, cheap for new tables, but requires a migration for the existing 4 TB and any external references to old ids; (2) auto-increment PK with the UUID as a secondary unique index — best write locality, but the secondary index still takes random inserts (smaller, so cheaper) and application lookups change; (3) buy your way out — more RAM so the working set fits — real, immediate, and merely postpones the problem as data grows. Proof the fix worked: p99 insert latency and pages-dirtied-per-insert at matched write volume, plus buffer pool hit ratio recovering. Would MyRocks (LSM) fix it? Yes for this symptom — an LSM is indifferent to key order (exp2's LSM columns), so random UUIDs stop hurting writes. But it would break other things: range scans and point reads get more expensive (read amp), you inherit compaction as a permanent operational burden (F-L1, F-L3) including the disk-headroom rule, secondary-index and transaction behavior differ, and you'd be migrating 4 TB onto a less familiar engine to solve a problem that a key-format change also solves. Correct answer: fix the keys; consider the engine only if the workload is genuinely write-dominated beyond what a B-tree can serve.
