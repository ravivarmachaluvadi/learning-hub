Lab 2 · The Ground Floor

Communication Protocols — how systems talk, and how to choose

Tell a friend: Priya is building Tiffin, a food-delivery app, and five very different conversations have to work inside it. Asha asks for a menu and then watches her order move; Meera's kitchen screen lights up when an order lands; PayFlow reports that the card cleared; billing must hear every order while it is down. Every famous protocol name, from REST and gRPC to WebSocket and Kafka, is a ready-made answer to one of those five conversation shapes. You choose by asking five questions: who speaks first, one answer or many, one listener or many, whose code sits at each end, and must the answer come now. Pick wrong and the app is slow, expensive, or quietly loses orders in every train tunnel.

The playground for this lab is visualization.html (double-click; it works offline). Three runnable experiments live in experiments/. Everything about how the lab was built, and where it sits among your other labs, is in section 19 at the end.

BEGINNER MODEL

1. Why are there ten ways to make an API call?

In one breath: Every protocol name you have heard moves bytes between programs. Each one exists because it fixes a specific pain the previous one caused. And the one you pick shapes your whole system, so it is worth getting right.

Priya opens a system-design discussion and a parade of names walks past: REST, GraphQL, gRPC, WebSockets, SSE, webhooks, MQTT, Kafka… At some level they all do the same job. They move bytes between programs. So why do all of them exist? And why do interviewers and architects care so much which one she picks?

Because the pick is structural. A protocol choice decides how her servers are deployed. It decides what her load balancers must support. It decides how clients handle failures, how much battery a phone burns, and how painful the next feature is. Choose polling where you needed push and you can 100× your server load. Choose WebSockets where a plain HTTP call would do and you buy months of reconnect-and-scale plumbing for nothing.

A protocol is also not a function you refactor in an afternoon. It is a contract with other people's code: other teams, other companies, millions of installed apps. A wrong pick calcifies.

The good news is that the parade is not ten random inventions. Each name is the answer to one specific pain that an earlier, simpler choice caused. By the end of this document you will look at any "A needs to talk to B" problem and derive the right protocol from five questions. Just as importantly, you will be able to say out loud when a fashionable protocol is the wrong answer.

2. What does Priya's app need to say, and to whom?

In one breath: Forget computers for a minute. Tiffin has five conversations to hold, and they are five different shapes: a letter, a phone call, a radio broadcast, a doorbell, and a mailbox.

Priya is building Tiffin, a food-delivery service. Ignore computers for a moment and just list who needs to tell what to whom.

  1. Asha asks for the menu. Asha is a customer. She asks: "What does Biryani House sell?" Tiffin answers. One question, one answer, done. It is like sending a letter and getting a reply.
  2. Asha watches her order. "Is my food cooked yet? Has the rider picked it up? Where is Arjun the rider right now?" The news changes every few seconds, and Tiffin knows first. Asha should not have to keep asking. It is like a phone call that stays open, where either side can speak.
  3. Meera's kitchen screen shows new orders. Meera runs Biryani House. Her kitchen screen, and every other restaurant's screen, must light up the moment an order lands. One event, many listeners. It is like a radio broadcast: the station speaks once, and every tuned-in receiver hears it.
  4. PayFlow confirms the payment. Tiffin uses an outside company, PayFlow, to charge cards. PayFlow finds out that the payment cleared seconds later, on its own schedule, and must tell Tiffin. Tiffin cannot keep a phone line open to a company it does not control. So PayFlow has to ring Tiffin's doorbell: come to Tiffin's address and announce the news.
  5. The order flows through Tiffin's own kitchen of services. Inside Tiffin, the ordering system must tell billing, notifications, and analytics about every order. It must do that even if analytics is down for maintenance right now. It is like leaving a message in a mailbox the recipient empties when they are back.

Five different shapes of conversation: ask-and-answer, open line, broadcast, doorbell, mailbox. Priya's question, and this whole lab's question, is this. How do computers hold each of these conversations, and what goes wrong when you use the wrong shape?

🔮 Predict: Analytics is switched off for an hour of maintenance. During that hour Asha places an order. Which of the five shapes can still get the news to analytics once it wakes up, and which ones lose it for good?

Reveal

Only the mailbox. A letter needs someone to read it. A phone call needs both ends awake. A radio broadcast is heard by whoever is tuned in at that second, and by nobody else, ever. A doorbell rung at an empty house is a doorbell wasted. The mailbox is the one shape where the message waits.

If you said "the radio, because analytics can listen to a recording", you have just invented the tape recorder that section 8 attaches to the radio. Plain radio has no memory. That difference is the whole reason two families of message systems exist, and section 10.9 splits them.

3. Why not just connect and send the data?

In one breath: The operating system gives every program a two-way pipe to another machine. Priya's first plan is to shout her own phrases down that pipe. It works. Then it breaks in four ways.

Priya knows the operating system gives every program a primitive for talking over the network. You open a connection to another machine and write bytes into it. The other side reads bytes out. It is a two-way pipe. This primitive is called a socket. It is the one bit of jargon we need this early, because everything else in this lab is built on top of it.

So here is the naive plan. Dev, who builds Tiffin's Android app, opens a connection to Priya's server and sends the text GIVE ME THE MENU FOR BIRYANI HOUSE. The server sends back the menu as text. Invent little phrases for each need. Done?

It genuinely works. You will run exactly this in experiments/Exp01HttpIsJustText.java. And it is how everything worked in the early days. But watch it break.

4. What breaks when you just send bytes?

In one breath: The pipe does not say where one message ends. Nobody else speaks Priya's invented phrases. Every conversation shape has to be rebuilt by hand. And nobody wrote down what happens when the line drops.

Problem 1: where does one message end?

A socket is a stream of bytes, not a sequence of messages. Suppose the server sends Asha the menu (500 bytes) and then a "restaurant is closing soon" notice (80 bytes). Dev's app might read 580 bytes in one gulp. Or 90 bytes, then 490. The network chops and merges freely.

🔮 Predict: Priya's server writes two JSON objects back-to-back into the socket: {"menu": [...]} then {"notice": "closing soon"}. The app does one read. What does it get?

Reveal

Very possibly both blobs glued together: {"menu":[...]}{"notice":...}. That is not valid JSON, and it crashes a naive parser. Or the app gets the first blob plus half of the second.

If you said "one object per read, obviously", you are assuming the pipe keeps your parcels apart. It does not. The reliable-delivery service under the byte pipe is called TCP (it gets its proper introduction in section 7). TCP guarantees the bytes arrive in order and uncorrupted. It says nothing about where your message boundaries are.

Every protocol in this lab must solve this framing problem somehow: a length prefix, a delimiter line, or a "close the connection when done" rule. Experiment 01 shows this live.

Problem 2: who else speaks Priya's language?

Priya invented GIVE ME THE MENU. Dev's Android app team must now implement it. So must PayFlow, the payment company. So must the caching proxy her cloud provider runs, the load balancer, the monitoring tools… none of which have ever heard of it. Every piece of the world's network infrastructure speaks standard protocols: caches, proxies, firewalls, browsers, test tools. A private language means rebuilding all of that yourself.

Problem 3: who builds the broadcast, the doorbell, and the mailbox?

Broadcast a new order to 5,000 kitchen screens like Meera's? Deliver a message to a service that is currently down? Notify an outside company behind its own firewall? Raw sockets give you none of this. You would hand-build subscription lists, retry queues, and authentication.

Problem 4: what happens when the line drops mid-order?

Asha's connection drops halfway through placing an order. Did the order go through? Can the app safely re-send? Who may speak first? A protocol is precisely an answer sheet for these questions. Priya has not written one.

Here is the deeper discovery. "How do two computers talk" is really two separate problems. Getting bytes across reliably is the easy, solved part. Agreeing on what the bytes mean and how the conversation flows is the real work. That is what all those protocol names are competing to solve.

5. What does Priya actually need?

In one breath: A shared, standard rulebook for each of the five shapes. It must say where messages end, what they mean, which shape it serves, and what happens when things go wrong.

What Priya actually needs is a shared, standard set of conversation rules for each conversation shape from section 2, such that:

  1. Framing. Both sides always know where a message starts and ends.
  2. Meaning. Requests, replies, success and failure are expressed the same way by everyone. Then strangers' code, and their infrastructure, can join the conversation.
  3. Shape fit. Ask/answer, open line, broadcast, doorbell, and mailbox each get rules designed for that shape, not hacked onto the wrong one.
  4. Failure rules. Every protocol states what happens on a dropped connection, a repeated message, a slow consumer.

Keep this list. Every protocol below is graded against it.

6. What is the key idea?

In one breath: Protocols stack in layers. Above the byte-delivery layer, every protocol is a reusable answer to one conversation shape, and five questions tell you which one you need.

Protocols are layered, and above the transport layer every protocol is just a reusable answer to one conversation shape. You choose one not by fashion but by five questions. Who starts the conversation? One answer or a stream? One listener or many? Do both ends live in your company? Must the answer come back right now, or later?

Everything with a name is a point in that small design space: HTTP, GraphQL, gRPC, WebSocket, SSE, webhook, MQTT. Once you can place a protocol on those five axes, you can derive when to use it and when it will hurt, without memorizing anything.

7. What are the words engineers use for what just happened?

In one breath: You have already met every idea in this section. Now each one gets its proper name, in the order Priya bumped into it.

Walk back through Priya's naive attempt and name each thing as she trips over it. Every term also lands in the glossary, section 18.

What is a protocol, really?

Priya's answer sheet from Problem 4 has a name. A protocol is the agreed rules of a conversation: who speaks first, what the phrases mean, how it ends. More precisely, it is a specification of message formats and exchange rules that two independent implementations can follow and still interoperate.

Why does the word exist? To separate the agreement from any one program that implements it. curl, Chrome, and a Java service all "speak HTTP" because the rules live in a document (an RFC), not in code. For example, HTTP says a request line looks like GET /menu HTTP/1.1.

What carries the bytes before they mean anything?

Under Priya's pipe is a delivery service that moves bytes between machines before any meaning is attached. Engineers call it the transport, and the operating system provides it. It comes in two flavours.

TCP gives a reliable, ordered byte stream. Lost pieces are re-sent and order is restored. UDP sends independent packets with no delivery or ordering promise, but also no setup delay and no retransmission stalls. Why two? Because reliability costs time, and some conversations would rather lose a syllable than pause. A video call is one. HTTP runs on TCP; live game traffic and DNS lookups run on UDP.

TCP is what made the naive plan in section 4 "genuinely work". And framing is the part TCP explicitly does not do.

What is the plug the program speaks into?

The socket is the plug. Technically it is the operating system's endpoint object for one network connection. You write() bytes in, and the peer read()s them out. Programs need a handle for "this one conversation" that is distinct from the machine, because one server holds thousands of sockets at once. Experiment 01 opens a socket to port 8000 and types HTTP into it by hand.

One warning. "WebSocket" is a different, higher-level thing (section 10.6) that merely borrowed the word.

What is the shape half the internet assumes?

Asha's menu question is request/response: ask once, get one answer, conversation over. The client starts the exchange; the server never speaks unprompted. Naming this shape lets us say precisely what push protocols change. On the wire it looks like GET /menu going out and 200 OK plus a body coming back.

Why does the number of questions matter more than their size?

Every exchange costs one trip there and one trip back. That is a round trip (RTT): network latency times two, paid per exchange regardless of how small the messages are. Within one data centre a round trip is about 1 ms. From Asha's phone to a server it is 50–300 ms. So the number of round trips, not the number of bytes, usually decides how slow an app feels.

Situation Round trip Sequential calls Time spent commuting
Inside one data centre ~1 ms 4 ~4 ms, nobody notices
Asha's phone, good signal 200 ms 4 800 ms before the screen can draw

This single number explains why GraphQL and HTTP/2 exist (section 10).

Does the news come to you, or do you go and get it?

Asha's order screen can keep asking "anything new?" That is pull. Or Tiffin can speak on an existing channel the moment there is news. That is push. Technically the question is whether the server can initiate delivery on an existing channel, or the client must poll with fresh requests. This is axis number one of the key idea, and the axis most often gotten wrong. Real-time features built on pull are the classic scaling accident. Order status via polling is pull; via WebSocket it is push.

How do objects become bytes?

Turning in-memory objects into bytes and back is serialization. JSON is one encoding (human-readable text). Protocol Buffers is another (compact binary, schema-driven). This is separate from the protocol: the same HTTP call can carry JSON, XML, or binary. Envelope and letter are independent choices.

In one breath: Carry the five human conversation shapes for intuition and five precise axes for decisions. Then learn the four places the human picture lies to you, because that is where the bugs live.

The simple picture: five human conversations

This is the one you carry around. Every protocol is one of five shapes.

Human picture Shape Protocols that implement it

Radio / newsletter one sender, many subscribed listeners SSE to browsers; MQTT, Kafka behind the scenes
Doorbell outsider shows up at your address with news Webhook

The engineer's picture: five axes

This is the precise one. A protocol is a position on five axes. Read the middle column as the question you ask about any conversation.

# Axis The question The two ends
① initiation who starts it? pull vs push
② cardinality how many answers, how many listeners? one answer vs a stream; one listener vs many
③ connection lifetime does the line outlive one exchange? per-request vs persistent
④ encoding what do the bytes look like? text vs binary; free-form vs schema-checked
⑤ coupling must both ends be alive at the same moment? synchronous (answer now) vs asynchronous (answer later, receiver may be down)

How do the two pictures line up?

Shape Initiation Cardinality Connection Coupling

Phone call push and pull streams both ways persistent synchronous

The mailbox is the only shape where the receiver may be dead during sending. That is why it alone survives a downed consumer, which is exactly what the Predict in section 2 asked.

Where the story lies to you

Read this part twice. It is where bugs live.

Letters never get lost silently in the postal picture; requests do. When an HTTP call times out, you cannot know whether the server did the work. A real letter-writer would eventually get a "return to sender". Networks just go quiet. This is why idempotency, not hope, is the real safety mechanism (section 12).

A phone call, once connected, stays connected. A WebSocket does not. Real connections die every few minutes on mobile networks. The "open line" is actually "open line, plus automatic redial, plus a plan for what was missed while redialing". Teams that take the phone-call picture literally ship chat apps that drop messages on every train tunnel (section 11).

Radio listeners who miss a broadcast miss it forever. Kafka listeners do not. Log-based queues keep history and let a listener rewind. The radio picture is right for live fan-out (SSE, MQTT fire-and-forget) and wrong for replayable streams. You saw the replayable kind in event-sourcing.

A doorbell ring is heard at most once; webhooks arrive at least once. Payment providers like PayFlow retry until you acknowledge. So the same doorbell can ring five times for one event, sometimes out of order (section 11).

INTERMEDIATE MODEL

10. How does each protocol actually work, and what pain made it?

In one breath: Read this section as one continuous story. Each protocol exists because the one before it hurt Priya somewhere specific. For each you get the real wire bytes, where it shines, and when not to use it.

The "when not" part is what interviews and architecture reviews actually probe, so it gets as much space as the "how".

10.1 Raw TCP sockets: what is the floor everything stands on?

This is Priya's naive plan from section 3, taken seriously. The operating system gives you the reliable byte pipe. You define everything else: framing, meaning, failure rules. Framing is typically a length prefix ("next message is 512 bytes") or a delimiter (a newline).

Where is raw TCP genuinely used? Database wire protocols: Postgres, MySQL and Redis each speak a custom TCP protocol, and that is what a "database driver" implements. Game servers. Exchange trading feeds. In every one of those cases a team controls both ends, needs every microsecond and every byte, and is willing to write and version its own protocol spec.

When should Priya not use it? Almost always. She would rebuild framing, auth, encryption, proxying, and tooling that HTTP gives her free. And no third party could talk to Tiffin without her custom library. Raw TCP is the last resort, not the pro move.

Run experiments/Exp01HttpIsJustText.java now. It talks raw TCP to a real HTTP server, so you can see with your own eyes that everything below is "just text over a socket", plus discipline.

10.2 HTTP: how did the letter get standardized, and why did it win?

Asha's letter, written in a format everyone agrees on. One request, one response, in a rigidly framed text format. This is literally what travels over the socket when Dev's app asks for a menu:

GET /restaurants/42/menu HTTP/1.1
Host: api.tiffin.com
Accept: application/json
                                          ← blank line = "headers done" (framing!)

HTTP/1.1 200 OK
Content-Type: application/json
Content-Length: 57                        ← framing: exactly 57 body bytes follow
Cache-Control: max-age=300

{"restaurant": "Biryani House", "items": [{"dosa": 120}]}

Check it against the list in section 5. Framing is answered by Content-Length and the blank line. Shared meaning comes from verbs and status codes, in the table below. Failure rules come along too: "GET is idempotent, so anything in the middle may retry it."

HTTP word What it means Examples
verb the action GET reads, POST creates, PUT replaces, DELETE removes
2xx status success 200 OK
3xx status go somewhere else redirect
4xx status your mistake wrong URL, bad request
5xx status the server's mistake crashed handler

Why did HTTP conquer the world? Because those shared semantics let infrastructure join the conversation. A CDN can cache any GET that carries Cache-Control without understanding Priya's business. A proxy can retry idempotent requests. A browser, a load balancer, curl, and your monitoring all speak it. This network effect, not technical elegance, is why "when in doubt, HTTP" is the correct default.

The versions tell a pain-driven story of their own. HTTP/1.1 handles one request at a time per connection. So browsers open about 6 parallel connections and still queue. Engineers call that head-of-line blocking at the request level. HTTP/2 (2015) multiplexes many concurrent streams over one TCP connection in binary frames, and compresses headers.

🔮 Predict: HTTP/2 puts 6 streams on one TCP connection. One packet belonging to stream 3 is lost in transit. What happens to streams 1, 2, and 4 to 6?

Reveal

They all stall. TCP promises in-order delivery of the whole byte stream. Until the lost packet is re-sent, TCP refuses to hand any later bytes to the application, even bytes belonging to unrelated streams. Head-of-line blocking moved down a layer, from HTTP into TCP itself.

If you said "only stream 3 waits", you were describing what HTTP/2 wanted. On TCP that is unfixable, which is why HTTP/3 abandons TCP entirely. It runs on QUIC, a transport built on UDP that keeps streams independent (a lost packet stalls only its own stream) and merges the TCP and TLS handshakes into one round trip. Google, YouTube, Facebook and Cloudflare serve most traffic this way today.

10.3 REST: why is it a discipline and not a protocol?

REST is a style for using HTTP, not a new protocol. Model your domain as resources with URLs. Manipulate them with the standard verbs. Keep the server stateless, meaning each request is self-contained. That last rule is what lets any of 50 identical servers answer any request, which is what makes horizontal scaling trivial. Tiffin's API in this style:

GET    /restaurants/42          → read one
GET    /restaurants?city=pune   → list/filter
POST   /orders                  → create (body: the new order)
PATCH  /orders/97               → partial update
DELETE /orders/97               → cancel

Reach for REST for public and partner APIs (Stripe, GitHub, Twilio, the entire API economy), for anything third parties integrate with, for browser and mobile backends of ordinary CRUD shape, and for anything that benefits from HTTP caching. It is boring, universal, and debuggable with curl. It is the right default.

Where would Priya be wrong to pick it?

  • Real-time push. REST has no way for the server to speak first. Period.
  • Screens that aggregate many resources. This is the pain that births GraphQL, next.
  • High-volume internal service-to-service traffic. JSON parsing and untyped contracts get expensive. That pain births gRPC (section 10.5).
  • Also know SOAP, the 2000s XML predecessor with rigid contracts and heavy tooling. It survives in banking and enterprise integrations. You read it; you do not choose it for new work.

10.4 GraphQL: what pain in REST gave birth to it?

Tiffin's app home screen needs four things: Asha's profile, her active order, that order's restaurant, and Arjun's location.

🔮 Predict: As clean REST that is GET /me, GET /orders/active, GET /restaurants/42, GET /riders/7. Calls 2 to 4 depend on call 1's response, so they cannot all run in parallel. On a phone with a 200 ms round trip, how long until the screen renders? And what fraction of the returned fields does the screen actually display?

Reveal

Two sequential rounds minimum, and likely three, before rendering. Screens typically use well under half the fields returned.

| Rounds | Time before the screen can draw | What it means | |---|---|---| | 2 | ≈400 ms | the best case, with calls 2 to 4 in parallel | | 3 | ≈600 ms | the likely case, when one of them depends on another |

If you said "200 ms, it's one screen", you counted screens, not round trips. Engineers have names for both halves of this pain. Over-fetching: full objects arrive when you needed 3 fields. Under-fetching: no single endpoint had everything, forcing the extra calls. Facebook's mobile team hit exactly this wall in 2012. Their fix became GraphQL, open-sourced in 2015. experiments/Exp02RoundTrips.java measures this, and the chatty version loses by roughly the predicted margin.

How does it work? One endpoint (POST /graphql). The client sends a typed query describing exactly the shape it wants, and the server resolves it in one round trip:

query HomeScreen {
  me { name }
  activeOrder {
    status
    restaurant { name }
    rider { location { lat lng } }
  }
}

The response is JSON mirroring the query, nothing more. A schema (types, fields) is the contract. Clients discover it by introspection, and tooling such as auto-complete and type-checked clients falls out of that.

Reach for GraphQL when mobile or web apps aggregate many resources per screen. Reach for it when many different frontends with different data needs share one backend. That is the killer case: iOS, Android, web, watch, each querying its own shape, while the backend team ships nothing. Reach for it in public APIs serving diverse integrators (GitHub's v4 API, Shopify).

Where would Priya be wrong to pick it? This list wins interviews.

  • Server-to-server internal calls. Services need fixed, known shapes. GraphQL's flexibility is dead weight there, and gRPC is faster and typed (section 10.5).
  • Simple CRUD with one client. You pay the complexity tax (schema, resolvers, query-cost limiting) for flexibility nobody uses.
  • HTTP-cache-friendly content. Everything is POST to one URL, so CDNs and browser caches, which key on GET plus URL, go blind. Client-side caches like Apollo's exist, but you rebuild caching rather than inherit it.
  • Naive resolvers hide N+1 catastrophes. orders { rider { name } } can fire one database query per order. You must add batching (DataLoader). And because clients compose arbitrary queries, a public GraphQL API needs query depth and cost limits. Otherwise one hostile query, { orders { items { restaurant { orders ... }}}}, is a denial of service (section 11).
  • File uploads and binary. Bolted on and awkward. Plain HTTP does it natively.

10.5 gRPC: why was JSON over HTTP/1.1 too slow inside the walls?

Inside Tiffin, the orders service calls the pricing service maybe 5,000 times per second. Costs that do not matter on a public API become the bill. JSON is verbose to ship and CPU-heavy to parse. Nothing checks that pricing did not rename a field until production. Every call re-pays HTTP/1.1's one-at-a-time connection handling.

gRPC (Google, 2015, grown from their internal "Stubby") is three decisions welded together.

Decision What it gives you Why it matters inside the walls
Protocol Buffers for encoding you write a schema; a compiler generates client and server code in any language the wire format is compact binary using field numbers, not names: typically 3–10× smaller than JSON and far cheaper to parse. Renaming a field incompatibly now breaks the build, not prod
HTTP/2 transport multiplexed, persistent connections no more one-at-a-time
Streaming as a first-class citizen four call shapes: unary (request/response), server-streaming, client-streaming, bidirectional Arjun's location can flow as a stream, not a series of calls 
service Pricing {
  rpc Quote (QuoteRequest) returns (QuoteReply);            // unary
  rpc WatchSurge (Area) returns (stream SurgeUpdate);       // server-streaming
}
message QuoteRequest { int64 restaurant_id = 1; repeated int64 item_ids = 2; }

Calling it feels like a local function call: stub.Quote(req). That is the "RPC" (remote procedure call) philosophy: hide the network behind a function. Contrast REST's philosophy, which is to expose the network's semantics. Keep both in your head. The hidden network still fails (section 11).

Reach for gRPC for internal microservice meshes (Netflix, Uber, Dropbox and Spotify run thousands of gRPC services). Reach for it in polyglot shops, where the schema generates consistent clients in Java, Go, Python and more. Reach for it for low-latency streaming between services, and for mobile-to-backend links where you control both ends. It also powers etcd's and CockroachDB's client protocols; you met etcd in the paxos lab's production notes.

Where would Priya be wrong to pick it?

  • Browsers. Browser JavaScript cannot speak raw gRPC, because it has no control over HTTP/2 frames. You need a translation proxy (grpc-web or Envoy). For browser-facing APIs, use REST or GraphQL.
  • Public third-party APIs. Integrators expect curl-able JSON they can poke in a terminal. Handing them a .proto file and a codegen step is friction. Stripe exposes REST, not gRPC, on purpose.
  • The debuggability tax. Binary payloads mean you need grpcurl and reflection where curl and eyeballs used to do.
  • Anywhere humans read the payloads. Config, webhooks, logs.

10.6 WebSocket: what if the server needs to speak first?

Asha's order tracking under pure HTTP means Dev's app asks every 2 seconds. "Status?" "Same." "Status?" "Same." Engineers call this polling.

🔮 Predict: 10,000 customers like Asha are polling every 2 s, and an order's status changes about every 60 s. (a) How many requests per second hit the server? (b) What fraction return nothing new? (c) What is the worst-case staleness of the screen?

Reveal

| Question | Answer | What it means | |---|---|---| | (a) load | 5,000 req/s | every one paying full HTTP, auth and database cost | | (b) waste | ~97% carry no news | thousands of requests per second of pure waste | | (c) staleness | up to the full 2 s interval | Asha's screen can be 2 s behind |

If you said "tighten the interval to fix (c)", notice that (a) and (b) get worse. Loosen it and (c) gets worse. Polling has no good setting. experiments/Exp03PollVsPush.java measures this trade directly.

The stopgap era used long polling. The server holds the request open until news arrives, then the client immediately re-asks. That gives real-time-ish latency, but it still costs one full HTTP cycle per event and a held-open request per client. A bridge, not a destination.

How does a WebSocket work? It starts life as an HTTP request that asks to change protocols:

GET /live HTTP/1.1
Host: api.tiffin.com
Upgrade: websocket                 ← "let's stop doing HTTP on this connection"
Connection: Upgrade
Sec-WebSocket-Key: dGhlIHNhbXBsZQ==

HTTP/1.1 101 Switching Protocols   ← server agrees

After the 101, the connection is no longer HTTP. It is a persistent, cheap, two-way message pipe with framing handled for you. Either side may write at any time, until someone closes it. The handshake trick is why WebSockets traverse firewalls and reuse port 443 like ordinary web traffic.

Reach for it for anything both real-time and two-way: chat (Slack), collaborative editing (Figma, Google Docs), multiplayer games, live trading UIs, and ride-hailing driver-to-dispatch links like Arjun's.

Where would Priya be wrong to pick it?

  • One-way flows. If the client only listens, SSE (next) does it with none of the following costs.
  • You inherit a distributed-state problem. Each open socket lives on one server. So load balancers must be connection-aware. Deploys and restarts drop every connection. Pushing to Asha means finding which server holds her socket, which is where Redis pub/sub or a message bus between your own servers comes in. And HTTP middleware no longer applies: caching, retries, auth-per-request. You re-invent request IDs, acks, and heartbeats inside your own message format.
  • The reconnect burden is yours. Mobile connections die constantly. Every client needs redial-with-backoff plus "what did I miss?" recovery (section 11). This is the phone-call lie from section 8, turned into a work item.
  • Occasional updates. Status that changes every few minutes does not earn a persistent connection. Poll politely, or use SSE.

10.7 SSE: what is the one-way push you probably want?

Server-Sent Events. Dev's app makes a normal HTTP GET. The server simply never finishes the response, trickling events as text lines whenever it has news about Asha's order:

GET /orders/97/events            ← ordinary HTTP request

HTTP/1.1 200 OK
Content-Type: text/event-stream  ← "this response never ends"

data: {"status": "COOKING"}

data: {"status": "PICKED_UP"}
                                  ← blank line = event boundary (framing again!)
id: 42
data: {"status": "DELIVERED"}

Because it is HTTP, everything HTTP-shaped still works: same auth, same load balancers, plain-text debugging. Browsers have it built in (EventSource) with automatic reconnection. On reconnect the client sends Last-Event-ID: 42, and the server can replay what was missed. The "what did I miss?" problem that WebSockets make you hand-build is half-solved in the protocol.

🔮 Predict: Asha's train enters a tunnel for forty seconds. Her order goes from COOKING to PICKED_UP to DELIVERED while she is underground. Compare two versions of the app, one on WebSocket and one on SSE. When the signal comes back, what does each one have to do to show her all three transitions?

Reveal

The SSE version does almost nothing. The browser's EventSource reconnects on its own and sends Last-Event-ID with the last event it saw. Tiffin's server replays everything after it. The WebSocket version has to do all of that in Dev's code: notice the death, redial with backoff, then ask "what did I miss?" in a format he invented himself.

If you said "TCP re-sends the missed data", remember that TCP only retransmits inside one connection. The tunnel killed the connection. Nothing below the application knows what Asha last saw. Section 11.2 walks the WebSocket version step by step, and the playground's two "Train tunnel" scenarios run both.

Reach for SSE for live one-way feeds: dashboards, notifications, sports scores, order tracking. And for the example you use daily: LLM token streaming. ChatGPT and the Claude API stream responses as SSE. The client asks once (a POST), and tokens trickle back as events. Under HTTP/1.1, browsers capped connections at about 6 per domain, which throttled SSE-heavy pages. HTTP/2 multiplexing erased that.

Where would Priya be wrong to pick it? When the client needs to send frequently (that is WebSocket territory). For binary data (SSE is text-only). For non-browser consumers she fully controls, where gRPC server-streaming is the typed equivalent inside the walls.

10.8 Webhooks: how does the doorbell work across company walls?

Back to shape 4 from section 2. PayFlow learns that Asha's card cleared 3 s after checkout. Tiffin cannot hold sockets open to every partner, and PayFlow will not let thousands of merchants poll its API every second.

The answer is an inversion. Tiffin gives PayFlow a URL, and PayFlow makes an ordinary HTTP POST to Tiffin when the event happens. The consumer becomes the server:

POST /callbacks/payflow HTTP/1.1        ← PayFlow calling Tiffin
PayFlow-Signature: t=1724832000,v1=5257a8ee...   ← HMAC: proves sender + content
Content-Type: application/json

{"event": "payment.succeeded", "order_id": 97, "amount": 45000}

Tiffin answers 200 OK quickly: acknowledge now, process asynchronously, do the real work off a queue. If no timely 2xx comes back, PayFlow retries with backoff, often for days. This is exactly how Stripe payment events, GitHub push events, and Twilio message-status callbacks work.

Reach for webhooks for cross-organization event delivery where the consumer can host a public HTTPS endpoint. It is the standard answer for "SaaS tells its customers something happened".

Where would Priya be wrong to pick it? For consumers behind firewalls or NAT, or on devices, because they cannot host an endpoint (flip to polling, SSE, or MQTT). For high-frequency streams, because per-event HTTP calls do not amortize (hand out a stream or a queue instead). And never naively. Retries mean duplicates and reordering are contractual, so an endpoint that is not idempotent will double-ship an order (section 11). And an unverified signature means anyone who finds your URL can fake "payment succeeded".

10.9 Message queues and event streams: how does the mailbox work?

Shape 5 from section 2. Ordering must tell billing, notifications, and analytics. With any synchronous protocol above, if analytics is down, either checkout fails with it or the event is simply gone. Synchronous protocols require both ends alive at the same moment. Sometimes that is the wrong requirement altogether.

Insert a broker: a server whose only job is holding messages. Producers publish to it and move on. Consumers take messages out on their own schedule. The broker retries until a consumer acknowledges. You know this pattern's guarantees cold from transactional-outbox. Delivery is at-least-once, so consumers must be idempotent.

There are two families, plus one small protocol that deserves its own line.

Family The message is… Who consumes it The human picture
Task queues (RabbitMQ with the AMQP protocol; SQS) a job one worker; deleted when acked the mailbox, exactly
Event logs (Kafka) a fact appended to a durable, ordered log many independent consumer groups, each at its own pace, able to rewind the radio with a tape recorder from section 8
MQTT a tiny binary pub/sub message topic subscribers over IoT and flaky mobile links a radio for devices on a SIM card

Kafka's rewindable log is the natural carrier for event-sourcing and cqrs event feeds.

MQTT is built for IoT and flaky mobile links. It has topic subscriptions, three delivery-guarantee levels (QoS 0, 1 and 2), "last will" messages that announce a device's death, and headers measured in bytes, because a sensor on a SIM card pays per byte. Smart meters and vehicle telemetry use it. Facebook Messenger famously used MQTT on mobile for battery-cheap push.

Reach for a queue to decouple services in time (the consumer may be down), to absorb bursts (the queue soaks up a spike the consumer drains later), for fan-out to many internal consumers, and for background jobs.

Where would Priya be wrong to pick it? When the caller needs the answer in this request. A price quote is RPC territory; a queue adds a broker hop and an ops burden for negative value. When request/response is faked over two queues with correlation IDs and timeout logic, because she has rebuilt RPC, badly. And in tiny systems, where a broker is one more thing to run, monitor, and upgrade.

10.10 UDP, QUIC, WebRTC: when is losing a packet better than waiting?

Some conversations tolerate loss but not delay. In a video call, a re-sent 50 ms-old audio packet is worse than silence. TCP's retransmission stalls (the reveal in section 10.2) are exactly wrong there. So latency-critical media runs on UDP and handles loss at the application layer.

Who How it copes with loss Why not TCP
WebRTC (browser-to-browser audio, video and data: Meet, Discord voice) jitter buffers and codecs that survive gaps a late syllable is worse than a missing one
most competitive multiplayer games custom UDP state sync; a lost position update is superseded by the next one anyway waiting for a re-send freezes the game
DNS one tiny question, one tiny answer a TCP handshake would triple the cost
QUIC (section 10.2) UDP's foundation with reliability rebuilt per stream TCP's promises without TCP's head-of-line stall

Where would Priya be wrong to pick it? Anything needing ordered completeness: payments, APIs. She would re-implement TCP herself, poorly.

11. What breaks, and what does each protocol do about it?

In one breath: Six things go wrong for Tiffin. Each one is a short story: what happened, what you would see, why, and which guarantee survived. The playground's chaos controls run three of them live.

The four requirements from section 5 get real here. The playground's chaos controls ("Cut the connection", and the server-down and endpoint-down scenarios) run scenarios 1, 2 and 4 live.

11.1 What happens when the POST times out?

This one hits every synchronous protocol. Asha taps "place order". Dev's app sends the request and the connection dies before the response comes back. To watch it, press the playground's "Cut the connection" control while a reply is in flight.

🔮 Predict: The request timed out. Which of these is true? (a) Tiffin never received the order. (b) Tiffin placed the order and only the reply died. (c) You cannot tell.

Reveal

(c), and that is the whole problem. One round trip carries both the action and its confirmation. When the trip is cut, the client cannot distinguish "server never got it" from "server did it; reply died".

If you said (a), "a timeout means it failed", you are the reason Asha gets charged twice. The obvious response is to retry. But a blind retry of POST /orders double-orders.

The fix works on REST, GraphQL and gRPC alike. The client sends an idempotency key: a client-generated unique ID. The server stores the key together with the result, and on a repeat it replays the stored result instead of doing the work again. Stripe's API works exactly this way.

Piece In words On the wire
the key a unique ID the client makes up before the first attempt Idempotency-Key: a91f…
the store server remembers key → result a row per key
the replay same key again, same stored answer, no second order identical response

What did the protocol keep? At-least-once attempt. What must you add yourself? Exactly-once effect. It is the same medicine as the consumer side of transactional-outbox, and this problem follows you across every protocol in this lab.

11.2 What happens when the WebSocket dies on a moving train?

Asha is on a train, watching Arjun's location move on a WebSocket. The train enters a tunnel. In the playground, run WebSocket and press "Cut the connection (train tunnel)" mid-stream.

What happens? Arjun's location updates silently stop. Neither side is told promptly. TCP death is discovered, not announced. So both ends need heartbeats (ping/pong) to notice within seconds.

Then Dev's client must reconnect with exponential backoff plus jitter. Why jitter? Because 10k clients redialing simultaneously after a server restart is a self-inflicted DDoS. Engineers call that the thundering herd. Then the client must resynchronize: either re-fetch full state ("where is Arjun now?") or replay from a last-seen sequence number.

What did the protocol keep? Nothing. WebSocket gives you a pipe, not delivery semantics. The recovery story is application code. This is the single most under-estimated cost of choosing WebSockets. SSE's built-in Last-Event-ID reconnect is precisely this lesson, pre-baked for the one-way case.

11.3 What happens when a GraphQL query is hostile, or merely naive?

Someone sends a deeply nested query against Tiffin's unprotected resolvers. One request fans out to thousands of database queries (N+1 at each level), or an exponentially exploding join. One client request, whole-backend outage.

Why? The protocol's core feature, that the client composes the query, is also its attack surface. REST's fixed endpoints made cost predictable per endpoint. GraphQL removes that ceiling.

The production response is DataLoader-style batching, query depth limits, cost analysis before execution, and persisted-queries-only for public APIs. What was traded? Server-controlled cost was exchanged for client flexibility. You must claw it back explicitly.

11.4 What happens when the webhook endpoint is down for five minutes?

🔮 Predict: PayFlow POSTs payment.succeeded for Asha's order. Tiffin is mid-deploy and returns 503 for 5 minutes. Is the event lost? What arrives later, and in what order relative to a refund.issued fired 1 minute after it?

Reveal

Not lost. No 2xx means the provider queues the event and retries with backoff (Stripe: up to about 3 days). But when Tiffin recovers, the retried payment.succeeded may arrive after the fresher refund.issued. Retries destroy ordering, and overlapping retries also produce duplicates.

If you said "lost, the server was down", you forgot that PayFlow keeps a mailbox on its side. If you said "they arrive in the order they happened", you trusted the doorbell to remember the sequence. It does not.

So a correct webhook consumer does four things. (a) It verifies the signature. (b) It acks 2xx immediately and processes asynchronously. (c) It dedupes by event ID. (d) It never trusts arrival order: either it uses the event's own timestamp or sequence, or it treats the webhook as a hint and re-fetches current truth from the provider's API ("trust but verify").

Kept: at-least-once delivery. Never promised: exactly-once, in-order.

11.5 What happens when the consumer is slow?

Meera's kitchen dashboard consumes 10 events/s from a server producing 1,000/s. Streams and queues both meet this.

🔮 Predict: After one second, 990 events have arrived faster than Meera's screen can draw them. Where are they?

Reveal

Somebody is holding them, and you did not choose who. There are only three places the difference can go: unbounded server-side buffering (memory death), dropping (data loss), or backpressure (telling the producer to slow down).

If you said "the network slows the producer automatically", that is true for raw TCP and only raw TCP. The table below says where each protocol lands.

Protocol Where the difference goes What you notice
raw TCP backpressure is built in: the window mechanism; a full receiver stalls the sender the sender blocks
WebSocket inherits TCP's backpressure your application rarely notices a full send-buffer until memory grows
gRPC streaming exposes per-stream flow control you can act on it
Kafka sidesteps it: the log persists to disk and slow consumers simply lag, visibly consumer lag, the same "how far behind is the read model?" metric from cqrs

The insight about guarantees: "the mailbox never fills" is an illusion. Every real mailbox has a size, and you must choose who suffers.

11.6 What happens when the network partitions?

From cap-theorem: the link between Dev's app and Tiffin's servers tears, for all of them at once.

Synchronous protocols (REST, GraphQL, gRPC, WebSocket) all fail visibly, with errors and timeouts. Recovery is retry plus idempotency (11.1). Asynchronous messaging fails invisibly. Producers keep publishing happily. Consumers just fall behind. The queue absorbs the partition.

That is the async superpower and its trap in one. Nothing errors, so only lag monitoring tells you something is wrong. Webhooks sit between: the provider eats the partition via its retry queue.

12. What can you honestly promise?

In one breath: No protocol delivers exactly once. Ordering only holds inside one connection. Someone has to notice failures, and every choice traded something away. Two laws hold across all of them.

Pull the threads together. For any protocol choice, be able to fill this ledger.

Is delivery guaranteed?

Family The honest answer
Synchronous protocols (REST, GraphQL, gRPC, WebSocket) no; you learn of failure and must retry
Queues and webhooks at-least-once; duplicates are contractual
Anything over a network exactly-once delivery is physically impossible over a lossy network; exactly-once effect is achievable, and only via idempotency at the receiver

🔮 Predict: A vendor's brochure promises "exactly-once delivery". Is that possible, and if it works, how must it be working?

Reveal

Not as delivery. Only as effect. Over a network that can lose the reply, the sender can never know whether the last message landed, so it must resend, so the receiver may see it twice. The only way "exactly once" can be true is that the receiver recognises the repeat and ignores it. That is idempotency at the receiver, wearing a brand name.

If you said "Kafka has an exactly-once mode", look at how it does it: the producer numbers its messages and the broker drops any number it has already seen. Same trick, inside the pipeline.

Is ordering guaranteed?

Where The honest answer
Within one TCP connection or stream yes
Across reconnects, retries, parallel requests, webhook redeliveries no; sequence numbers or versioning are your job
Kafka per-partition only

Who notices failure?

Style Who notices, and when
Synchronous the caller, immediately (timeout)
Asynchronous nobody, until lag or dead-letter-queue monitoring fires
Persistent connections nobody, until heartbeats notice

What was traded?

Protocol Gave up Got
REST flexibility universality
GraphQL server cost-control client flexibility
gRPC human debuggability speed and types

The two laws that hold for every protocol

  1. Timeout, retry and idempotency are one atom. A retry without a timeout never fires. A timeout without a retry just fails harder. A retry without idempotency corrupts data. Every production protocol stack implements all three, or it is wrong.
  2. Any persistent connection is distributed state. The moment a connection outlives one request, some server holds something for a specific client, and you own its lifecycle: discovery, failover, resync. Stateless request/response is what you give up, knowingly or not.

Solutions

Attempt EXERCISES.md first. Rungs 7–8 are worked discussions, not single correct answers.

Rung 1

1a. Polling means asking "anything new?" on a fixed clock. Between asks, news just sits there — so the user sees stale data (feels slow). And most asks find nothing — but each one still costs a full request: connection, authentication, a database check (wastes money). Push flips it: the user's device keeps one open channel, and the server speaks only when there is news. You pay once for the channel instead of repeatedly for empty answers, and news travels the moment it exists.

1b. Missing: (1) framing — the receiver can't tell where one message ends (TCP is a byte stream); (2) shared meaning — nobody else's code or infrastructure understands your invented phrases; (3) conversation shapes — broadcast, mailbox, doorbell all have to be hand-built; (4) failure rules — nothing says what a dropped connection implies or what may be safely retried.

1c. GraphQL solves one specific REST pain — many round trips and over-fetching when diverse frontends aggregate data — not REST in general. For public APIs, third-party integrators, and cacheable resources, REST remains the better fit (curl-able, CDN-cacheable, per-endpoint cost control). They coexist in the same company: GitHub ships both.

Rung 2

2a. Your system is the top lane — in webhook mode you are the receiver of calls, i.e. the server, even though you're the "customer" of PayFlow. The provider (bottom) initiates every data message; your only messages are 2xx acknowledgements. Red dots on the bottom lane = events occurring at the provider (news you don't know yet). Green ring on top = the moment that event was accepted by your endpoint.

2b. (i) The blank line (\r\n\r\n) ending the request's headers. (ii) Content-Length: 75 — exactly 75 body bytes follow. (iii) the status line HTTP/1.1 200 OK — a cache, proxy, or monitor knows the outcome without understanding the body.

2c.

  • SSE: client-initiated then server-push · one→one, stream of many events · persistent (one never-ending response) · text · synchronous (both alive).
  • gRPC unary: pull · one→one, single answer · persistent HTTP/2 connection reused across calls, but the call itself is per-request · binary, schema-checked · synchronous.
  • Kafka topic: push into broker, consumers pull at their own pace · one→many (consumer groups) · persistent broker connections · binary · asynchronous — the only one of the three where the consumer may be down while the producer sends.

Rung 3

3a. At 30 ms: ~4×30=120+ ms vs ~30+ ms — measured ~135 ms vs ~34 ms, ratio ≈ 4×. At 200 ms: measured ~825 ms vs ~206 ms — the ratio stays ~4× (it's the call count), but the absolute gap grows from ~100 ms to ~620 ms. Lesson: chattiness is a latency multiplier, so it hurts in proportion to how far away your users are.

3b. "Client asks" grows fastest (one every 2 s regardless of news); wasted/total ≈ 4/5 = ~80% (one useful poll per 10 s window of five).

3c. WebSocket: "lost forever" equals the events that fired during the gap (and any mid-flight when it died); SSE: zero lost, all delivered late. The responsible feature: SSE's Last-Event-ID reconnect replay — the client tells the server the last event it saw, and the server re-sends everything after it; WebSocket has no equivalent, so recovery is app code.

Rung 4

4a. At 0.1 s: detection latency drops to ~70 ms average (measured 45–105 ms), and request count grows ~10× — from 4–5 to ~35–41 — so you bought latency with server capacity (and phone battery). At 5 s: requests drop to 2, latency balloons to ~2 s average (measured 1.0–2.6 s). The law: polling trades staleness against waste on one dial; no setting removes both — removing both requires changing who initiates (push).

4b. Probably yes, one response per read (measured: 183 bytes, then 134 bytes — exactly one response each) — but it is a coincidence: both responses are small, the server wrote them separately, and loopback rarely fragments. Nothing in TCP guarantees it; under load, across real networks, with larger bodies, the boundaries move. That's why framing bugs pass every local test and explode in production — the classic "works on my machine" with a protocol-shaped cause.

Rung 5

5a. Sketch of the endpoint and client:

// server, a third context alongside /status and /events:
server.createContext("/status-wait", exchange -> {
    exchange.getRequestBody().readAllBytes();
    boolean fired = EVENT.await(8, TimeUnit.SECONDS);   // HOLD the request (long poll)
    byte[] body = ("{\"status\": \"" + (fired ? "DELIVERED" : "COOKING") + "\"}")
            .getBytes(StandardCharsets.UTF_8);
    // ... send 200 + Content-Length + body as in /status
});

// client:
static void longPoller() throws Exception {
    HttpClient client = HttpClient.newHttpClient();
    int requests = 0;
    while (true) {
        requests++;
        HttpRequest req = HttpRequest.newBuilder(
                URI.create("http://127.0.0.1:" + port + "/status-wait")).GET().build();
        if (client.send(req, HttpResponse.BodyHandlers.ofString()).body().contains("DELIVERED")) {
            LP_RESULT.put("latency", (System.nanoTime() - eventFiredAt) / 1e9);
            LP_RESULT.put("requests", requests);
            return;
        }
    }
}

Expected: latency within a few ms of SSE (the request is already parked at the server when the event fires), requests 1–2 (one per 8 s hold window). Measured: 1 request, ~1 ms — against SSE's ~0.3 ms and the 1 s poller's ~750 ms over 5 requests.

5b. The third framing rule: a delimiter — SSE ends each event with a blank line (\n\n); header blocks end with \r\n\r\n. (HTTP also has a fourth, chunked transfer encoding — length-prefixed pieces — if you found that, better still.)

Rung 6

6a.

  1. Line 5 reached only after line 4 — the ack is ~45 s late. Webhook providers time out in seconds; a timeout = "not delivered" = retry. Every delivery looks failed, hence "degraded". Fix: ack first, work later — validate, enqueue, return 200 in milliseconds (the queue from §10.9 is exactly the right tool downstream of a webhook).
  2. No idempotency (lines 3–4). Since retries are contractual, the same event arrives more than once — and each arrival ships the order again. Fix: dedupe on the provider's event ID (store processed IDs; skip repeats), and make ship_order refuse an already-shipped order (state-machine guard), since "at-least-once + non-idempotent = double effect" (§11.4, §12).
  3. No signature verification (line 1). Anyone who discovers the URL can POST payment.succeeded and get free food shipped. Fix: verify the HMAC signature header against the shared secret before parsing anything. (Bonus 4th: trusting arrival order — a retried payment.succeeded can arrive after payment.refunded; re-fetch current truth or compare event timestamps before acting.)

6b. Correct statement: the protocol never promised delivery — WebSocket is a pipe with framing, not a delivery contract; loss during disconnection is the application's to handle by design. Production clients add (1) heartbeats

  • reconnect with exponential backoff and jitter, and (2) resynchronization — sequence-numbered messages with a "give me everything after N" request on reconnect (or a full state re-fetch if N is too old).

Rung 7 (worked discussion)

7a. Client stores the last applied event's sequence number per order (monotonic, assigned by the server: 1, 2, 3…). On reconnect it sends it — with SSE this is literally Last-Event-ID: 17. Server case A (history covers it): replay events 18…now, in order, then continue live — the client buffers live events that race the replay and applies strictly by sequence number, which is what guarantees no out-of-order display. Server case B (truncated, i.e. gap > 100 events): send a snapshot (current full order state + current sequence number) instead of a replay; client replaces its state wholesale and resumes from there. Note this is the snapshot-vs-replay design straight from event-sourcing, reappearing at the protocol layer — same problem, same solution.

7b. Client: generate a UUID before the first attempt; send it as Idempotency-Key on the attempt and every retry (same key!); retry on timeout with backoff, max ~3 attempts, then surface "we're checking your order" and reconcile via GET. Server: atomically (unique constraint) record the key with the request's outcome in the same transaction as the order insert; on a duplicate key, return the stored response without re-executing; retain keys ≥ 24 h (Stripe's order of magnitude). Result: any number of retries produces exactly one charge and identical responses — the §12 atom (timeout + retry + idempotency) fully assembled.

Rung 8 (worked discussion — defensible, not unique)

  • Browse/search/order placement (app → backend): REST over HTTP/2-3, with idempotency keys on order placement. Runner-up: GraphQL — adopt it if the home screen's aggregation pain (exp 02) shows up across many client types. Deciding failure mode: timed-out POST double-ordering (§11.1). Metric: p99 latency + duplicate-key hit rate (a rising hit rate = users retrying = something upstream is slow).
  • Order tracking (backend → app): SSE, sequence numbers per order, snapshot fallback (7a). Runner-up: WebSocket — only if the client later needs high-frequency upstream too. Deciding failure mode: train-tunnel reconnect; SSE's replay is free. Metric: reconnect rate + replay-gap size.
  • Rider app: MQTT (QoS 1 upstream GPS, downstream dispatch offers on a per-rider topic), designed for flaky links and battery; last-will detects dead riders. Runner-up: WebSocket with app-level acks. Deciding failure mode: 60k mostly-idle flaky connections. Metric: undelivered-offer rate (offers expiring unanswered) and rider disconnect churn.
  • Restaurant dashboard (browser): SSE (one-way, browser-native, reconnect built in; 2 s budget is easy). Runner-up: WebSocket if order accept/reject flows get chatty enough to justify it (they're fine as plain POSTs). Metric: event delivery lag p99.
  • Partner grocery chains: webhooks (signed, event IDs for dedupe, documented retry schedule) + a GET events feed as the reconciliation path for partners who were down past retry expiry. Runner-up: partner pulls from the feed alone (simpler for you, worse freshness). Deciding failure mode: partner endpoint down (§11.4). Metric: delivery success rate + retry-queue depth per partner.
  • order ↔ pricing (5k req/s, needs answer now): gRPC unary; typed contract, cheap encoding, deadline propagation. Runner-up: REST+JSON if the team values curl-debuggability over the CPU/latency win. Metric: p99 with per-call deadlines; deadline-exceeded rate.
  • Order events → billing/notifications/analytics/data team: Kafka, fed via the transactional outbox (never dual-write, as you learned); consumers idempotent; data team replays from offset 0. Runner-up: RabbitMQ if replay weren't required — but the data team's "replayable" requirement decides it. Metric: consumer lag per group, DLQ depth.
