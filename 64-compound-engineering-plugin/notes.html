Lab 64 · Running It In Production

The compound-engineering plugin

Tell a friend: Maya works out on Monday that the left oven runs hot, and by Tuesday nobody in the kitchen knows it, so Dev burns a tray and works the same thing out again. Your sessions with an AI coding agent are those shifts. The code gets committed; the reasoning does not, so the same discovery is paid for twice. This plugin turns the work into a loop whose last step writes one small labelled file saying what was learned, and whose first step searches those labels before planning anything. That single backward arrow is the whole idea, and the other thirty-odd commands are scaffolding around it.

The simulator is visualization.html in this folder, also published as The Compounding Loop. Section 19 says what this lab is made of and where its numbers came from.

BEGINNER MODEL

1. Why does finishing a job lose the thing that made it hard?

In one breath: When work ends, the code is saved and the thinking is thrown away. So the next person pays full price for a lesson somebody has already learned.

You finish a piece of work with an AI coding agent. Along the way the two of you worked out something real. Staging names that variable differently. The obvious fix causes a deadlock. The third-party API returns 200 on failure.

Then the session ends.

All of it is gone. Not the code — the code is committed. What is gone is the reasoning: the dead ends, the reason you chose B over A, the trap you nearly fell into.

Next month a different task arrives. You, or a different agent, walks into the same trap. You pay the same cost to get out of it.

The consequence is that work does not get easier. Each change adds code to hold in your head. Meanwhile the understanding that would make the next change cheap evaporates every time a session closes.

That is the default. This plugin is one attempt to invert it.

2. What goes wrong in a kitchen with a new cook every shift?

In one breath: Every shift a different cook. Monday's discovery never reaches Tuesday. So the same twenty minutes get spent again, and again, and again.

A restaurant kitchen. Omar owns it. Asha runs the rota. Every shift, a different cook works the line.

Monday, Maya works the line. Two trays in, she works out that the left oven runs about 30 degrees hot. Everything on the top shelf comes out scorched. She adapts, drops the temperature, and the rest of her shift is fine. Shift ends. Maya goes home.

Tuesday, Dev works the line. He has never met Maya. He puts a tray in the left oven at the temperature the recipe says. It burns. He spends twenty minutes working out why. He arrives at exactly Maya's conclusion, and adapts.

Wednesday, Priya. Same tray. Same twenty minutes.

Nobody here is incompetent. The kitchen simply has no way for Monday to reach Tuesday. Every shift starts from zero, and the same discovery gets paid for over and over.

Your sessions with an agent are the shifts. The oven is every quirk of your codebase that is not written down anywhere.

Four more people will join the story as it goes. Meera likes to write one tidy summary at the end of the week. Arjun rearranges the kitchen. Ben is the customer who says what the dish should be. Asha and Omar keep the whole thing honest.

3. Why not just leave a note for the next cook?

In one breath: Write the oven fact on a card and pin it where tomorrow's cook will see it. This is the naive solution, it genuinely works, and you should keep doing it.

Asha's first answer is the obvious one. Leave a note. Put the oven fact somewhere Dev cannot miss it before his shift starts.

In agent terms, that note board is the file of standing instructions. Your tool reads it at the start of every session. It might be called CLAUDE.md, or AGENTS.md, or a CONVENTIONS.md. Write the trap into it, and every future session knows.

This is not a straw man. It is where most teams start, and for a handful of facts it is the right answer. Keep it. Nothing below replaces it.

4. What happens to the note board after a year?

In one breath: Two things break. The board gets too big for anyone to read before starting, and the cook who is exhausted at the end of a shift never writes the card in the first place.

Run Asha's fix for a year and watch it fail twice.

The board gets too big to read. By month six there are 300 notes on it. The oven. The sticky walk-in door. The supplier who delivers late on Fridays. Three notes about the oven that contradict each other. Dev reads all of it at the start of every shift, before touching a pan. Most of it has nothing to do with tonight's menu, and the one note that matters is buried in the middle.

For an agent this is not an inconvenience. It is a hard wall. Everything in that instruction file is read into the context window on every single task, whether or not it relates. A file that grows without bound eventually crowds out the actual work. Long before that it dilutes attention, because 300 facts competing means the important one is just one line among many.

Nobody writes the note. The second failure is worse, and much less obvious. Picture Maya at the end of her shift. She is exhausted. She has just solved the problem. That is exactly the moment when nobody wants to write documentation. The note gets written when someone remembers, which is rarely, and never for the small traps that cost twenty minutes each.

🔮 Predict: Both failures are real. Which one do you think is the harder one to engineer around?

Reveal

The second. If you said the first, you picked the one that looks bigger, and it is the one with an answer. It is a technical problem with a technical fix: do not read everything, search it. That is mechanical, and stages 6 to 10 are about how.

The second is a human problem. It depends on Maya choosing to do unrewarding work at the worst possible moment. You cannot engineer discipline. What you can do is make writing the card a named step of the process that the tooling performs, so it happens by default rather than by virtue. That is the entire reason /ce-compound is a step in a loop rather than advice in a wiki.

The simulator has a control labelled "Does the team write the learning down?". That control is this failure. Turn it off and the compounding team collapses to exactly the forgetful team: 8 repeats against 8. Every other way of breaking the mechanism still leaves some value behind. Skipping capture leaves none.

5. What do we actually need, then?

In one breath: Capture has to happen without anyone remembering. Looking things up has to stay cheap as the pile grows. And a card has to be findable by someone who only knows tonight's symptom.

Both failures point at the same three requirements.

  1. Capture must not depend on anyone remembering. It has to be a step in the process, not a virtue Maya has at 11pm.
  2. Retrieval must be selective. Consulting the past has to cost roughly the same whether there are 10 cards or 500. Otherwise the system punishes you for learning.
  3. A card must be findable by someone who only knows the new problem. Dev does not know what he does not know. He cannot search for "the oven runs hot". All he knows is "my tray burned".

Everything from here is the pursuit of those three. Number three is the one almost everybody underestimates, and stage 10 will show you why.

6. What is the one idea underneath all 33 skills?

In one breath: Make the work a loop. The last step writes down what was learned, in a shape a search can find. The first step searches before it plans.

Here is the whole thing, in one box.

Make the workflow a loop whose closing step writes what was learned into a small file with structured labels, and whose opening step searches those labels before planning anything.

Knowledge moves out of human memory and session context, into the repository, where finding it again is a search rather than an act of recall.

That is it. The plugin is 33 skills, a pipeline, review panels and a config system. All of it is scaffolding around that one arrow, running from the last step back to the first.

Everything that follows answers one of two questions. How do you make that arrow get drawn at all? And how do you make what it writes findable later?

7. What are the words, and where does each one come from?

In one breath: Nine words do all the work. Each one gets earned in the kitchen first, then given its real name, then written down.

The terminology arrives here in the order you need it, in three passes: who does the work, what a shift leaves behind, and how tomorrow finds it. Minor words are defined where they first appear and collected in the glossary at the end.

Who does the work, and who sends whom?

Omar does not buy a card index, then a rule about filling it in, then a quarterly weeding routine. He buys one box with all of that inside, and the box has a version number on the side. Engineers call that box a plugin: a bundle of extra abilities you install into a coding agent, as one unit.

Precisely, it is a distributable package of skills, agents, commands and hooks, described by one manifest called plugin.json, installed into a host such as Claude Code, Cursor or Codex. The word exists to name the unit of distribution and versioning, as distinct from any one capability inside it. You install and upgrade a plugin. You invoke a skill. The box this lab reads from is compound-engineering@compound-engineering-plugin, version 3.23.4, installed at user scope on 2026-08-27. Stage 19 says where that came from.

Asha never asks for "the box". She asks for one named thing: plan tonight's service. That named thing is a skill — one capability the plugin has, that you ask for by name. Under the hood a skill is a directory containing a SKILL.md file. Its instructions are loaded into the agent's context when you invoke it, and it pulls in reference files as it goes. You type /ce-plan, or $ce-plan if your host is Codex.

Now watch what Asha does not do. She does not walk to the card drawer herself. She sends a runner, who flips the tabs, brings back three cards, and reports. Engineers call that runner an agent, or a subagent.

That is the whole distinction, and it is worth holding on to. A skill is the thing you invoke, and it coordinates. An agent is a worker the skill dispatches to do one scoped job and report back. The plugin's own glossary is explicit about it: a skill orchestrates, an agent performs. So /ce-plan is a skill, and the learnings-researcher it sends off to search past work is a subagent.

Six of those skills run in a fixed order, and engineers here call that order the loop: brainstorm, plan, work, simplify, review, compound. Each step hands a durable file to the next. The last one writes something the first will read next time.

The word "loop" is doing one specific job. It names the return arrow. A pipeline that ends at "review" is just a checklist, and the arrow from the end back to the beginning is what makes the word "compound" mean anything. In practice: /ce-brainstorm writes a requirements document, /ce-plan reads that and writes an implementation plan, and /ce-work reads that and writes code.

What does a shift actually leave behind?

Maya's twenty minutes at the oven turn into one card. That card is a learning, also called a solution doc: one written-down lesson from one solved problem.

On disk it is a markdown file under docs/solutions/<category>/. It carries a block of structured labels, then prose saying what the problem was, what did not work, what did, and how to avoid it next time.

The word exists to name the unit of compounded knowledge. Not "a doc", not "a note", but a specific shape: one per solved problem, with a metadata contract that makes it retrievable. That one-problem-per-file rule is load-bearing, and stage 11 shows exactly what breaks without it. In the plugin's own repository there are 81 of these. One of them is docs/solutions/skill-design/strong-models-mask-defensive-skill-fixes.md.

Now the tabs down the side of the card. On the file they are the labels at the top, sitting between two lines of dashes, and engineers call that block the frontmatter.

It is a YAML block. It always carries title, category, module, problem_type, component, severity and tags. Then, depending on which of two tracks the document is on, it carries either symptoms, root_cause and resolution_type, or else applies_when.

Why give this block its own word? Because this block, and not the prose, is what search reads. It is the difference between a document and a findable document. Every one of the three requirements in stage 5 lands here.

Here is a real one, from the plugin's own corpus:

Read that block once more and notice one thing. The label module holds one value. So does component. So does problem_type. Only tags holds a list. That single fact does an enormous amount of work in stage 11, so park it somewhere you can find it again.

The card is not the only thing a shift leaves behind. The prep list, the tasting notes, the order to the supplier: each is a piece of paper one step leaves for the next step to pick up. Engineers call any of those an artifact.

Technically, an artifact is a durable, repo-relative document. A plan under docs/plans/, a learning under docs/solutions/, a requirements doc, a review report. Where they all live can be relocated in bulk by a docs_root setting.

The word matters because the steps do not pass information to each other in conversation. They pass files. That is what lets a step run in a fresh session, days later, on a different machine, and still know what came before. A learning is one kind of artifact, and it is the only kind that flows backwards.

One warning before you go on, because the word is overloaded. Claude Code also calls a published web page an Artifact, and that is what the simulator link at the top of this file points at. Unrelated meaning. Everywhere below, "artifact" means a file in your repository that one step leaves for another.

How does tonight's cook find Monday's card?

Dev's tray has burned. He does not read the drawer from front to back. He flips to the tabs that match tonight's menu, pulls three cards, and reads those.

That flip-then-read move is retrieval, and the plugin's version of it is called grep-first. In full, it is a dispatched subagent that does five things in order. First it reads the project's shared vocabulary file. Second it extracts keywords from the new task. Third it searches frontmatter fields only across the whole corpus, to build a shortlist. Fourth it reads the shortlist's labels. Only then, fifth, does it read the bodies of the best few.

The term is really naming the ordering. "Search then read" and "read then search" return the same documents at wildly different costs. Requirement 2 from stage 5 is satisfied by this ordering and by nothing else. On a corpus of 96 documents, read-everything costs 68,187 tokens before any work starts. Grep-first costs 4,723.

Those two numbers only mean something if you know what they are spent from. An agent can hold a fixed amount of text at once. That amount, and the question of what you spend it on, is the context budget.

Precisely, it is the model's context window. Everything loaded takes a share: standing instructions, skill bodies, retrieved documents, and the code itself. Anything spent on irrelevant material is not available for the task. The term earns its keep because it converts a vague question into an arithmetic one you can actually answer. It is why the note board in stage 3 hits a wall, and why retrieval is ordered the way it is.

There is one more thing in the room, and it beats the card every time. Above the oven, taped to the wall, is the recipe the kitchen actually follows tonight. That is the guidance layer: the instructions the agent is reading while it works.

Concretely, that means a skill's own SKILL.md, a runbook, or the project's root instruction file. Anything loaded at the moment of acting.

This word names a conflict that outranks ordinary staleness, so it is worth being slow about. A learning that merely got out of date is wrong. A learning that contradicts the guidance layer is worse. The agent reads the guidance at the moment it acts, so the learning simply loses, silently, every time.

Suppose Maya's card says "always run the migration in two steps", and the project's runbook says "use the one-step helper". The runbook wins in practice, and the card is decorative. The plugin's maintenance skill treats that as a higher-priority defect than a stale file path. It resolves it by checking which side the current code follows, and it reports a wrong guidance file rather than editing it.

🔮 Predict: Maya's card has tabs down the side and three paragraphs of prose on the front. Dev's runner searches 96 cards in a second. What did the runner actually look at?

Reveal

Only the tabs. If you said "it skims each card", you have imagined a reader. What is really there is a filter. The first pass never opens a card at all. It matches on the label block, because matching labels costs nothing and opening cards costs context budget.

This has a consequence that runs through the rest of the lab. Prose you write beautifully and label carelessly is invisible. The prose is only ever read after the labels have earned the card a place on the shortlist.

8. What two pictures should you carry?

In one breath: Picture a card index with tabs, not a wall of loose notes. Then swap it for a folder of labelled files and a runner who reads labels before bodies.

The simple picture: what does a card index do that a note board cannot?

Take Asha's note board and fix it. Instead of a wall of loose notes read start to finish, put in a card index. One card per lesson, each with tabs down the side saying which station, which dish, and what kind of problem it was.

Dev does not read the catalogue. He flips to the tabs matching tonight's menu, pulls three cards, and reads those.

The engineer's picture: what is it actually made of?

A directory of markdown files, each with a typed YAML header. A dispatched search agent that pre-filters on header fields without opening files, shortlists, and reads a handful of bodies. A writer step, invoked as the closing phase of a work cycle, that appends exactly one new file per solved problem and validates its header against a schema. And a separate maintenance pass that re-checks the corpus against the current code.

Where does the card catalogue lie to you?

Three places, and each one matters.

A card cannot go quietly wrong, but a learning can. The oven either runs hot or it does not. But a card can say "the retry logic lives in PaymentRetryService", and six months later Arjun renamed that class. The card still reads confidently. Nothing in the drawer knows. In this lab's model, a rename of two modules left 38% of a 96-document corpus citing paths that no longer existed, and 52% of everything retrieval handed back was silently rotten. That is why a maintenance pass exists at all. A physical catalogue needs no such thing.

Dev can tell a stale card from a fresh one, and an agent mostly cannot. A human sees yellowed paper and 1998 handwriting. An agent sees text, and text written in 2024 looks exactly like text written this morning. Confidence does not decay with age. This is why the plugin mechanically checks the paths and links a document cites, rather than trusting a reader to notice.

A card catalogue is a cache, and a learnings corpus is not quite. This one is worth being careful about, because it connects to something you already know. In your caching lab you established that a cache is a bounded copy of answers whose truth lives elsewhere. The test of being a cache is that deleting it loses only speed.

A learnings corpus behaves like a cache in two ways. It is a derived copy, and it goes stale. But it fails that test. Delete docs/solutions/ and you do not lose speed alone. You lose the reasoning, the dead ends, and the "we tried X and here is why it did not work". None of that is recomputable from the code, because the code only records what did happen. So borrow the staleness and invalidation intuitions from caching, but leave the "it is only a performance optimisation" one behind.

INTERMEDIATE MODEL

10. How does the mechanism actually work, step by step?

In one breath: Build the obvious version, break it, fix it, break it again. Every strange detail in the final design is the scar of a failure you are about to watch.

Asha is going to build the card index four times. Version one is what anybody would do. Versions two, three and four each exist because version one, two or three failed in a specific way.

v1 — what if you just write the files and read them all?

Two rules, and nothing else.

  1. At the end of a task, write a markdown file into docs/solutions/ describing what you learned.
  2. At the start of the next task, read docs/solutions/ before planning.

Concretely: three tasks in, the directory holds three files. Task four begins. The agent reads all three, notices one is about the trap it is walking toward, and avoids it.

This works. It is also the entire idea. Everything below is damage control.

🔮 Predict: It is now nine months later and docs/solutions/ holds 200 files averaging 2,600 characters. What is the first thing that breaks?

Reveal

Cost, and it breaks hard. If you said "the files start contradicting each other", that happens too, but much later and much less violently.

200 documents at roughly 2,800 characters is about 140,000 tokens loaded before the agent has looked at a single line of your code. On most models that is most or all of the budget, spent on material that is about 99% irrelevant to the task at hand.

The cruel part: this failure gets worse the more the team learns. A repo that has been diligent about capture is punished for it. That is the precise opposite of compounding, so v1 is not merely inefficient. It is self-defeating.

v2 — what if you search the labels instead of reading the cards?

The fix is to stop reading and start searching. Give every document a structured header. Then split retrieval into two phases with very different costs.

The diagram below is that split. Read it top to bottom, and notice that the box touching every file is the cheap one.

The asymmetry is the whole trick. Phase 1 touches every file and costs nothing, because grep reads the disk and not the context window. Phase 2 is expensive per file, and it touches almost none of them.

That flatness is the property the whole design exists to buy, and requirement 2 from stage 5 is now met.

🔮 Predict: Maya stores a card with module: payments and tags: [n-plus-one, jpa, lazy-loading]. Months later Priya arrives with "the orders page takes forever to load and the database looks busy". Does the search find Maya's card?

Reveal

No. Not one word overlaps. Priya says slow, page, load, database, busy. The card says n-plus-one, jpa, lazy-loading, payments, performance_issue. Phase 1 is a text match on labels, so nothing matches, the card is never shortlisted, and its body is never read.

If you said yes, you were imagining a reader who understands both phrasings. Phase 1 has no such reader in it.

The card exists. It is correct. It is about exactly this problem. And it is invisible, which is operationally identical to never having written it, except that it cost time to write and now needs maintaining.

In this lab, across 12 topics, house vocabulary found 12 of 12 and symptom vocabulary found 0 of 12.

v3 — what if tomorrow's cook uses different words?

The failure above is a vocabulary failure, not a search failure.

Priya describes the symptom, because she does not yet know the cause. Maya described the cause, because by the time she wrote the card she did. Those two never share words. Dev's burned tray and Maya's hot oven are the same fact in two vocabularies.

So the fix is a phrasebook. Before extracting keywords, the search agent reads CONCEPTS.md, the project's shared glossary of domain terms, entities and named processes. It maps "the orders page is slow" onto the vocabulary this repository actually uses, and searches with that instead.

Experiment 2 runs the same 96-document corpus against the same 12 traps, and varies only how the new task was phrased.

How the new task was described Traps found
In the team's own vocabulary 12 / 12
In symptom words 0 / 12
In symptom words, with CONCEPTS.md 9 / 12

Note honestly what this shows. The glossary recovers most of the loss, not all of it. Three topics stay unreachable, because nothing connects their symptom words to their house words. Vocabulary grounding is a large mitigation, not a cure. A corpus whose tags drift away from how people actually describe problems will quietly stop being consulted.

Worth noticing: CONCEPTS.md is itself maintained by the capture step, as a side effect. Every time a card is written, qualifying domain terms are folded into the glossary. The mechanism that makes retrieval work is fed by the same loop it serves.

🔮 Predict: Everything now works. Meera has a long session that solves five separate problems. Writing five files feels bureaucratic to her. Why not one tidy "lessons from this week" document?

Reveal

Because a file has one set of labels. Look again at the schema in stage 7. module is one string, component is one string, and problem_type is one enum value. A document about five problems must pick one set, so it is labelled correctly for one of them and wrongly for the other four.

If you said "it is fine, the prose is all there", you are thinking of a human browsing. Phase 1 only ever sees labels.

So four of Meera's five lessons are written down, stored, committed, backed up and structurally unfindable. That is worse than not writing them, because it looks done.

Experiment 3 puts numbers on it. Of five learnings folded into one weekly file, search found the file for 1 of the 5 queries. On the one query where it did match, context read rose from 4,758 to 7,398 tokens, which is +56%, while the share of that context actually relevant to the task fell from 15% to 10%.

This is why /ce-compound states "One learning per run" as a rule, and sends a session that produced five learnings through five sequential runs.

v4 — why one card per run, and what happens when a card rots?

Two final refinements, both scars.

One learning per run is now forced, for Meera's reason above.

And the drawer needs weeding. A card is written once and read for years, while the code underneath it keeps moving. Nothing in the retrieval path can detect this. Phase 1 matches labels, and the labels do not change when the code does. Arjun renames a class and every card citing it goes quietly wrong.

So the plugin adds mechanical checking at two moments.

At write time, a bundled script validates that the frontmatter parses safely. A second script checks every file path, commit SHA and link the document cites against the actual tree. A dead path is treated as a question, not a verdict, because a card may legitimately cite a file that the very fix it documents deleted.

Then later, on a schedule, /ce-compound-refresh re-runs that across the whole corpus. It classifies each document into one of five outcomes: Keep, Update, Consolidate, Replace, or Delete.

Experiment 4 says how bad the rot gets. After a refactor renaming two modules and deleting a third, 36 of 96 documents cited a dead path, and 52% of everything retrieval handed back was silently rotten. The mechanical check does not fix those. It narrows 96 documents down to 36 that Omar or an agent must adjudicate.

Why five backward arrows and not one?

The single most useful thing to understand about this pipeline is that it has five backward arrows, not one. Each exists because a different kind of mistake is cheapest to catch in a different place.

That is precisely why E is the one that gets skipped. It is the only loop whose benefit lands on someone who is not in the room. It is also why the plugin makes it a step with a command rather than a line in a wiki.

What does Ben's sign-off actually give you, and what does it not?

Be precise here, because it is easy to over-claim.

It gives you the artifact. /ce-brainstorm produces a requirements-only document, literally stamped artifact_readiness: requirements-only. That is the "here is what we think you asked for" spec you send back to Ben. /ce-doc-review reads that stamp, and reviews such a document against a Product Contract only, so it does not report missing implementation detail as a defect.

It gives you review and a way to share. /ce-doc-review critiques the spec with role-specific reviewer personas. /ce-proof publishes markdown for someone to comment on. Setting brainstorm_output: html renders it for a human reader instead of an agent.

It does not give you an enforced gate. There is no state in the tool meaning "blocked awaiting approval". Nothing prevents you running /ce-plan on an unapproved spec, and /lfg has no human checkpoint at all until a PR exists.

So gate 2 is real and it matters. But it is Asha's discipline plus the tool's artifacts, not a state machine the tool enforces. Worth knowing before you rely on it.

One more nuance helps here. The plugin has a notion of a session-settled decision: a tradeoff that was actually examined and chosen in conversation, then carried downstream as a labelled constraint. Later skills may augment it, but they must not silently re-ask it or contradict it. A decision merely asserted is a directive, and gets one challenge. A decision that survived examination is settled. That is the mechanism by which Ben's answer at gate 2 travels into step 3 without being relitigated.

ENGINEERING MODEL

11. What are the nine ways this breaks?

In one breath: Two failures stop the card being written usefully. Five stop it being found or believed. Two happen at the edges, where the loop meets the outside world.

Nine failure scenarios, numbered, and the numbers stay fixed so the exercises can point at them. Each one is a short story about somebody in the kitchen. The comparison table at the end lines all nine up side by side.

Which failures stop the card being written usefully?

Failure 1, capture never happens. Maya goes home without writing anything, every night, forever. In the simulator you cause this by setting "Does the team write the learning down?" to Never. The two teams then become numerically identical, at 8 repeats each. The drawer stays empty, and every other mechanism in the design is intact and irrelevant. The reason is blunt: the loop has no closing step, so it is not a loop.

There is no response available in the tool. This is the one failure the design cannot defend against, only make less likely, by making capture a named step with a command rather than a good intention. Nothing here is ever wrong; you simply lose everything the loop is for. On a real repo the honest signal is corpus growth rate. A docs/solutions/ that has not grown in two months means the loop is not running, whatever anyone says in standup.

Failure 2, capture happens but batched. This is Meera's weekly summary. In the simulator you set "One learning per document?" to No. Watch the store: it shows a single card, and clicking it makes the inspector say how many problems are inside and how many of them are unreachable. The repeat count lands at 6 instead of 0. In the Java model, 1 of 5 learnings stays retrievable, with +56% context on the one that works.

The cause is the single-valued module, component and problem_type fields from stage 7. Labels can only describe one problem. The response is a contract: /ce-compound refuses to batch. The prose Meera wrote is not lost, but retrievability is, for all but one of her five. In production this is what a "weekly learnings" doc becomes. It reads like diligence and functions as a write-only log.

Which failures stop the card being found, or believed?

Failure 3, retrieval misses because the vocabulary drifted. Priya arrives describing symptoms. To cause it in the simulator, set "How is the next task described?" to symptom words and the shared vocabulary file to No. In Java it is experiment 2. You get 8 repeats with 7 documents sitting in the store, and 0/12 recall in the Java model. Phase 1 is a text match on labels, and symptom words and cause words do not intersect.

The response is CONCEPTS.md, read before keyword extraction, which recovers 9/12 and not 12/12. Correctness is kept, in the sense that nothing wrong is ever returned. What is lost is the entire benefit, silently. This is the most dangerous failure in the set, because a miss looks exactly like "there was nothing to find". The counter-measure in production is to write tags in the words people use when the problem appears, not only the words they use once it is understood.

Failure 4, the card went stale. Arjun renames the payments module. In the simulator you press "Rename the payments module" and keep stepping; in Java it is experiment 4. Affected cards get an amber top edge and a rot marker, and they are still retrieved. The outcome pill reads followed stale advice. The Java model reports 36/96 documents citing dead paths and 52% of retrieved documents rotten.

The cause is that labels do not change when code does, and retrieval never opens the code. The response is the mechanical claims check at write time, plus /ce-compound-refresh on a schedule; press "Run /ce-compound-refresh" in the simulator to watch the amber clear. Nothing is lost here, which is the trap. What you lose is trustworthiness, and that is worse than a miss, because the answer arrives confident. In production, schedule the refresh against the same events that invalidate knowledge: big renames, framework upgrades, module deletions.

Failure 5, the card contradicts the recipe on the wall. Maya's card says "always X". The skill instructions or runbook the agent loads while acting say "never X". The guidance wins, every time, silently, and the card is decorative. The reason is ordering: guidance is loaded at the moment of acting, while a retrieved learning is only context, and instructions outrank references. The maintenance skill ranks a guidance contradiction above ordinary staleness. It resolves it by checking which side the current code follows, and it reports a wrong guidance file rather than editing it. The system keeps doing what the guidance says; what is lost is the card's authority.

Failure 6, two cards about the same thing, drifting apart. Maya and Dev both capture the oven, from two different sessions. Now there are two answers to one question, gradually disagreeing, and retrieval returns whichever ranks higher. Nothing prevents a near-duplicate at write time except a check, so a check is what the plugin adds. Before writing, it runs an overlap assessment. High overlap updates the existing document rather than creating a second. Moderate overlap creates the new one and flags it for consolidation review. Nothing is lost, except a single source of truth, and only until consolidation runs.

Failure 7, context blowout from reading everything. This is v1 again, and you can cause it deliberately. Set "How does the next task consult the past?" to Read every document, or run experiment 1 in Java. The cost meter turns red. In the simulator's small corpus that is 4,918 tokens against 2,108. In the Java corpus it is 68,187 against 4,723. The cause is cost proportional to corpus size, and the response is the phase split. Recall is fine here, because it finds the same documents. What you lose is affordability, and therefore the willingness to keep learning at all.

Which failures happen at the edges of the loop?

Failure 8, an autonomous run loses its residuals. Run /lfg on work where review raises findings the run decides not to fix. The finding then exists only in a session that is about to end, and nothing in a transcript survives it. The pipeline's response is to define a residual: an accepted or deferred finding. It then refuses to report itself done until each residual reaches a durable sink, meaning a ticket or a comment on the PR. Never the session. The change ships, and nothing is lost, provided the sink rule holds.

Failure 9, the host truncates the skill body. Install on a host whose prompt budget is smaller than the skill's instructions. The tail of the instructions is silently dropped. Every known truncation keeps the beginning and discards the rest, and none of them reports an error. The ceiling is enforced by the host's loader, not by the plugin.

The response is that ordering becomes load-bearing. What must survive is written above what may be cut, and heavy detail moves into reference files pulled in at the step that needs them. Whatever fit is kept; what may be lost is a safety rule, with every mechanical check still green.

This is a genuinely nasty class of bug. It is worth knowing about even if you never author a skill, because the general shape is this: a size limit enforced by silent truncation turns document order into a correctness property.

12. What is actually guaranteed, and what is only hoped for?

In one breath: Some of this is a script that either ran or did not. Some of it is a design that makes the good outcome likely. And some of it is not promised at all, which is fine as long as you know which is which.

Be clear-eyed here. This is a process and prompt system, not a protocol. It has nothing like the guarantees you established in your paxos or two-phase-commit labs. Sorting its claims into three buckets is the useful move, and Asha should be able to say which bucket any claim lives in before she relies on it.

What is mechanically enforced?

A script or a hard rule, and it either happened or it did not.

  • Frontmatter parses, and parses safely. A bundled validator catches an unquoted #, which silently truncates the value as a comment, and an unquoted : , which silently reads as a nested mapping. Both corrupt a card without erroring.
  • Every file path, SHA and link a document cites is checked against the tree.
  • The artifact root fails closed. An unusable docs_root stops the run rather than silently falling back to docs/, so a typo cannot scatter artifacts across the repo.
  • Write boundary: subagents write only to scratch. Only the orchestrator writes files in your tree.
  • One learning per run.

What is only structurally encouraged?

The design makes the good outcome likely, not certain.

  • Retrieval cost stays near-flat as the corpus grows, because of the phase split.
  • Learnings stay findable, because of the schema plus the glossary. That is 9/12, not 12/12.
  • Capture happens, because it is a named step. But only if somebody invokes it.

What is not guaranteed at all?

Pretending otherwise is how people get burned.

  • That the right learning is retrieved. Relevance is a model's judgment.
  • That a retrieved learning is still true. Only that its citations were checked the last time somebody checked.
  • That two runs behave identically. There is no determinism here.
  • That the corpus stays consistent with itself.

What did you trade away to get this?

Determinism, and immediate speed. Every step costs tokens and wall-clock now, against a payoff that is diffuse, delayed and hard to attribute. Nobody will ever be able to point at the incident that did not happen in September because of a card written in March.

That asymmetry is the real reason this kind of practice gets dropped. The cost is certain and now; the benefit is invisible and later. Name it before you decide to adopt it, and name it again when somebody proposes dropping loop E.

What does it cost, and when should you not bother?

The costs are real tokens and real minutes per task, spent up front. There is more process between having an idea and having code. There is a drawer that needs weeding. And there is genuine risk from stale cards, which are worse than absent ones, because they arrive confident.

The benefits are that traps stop repeating. Onboarding shortens, because the reasoning is in the repository rather than in whoever happened to be there. Agents start each task with grounding nobody had to remember to supply.

And then there are the cases where the honest answer is no. A throwaway prototype. A repo you will not touch again in three months. A solo project small enough to hold entirely in your head. The payoff is proportional to repeat encounters with the same codebase. If there will not be many, the cost is all you get.

What does it build on that you already know?

  • From caching: a learning is a derived copy whose truth lives elsewhere, and it goes stale exactly the way a cache entry does. Invalidation is the hard half here too. The difference, which stage 8 spells out, is that deleting a cache loses only speed, while deleting a learnings corpus loses reasoning that cannot be recomputed from the code.
  • From software-architecture-patterns: an ADR records why a decision was made, so the next person does not relitigate it. A learning is the same instinct, aimed at traps rather than decisions, and automated rather than remembered.
  • From cqrs: the corpus is a lossy projection of past work, read on a different path from the one that wrote it, always slightly behind the truth. Same consistency window, same need for a maintenance pass.

What is it commonly confused with?

  • A wiki. A wiki is written for humans to browse. A learnings corpus is written for a machine to search, which is why the labels matter more than the prose.
  • RAG. Retrieval-augmented generation retrieves from a corpus somebody else produced. Here the same loop produces and consumes the corpus, which is the compounding part.
  • A big CLAUDE.md. That is Asha's naive fix from stage 3. It is read every time regardless of relevance; this is searched.
  • Automated documentation. Docs describe what the code does. A learning describes what went wrong and why, which is information that is nowhere in the code.

Real things using this shape: ADR practice, postmortem corpora, runbook libraries, and most agent "memory" systems, which are this same capture-plus-retrieval pattern with different storage.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Something like:

When you solve a problem with an AI agent, the code gets saved but the reasoning doesn't — the dead ends, the trap you nearly fell into, why you chose this approach. Next month someone hits the same trap and pays the same cost. This tool makes "write down what we just learned" a step in the process rather than a good intention, and stores it in a shape that the next task can search — so the discovery gets made once instead of every time.

The test of a good answer: no jargon, and it names both halves. Writing it down is only half; the other half is that a future task, which doesn't know what it doesn't know, can find it.

1.2 The arrow from the last step back to the first — /ce-compound writes to docs/solutions/, and the next run's /ce-plan searches it. Without that arrow the six steps are an ordinary development checklist: brainstorm, plan, build, tidy, review. Useful, but the hundredth task would cost exactly what the first one did. The arrow is the only thing that makes the word "compound" mean anything.

1.3 Two reasons:

  1. Cost. A big instructions file is loaded on every task regardless of relevance. It grows without bound, and eventually crowds out the work — a repo that has learned more is punished for it. The corpus is searched, so consulting 96 documents costs about what consulting 12 does (4,723 vs 3,979 tokens, measured).
  2. Granularity and maintenance. One giant file has no structure to search, no per-item labels, and nothing to validate. A corpus of labelled documents can be pre-filtered, checked against the code, and weeded individually.

The instructions file isn't replaced — it's the right home for a handful of always-relevant facts. It's the wrong home for three hundred.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 Seven documents from 14 tasks. Because a document is only written the first time a given trap is met in a given module — a repeat has nothing new to capture. Six traps, one of which (n+1) appears in two different modules, gives seven.

2.2 Read: everything between the two --- lines — title, category, module, problem_type, tags. Not read (unless the labels earn a shortlist place): the prose under the heading. That asymmetry is the mechanism.

Rung 5 — Implement

5.1 — recency weighting. Expect it to hurt recall while improving trust. Recency is a proxy for correctness, not a measure of relevance, so weighting it into the same score conflates two different questions. The better design is to keep it out of ranking and use it as a presentation signal — surface the document, flag it as old — or as a trigger for the maintenance pass. Anything that lets a fresh-but-irrelevant document outrank a stale-but-exactly-right one has made the system worse. Report your measured numbers either way; being surprised here is the point.

5.2 — two-pass narrowing. The key property to demonstrate: intersecting with a second pattern is not the same as truncating. Truncation drops whatever sorts last, which is arbitrary with respect to relevance; narrowing drops documents that genuinely fail a second criterion. This is exactly the bug I hit while building this lab — the shortlist cap was originally applied in file order, before ranking, and the newest documents fell off the end.

Rung 7 — Handle failure (worked discussions)

7.1 — Detecting the silent miss. The core difficulty: a miss and a genuinely empty corpus produce identical output. So you cannot detect it from a single retrieval — you need a base rate.

A workable design: log every retrieval as a structured record (query terms, candidate count, bodies read, whether the resulting plan cited any of them). Then two derived signals:

  • Shortlist starvation — the fraction of retrievals returning zero candidates, tracked over time. A corpus that is growing while this rises is drifting out of reach. This is the cheap signal and it needs no labelling.
  • Citation rate — how often a plan actually references a retrieved document. Near zero against a large corpus is the alarm.

The clever cheap trick: periodically re-run retrieval using the titles of existing documents as synthetic queries. Every one should retrieve itself. Any document that cannot retrieve itself has labels disconnected from its own content, and needs no human labelling to detect.

7.2 — Trust decay. A defensible scheme: confidence starts high and is reduced by evidence, never by time alone — cited paths gone, contradicted by a newer learning, contradicts the guidance layer. Restored by a maintenance pass that re-verifies the claims.

What a low-confidence learning does differently: it is still retrieved, but presented as "this may be out of date; verify before relying on it" and it never suppresses a competing higher-confidence document.

The failure mode you introduce: confidence becomes a thing to game and a thing to trust. A document marked high-confidence gets more deference than an unmarked one does today — so a wrong high-confidence document is more dangerous than a wrong unmarked one. You have converted an unknown-quality signal into a falsely-precise one. Decaying by time alone is worse still: it makes documents look untrustworthy exactly when they have been stable and correct for years.

7.3 — Capture that survives a crash. The obvious answer — capture continuously as you go — reintroduces experiment 3's problem, because you cannot label a learning until you know what it was, and mid-session you don't.

A better shape: a running scratch note during the session that is not a learning and is not retrievable, plus the real capture step at the end that turns it into one properly-labelled document. If the session dies, the scratch note survives as raw material for a later capture run.

The trade you are making, stated honestly: a scratch note that is never promoted is clutter, so you need a rule for expiring them. You have traded "learnings are lost on a crash" for "half-formed notes accumulate", which is a better problem — it is visible, and it fails toward noise rather than toward silence.

Rung 8 — Design (worked arguments)

8.1 — Forty engineers, eight repositories.

One corpus or one per repo? Per repository, with a small shared corpus for cross-cutting conventions. The reasons: retrieval quality depends on selectivity, and a single 8-service corpus makes every query less selective, which is exactly the property experiment 1 depends on. It also breaks ownership — a document about service A's database is noise for service B's frontend work. What breaks: genuinely cross-cutting learnings get duplicated or lost, which is why the shared corpus exists for exactly that category and nothing else.

The cross-repo learning. Honestly: this is the weakest part of the model. The pragmatic answer is that the shared corpus holds conventions and patterns — the generalised form — while incident-level learnings stay local. A learning that would prevent a bug in another service is usually a sign it should be promoted to the shared corpus as a pattern, and that promotion is a judgment call somebody has to make.

Instrument first: corpus growth rate, because it detects the failure that kills everything (capture stops) and costs nothing to measure. Threshold: no new documents in a repository for 30 days triggers a conversation, not an alarm — because a quiet repository may simply not have been worked on. Second: shortlist starvation from 7.1.

The "extra process" objection. The honest answer concedes the cost. It is real: tokens, minutes, and a corpus to maintain, paid now, against a benefit that is delayed and impossible to attribute — nobody can point at the incident that didn't happen. The case for it is that the cost is proportional to tasks while the benefit is proportional to repeat encounters with the same codebase, so it pays off on long-lived systems and does not on short-lived ones. If the objector's work is genuinely short-lived, they are right and should not adopt it.

8.2 — The staleness budget. Trigger refresh on events that invalidate knowledge, not on a calendar: large renames, module deletions, framework major upgrades, and a threshold on the stale fraction from a cheap mechanical scan (path existence is nearly free — it is only the adjudication that costs).

Scope: the mechanical check runs over everything, always, because it is cheap. The expensive semantic pass runs only over documents the mechanical check flagged, and over the highest-leverage documents regardless.

Which are worth checking most often: pattern documents — the ones generalised from several learnings — because future work treats them as broadly applicable, so a stale one does proportionally more damage than a stale incident report. After those, the documents that are actually retrieved most often. A document nobody retrieves is not worth spending a model call to verify.

8.3 — The case against. A strong version:

The benefit is real but unattributable, and unattributable benefits lose budget fights. The cost is immediate, visible, and paid by the person doing the work. Meanwhile the corpus is a liability that grows: 38% of it can go stale from a single refactor, and a stale learning is worse than none because it arrives confident. On a codebase being actively rewritten, most of what you capture will be obsolete before it is ever retrieved. And the whole thing depends on discipline that decays the moment someone is in a hurry — at which point you have the cost of a half-maintained corpus and none of the benefit.

What would change my mind: evidence of repeat encounters. If I can point at three incidents in the last quarter that were the same trap twice, the argument is over. That is also the cheapest thing to check before adopting — look at your own incident history first, and if the traps don't repeat, don't adopt this.
