Lab 51 · Work That Spans Services

CQRS — one guarded ledger, many chalkboards

Tell a friend: Asha's shop keeps one ledger at the counter, and every sale goes into it under strict rules. Meera the manager keeps flipping back through that same ledger to add up the day's totals, and the queue at the counter grows while she does it. So Asha hires Dev, who reads the new entries every few minutes and copies the answers onto chalkboards that Meera reads instead. That is CQRS: one guarded ledger for changes, many cheap boards for questions, and a price paid in the boards always being a little behind. The one rule that keeps it safe is that nobody ever decides anything from a board.

The trainer is visualization.html — five panels, and panel 1 is the one to open first. What this lab is made of, where every number came from, and where it sits among your other labs are in section 17 at the end.

1. Why did checkout die when nobody touched checkout?

In one breath: Asha's order service puts one queue in front of eight workers. One of Meera's reports holds a worker for most of a second. Priya's checkout write needs five milliseconds, and ends up waiting thirteen.

Start with the shape of the outage, because it is the reason the pattern exists. It is also not the reason usually given.

Asha runs a retail order service. One Postgres, one connection pool of eight workers. Checkout writes are small and fast. Each takes about 5 ms, and there are 300 of them a second.

Do that arithmetic. It is 1.5 seconds of work arriving every second, spread over eight workers. That is 19% utilisation. The database is nearly idle, and every dashboard says so.

Then Ben on the analytics team ships a merchandising page for Meera. It runs a five-way join over orders, order_items, products and customers. It aggregates by day. It takes about 800 ms, and it is called a handful of times a second.

Nothing about checkout changed. Not its code, not its traffic, not its hardware. Read down the table below. The only thing moving is how many of Ben's reports per second share the pool with Priya's order.

Reports/s Pool utilisation Checkout mean Checkout p99 What Priya feels
0 0.19 5.01 ms 23 ms instant
2 0.39 5.13 ms 24 ms instant
4 0.59 19.44 ms 406 ms a hesitation
6 0.79 161.24 ms 1,590 ms "is this working?"
8 0.99 5,412 ms 13,192 ms she gives up and leaves

Checkout's p99 goes from 23 ms to 1.59 seconds. That is 69×. At eight reports a second checkout is simply dead: thirteen seconds to place an order.

The mechanism is not mysterious, and it is not about CPU. There is one queue in front of eight workers. An 800 ms report occupies a worker for 800 ms. A 5 ms checkout write that arrives while several workers hold reports waits behind them.

Engineers call that head-of-line blocking, and you can read it straight off the number. At 4 reports/s the checkout p99 is 406 ms. That is about half of one report's service time, which is exactly the average wait behind a job you are stuck behind.

Now the part that makes this a pattern and not just a capacity story. Two completely different things are wrong here, and they need different fixes.

One: two workloads with very different service times share one queue. Your load-balancing lab proved that pooling is a large win when the work is identical. This is the same result read backwards. When the work is not identical, sharing a queue means the short jobs inherit the long jobs' latency.

Two: the report recomputes, on every single view, an answer that changes only when someone places an order. The join is not slow because the database is bad. It is slow because the schema is shaped for recording a sale, and Meera's question is how much did we sell today. Those are not the same shape.

CQRS is the design that takes both of those seriously at once. Most explanations lead with the second. The first is what actually gets it approved.

2. What are the two jobs one ledger is being asked to do?

In one breath: The people who write into the ledger and the people who read out of it want opposite things. One wants rules and care. The other wants speed and totals. Only one of them minds being half a minute out of date.

The abstraction is easier to hold if you have a physical picture first, so put the software down and walk into Asha's shop.

A busy shop keeps one ledger at the counter. Cashiers write every sale into it: date, customer, each item, each price. They are careful. Prices are checked, quantities cannot be negative, one entry per sale. The ledger is the truth, and it is guarded.

Meera the manager reads that same ledger constantly. Revenue so far today? Which product is moving? How much has Priya spent this year? None of those answers are written anywhere in the ledger. Each one is found by flipping back through pages and adding numbers up.

So the ledger is doing two jobs with opposite requirements. The last row is the one to remember.

The cashier's job Meera's job
Needs rules, one-at-a-time care, exact speed, totals, convenient shape
Touches one new entry hundreds of old entries
Frequency once per sale many times per hour
If it is 30 seconds out of date unacceptable — you would sell stock twice fine, nobody notices

Why does that last row matter more than the others? Because the two jobs do not merely have different performance profiles. They have different tolerances for being wrong about time.

That asymmetry is the entire opening for the pattern. A design that does not exploit it has nothing to trade.

And now the shop's actual fix, which is what CQRS is.

Cashiers keep writing into the ledger, unchanged. But every few minutes Dev the clerk walks over, reads the entries added since he last looked, and updates chalkboards on the wall. TODAY'S REVENUE: ₹12,400. TOP SELLER: keyboards. PRIYA — 4 ORDERS THIS YEAR.

Meera stops opening the ledger entirely. One glance at a board answers each question. The boards are a few minutes behind. Everyone accepts that, and the queue at the counter disappears.

Three properties of that arrangement are worth naming now, because everything technical later is one of these three.

The boards are derived. Erase one and Dev can rewrite it from the ledger. They hold no truth of their own.

The boards are behind. Always. Not sometimes, not under load — always, by at least the time it takes Dev to walk over.

Nobody makes a decision from a board. The cashier checks stock in the ledger, not on a chalkboard. Notice this one. It is doing more work than it appears to be, and §11.2 is what happens when it is violated.

3. What is the obvious solution, and why should you try it first?

In one breath: Nobody sane invents CQRS while looking at §1's table. They add an index, then a cache, then a read replica. All three are good moves, and one of them fixes the table completely.

Faced with §1's table, no competent engineer invents CQRS. They do these, in roughly this order, and they are all reasonable.

Add indexes. Correct first move, often sufficient, and cheap. Its limit is structural. An index makes finding rows fast, but the merchandising query's cost is in joining and aggregating them, and no index removes that. Every index added for Meera's side is then maintained on Priya's side, on every write, forever.

Add a cache. Also correct, also often sufficient. Its limit is that a cache is a guess about what will be asked again, with invalidation as the price. Your caching lab covered where that guess goes wrong. A cache also does nothing for the first request after every write.

Add a read replica. This is the strongest of the three and it deserves a straight answer: it fixes §1's table completely. Move Ben's reports to a replica and checkout gets its eight workers back and its 23 ms p99. If §1 were the whole problem you would stop here, and many teams correctly do.

Write cleverer SQL, or a materialised view. Now you are close to the real thing, and §10.1 comes back to it.

4. What survives the read replica, and why is that a failure?

In one breath: The replica moved Ben's reports off Priya's queue and changed nothing else. The five-way join is still a five-way join. And you paid for staleness without buying anything with it.

Two things survive the read replica. Each one is a reason for a different half of CQRS, and there is a third that no benchmark will ever show you.

4a. Did you pay for staleness and forget to spend it?

A read replica is asynchronously replicated. The moment you route Meera's reports to it, her answers come from a slightly old copy of Asha's ledger.

Look at what that means. You have accepted exactly the property people cite as the reason not to adopt CQRS. That property is "eventual consistency is too hard". And in exchange you received the same schema, somewhere else.

That is the trade worth staring at. Staleness is the currency here. A replica spends it and buys isolation. CQRS spends the same currency and buys isolation and shape.

If you were willing to pay once, the only question left is whether you want the second thing too.

4b. Which end of the shop is paying for the arithmetic?

Here is the arithmetic, and it is the whole economic case for the pattern. Ask it in the shop first, in words, before any letters appear.

How many times a day does something in the shop change? That is the number of sales. How many times a day does someone ask a question? That is the number of times Meera looks. And how much work is one answer, computed the hard way by flipping through the ledger?

Now the letters, each carrying its words for a while. Let N be the changes per day. Let R be the reads per day. Let J be the cost of computing one answer from the normalised schema, the hard way.

There are exactly two places to do that work.

Compute at read time and the total cost is R · J, which is reads per day times the cost of one answer. You pay once per question asked.

Compute at write time and the total cost is about N · J′, which is changes per day times the cost of incrementally updating a stored answer. You pay once per change made.

So the deciding quantity is R/N, which is reads per change, and nothing else. Not data volume. Not table size. Not "we're at scale now".

CQRS is not a scaling technique that helps more the bigger you get. It is a bet on a ratio, and the bet loses when the ratio is below 1.

And now the distinction the replica option obscures.

A read replica multiplies the number of machines paying R·J. A projection reduces R·J itself.

Replicas add capacity to absorb the work. Projections delete the work. If Meera's dashboard is called a million times a day, three replicas means three machines each doing five-way joins a million times a day.

That is why "just add replicas" runs out. Not at some magic scale, but at the point where reads times cost-per-answer exceeds what you are willing to buy hardware for, over and over.

4c. What breaks that no benchmark can see?

There is a slower failure that no table in this lab will show you.

Once the write model is also the read model, display concerns leak into it. A total_amount column denormalised so one screen is fast. A last_order_at maintained inline. An order that grows six fields belonging to one of Meera's reports.

Each of those is individually justified. Collectively they mean the one place Asha's business rules must stay crisp is now also a view model. Every rule change then has to reason about screens.

This one does not appear in any table in this lab, and in my experience it is the reason teams that adopt CQRS say they would do it again. Flagging it as judgement rather than measurement.

5. What is the real requirement, said in plain words first?

In one breath: The code that changes things and the code that shows things want different shapes on different machines, and neither should be able to hurt the other. The showing side pays for that by being allowed to lag.

Everything above points at one requirement, and it is worth writing without any vocabulary at all.

The code that changes things and the code that shows things should be able to use different data shapes, on different hardware, without either one being able to slow down or corrupt the other — and the showing side must be allowed to be slightly out of date, because that is the only thing we have to pay with.

Three commitments hide in that sentence. Each one becomes a mechanism later.

Different shapes. So there must be more than one representation of the same facts, and something must keep the second one current. That something is Dev.

Neither can hurt the other. So the coupling between them must be asynchronous. And the moment it is asynchronous, they are not equal in time.

Out of date is allowed. Allowed where? That is the question this lab spends the most effort on. The honest answer is not "on the read side". It is narrower than that, and §6 states it.

6. What is the key idea, and what one rule licenses it?

In one breath: Use one model for changes and a different model for questions. That is safe only because no change ever consults a question model. Break that one rule and staleness stops being a delay and becomes a bug.

The idea in two sentences:

Handle changes and questions with two different models. One model receives commands, enforces every rule, and records what happened; from that record, any number of query models are built, each shaped like one question, each rebuildable, each allowed to lag.

That is CQRS. It fits on a napkin.

But stated that way it invites the mistake that ruins it. So here is the rule that makes it safe, and it is the single most important sentence in this lab.

The read model makes no decisions.

It is allowed to be stale, denormalised, duplicated, wrong-shaped and disposable precisely and only because nothing depends on it being right at any particular instant. The moment a command consults a read model in order to decide whether to accept, the consistency window stops being a UX cost and becomes a correctness bug.

In the shop: Meera reads chalkboards all day and no harm comes of it. The cashier at the counter never looks at a board before saying yes to a sale. She looks at the ledger.

Everything else in this lab is either machinery that supports that rule, or a measurement of what happens when it is broken. §11.2 breaks it on purpose and counts the damage.

Notice what the rule does not say. It does not say the read side is unimportant. It does not say it can be sloppy. It does not say staleness does not matter.

It says the read side's staleness is survivable because it is never an input to a decision. That is a narrow, checkable property. You check it by reading your command handlers and asking one question: does anything in here read from a projection?

7. Which terminology does this actually need, and which two words get confused?

In one breath: Five words, and you have met all five in the shop already. Two of them look like each other and are not, and the confusion costs teams a year.

You have met most of these. The entries here are about the distinctions, not the definitions. The full list is in §16.

What is the difference between asking for a change and asking a question?

A command asks for a change. PlaceOrder(ord-1, priya, [...]). It is named imperatively, it may be rejected, and it returns success or failure rather than data.

A query asks a question. It changes nothing, and it is never rejected for a business reason.

Why bother naming them? Because they have different failure vocabularies. A command can fail because you are not allowed to do that. A query can only fail because something broke.

Once you notice that, it is obvious they should not share a code path. It is less obvious, and equally true, that they need not share a data model.

CQS or CQRS — which one did your team actually adopt?

This is the confusion that costs people a year, so take the two apart slowly.

CQS is Command–Query Separation, from Bertrand Meyer in 1988. It is a rule about methods. A method either changes state or returns a value, never both. It applies inside one object, one model, one database. list.pop() violates it.

CQRS is Command Query Responsibility Segregation, from Greg Young around 2010. It is a rule about models. The object model you write through and the object model you read through are allowed to be different objects, in different stores, with different schemas.

CQRS is what happens when you take CQS seriously enough to notice the two sides also want different data.

A team that splits its handlers into PlaceOrderCommand and GetOrderQuery classes, keeps one schema, and announces it has adopted CQRS has adopted CQS. That is a perfectly good thing to have adopted. It buys none of §1's table.

Why is "read model" a slightly unfortunate name?

The write model is the one that validates commands and owns the business rules. It is Asha's ledger and her counter. Normalised, transactional, one per bounded piece of the domain.

The read model is a store shaped like one family of questions. It is one of Dev's chalkboards. Denormalised, disposable, and there are usually several.

The name misleads because a read model is not a view of the write model. It is a separate stored answer, maintained from the same facts. Think "chalkboard", not "window".

What is an event, what is a projection, and what is a bookmark?

An event is a fact that a command succeeded. OrderPlaced(seq=17, total=120.00). Past tense, immutable, and it carries a sequence number.

A projection is the code that consumes events and updates a read model. That is Dev.

A checkpoint is the projector's bookmark. It says: I have applied everything up to sequence N. It is the pencil mark Dev leaves in the ledger margin so he knows where to start next time.

The checkpoint is the most under-rated of the three. It is what makes a projector restartable. It is what makes duplicates detectable. It is what makes lag measurable. In §10.5 it is half of the correctness argument.

Kafka calls it a consumer offset. Debezium calls it an LSN. It is the same object.

What exactly is the consistency window?

The consistency window is the time between an event committing on Asha's side and becoming visible on one of Dev's boards. It is how far behind the board is, right now.

It is not a constant. It is a distribution, and §10.2 gives it edges. Every number in this lab that matters is a property of that distribution.

8. What are the mental models, in five objects and two promises?

In one breath: One guarded ledger, many chalkboards, one clerk per board. Two promises hold it together: a sale and its announcement commit together, and replaying the announcements twice changes nothing.

The simple model: what is the shop doing?

One guarded ledger, many chalkboards, one clerk per board.

Changes go through the counter, which checks the rules and writes the ledger. Clerks copy from the ledger onto boards, each board pre-answering one question. Everyone reads boards. Erase a board and a clerk rewrites it from the ledger's history.

The engineering model: what are the five objects?

State it as five objects and two invariants, because that is all it is.

Object What it holds Who it is in the shop
Write store Current state, normalised. The truth Asha's ledger
Event feed An ordered, append-only sequence of facts, each with a sequence number the ledger read in order, newest last
Projector A loop: read after checkpoint → apply → advance checkpoint Dev
Read store One denormalised table (or index, or document) per question one chalkboard
Checkpoint One integer per projector, per read model Dev's pencil mark in the margin

Invariant 1, atomicity at the source. A state change and its event commit together or not at all. This is the transactional-outbox guarantee. Without it the feed can describe a write that was rolled back.

Invariant 2, effectively-once application. Applying the feed from sequence 0 to sequence N produces the same read-store state, regardless of how many times any individual event was delivered. It also holds regardless of how many times Dev crashed on the way. §10.5 is entirely about buying this.

Given those two, one property follows. It is the property people mean when they say "eventual consistency".

Stop writing. Wait. Every read model converges to exactly what a from-scratch replay would produce.

Your eventual-consistency lab already established what kind of promise that is. It is a liveness property. It promises about the limit and says nothing about any present moment, and no finite observation can falsify it.

That is why the rest of this lab measures the window rather than testing the promise. The promise is not testable. The window is.

Where does the shop story lie to you?

Three places, and all three are load-bearing.

Dev is not one clerk. Real projectors run in parallel for capacity, and parallel clerks can update a board out of order in a way one clerk never could. §10.4 and §11.6.

Chalk does not double-write. A clerk who is interrupted and starts over does not accidentally add today's revenue twice. Code does, and it is the most common real corruption in this pattern. §10.5 and §11.7.

The boards are not "a few minutes behind". That phrase describes a distribution with a tail, and the tail is where every incident lives. Replace "a few minutes behind" in your head with "a random variable with a cliff in it".

10. What is the actual mechanism, built one forced step at a time?

In one breath: Five designs. Each one is the simplest thing that could work after the previous one broke, and each break is a specific number you can read off a table.

This is the spine of the lab. We do not start at the final architecture. We start at the simplest thing that could work, and each step is taken because a specific thing went wrong.

10.1 — v1: what if Dev updates the boards inside the sale itself?

The smallest possible split: one database, one transaction, and the boards are never behind at all.

Keep one database. Add a second set of tables shaped for reading. When a command commits, update those tables in the same transaction. In the shop, Dev stands at the counter and updates the boards before the cashier hands Priya her receipt.

BEGIN;
  INSERT INTO orders ...;                          -- write model
  INSERT INTO order_items ...;
  INSERT INTO customer_dashboard (...) VALUES (...);   -- read model, inline
  UPDATE daily_revenue SET total = total + 120 WHERE day = '2026-09-01';
COMMIT;

This is often called CQRS-lite, and it deserves more respect than it gets. Look at what it already has.

Meera's dashboard query is now SELECT * FROM customer_dashboard WHERE customer = 'priya'. No joins, no aggregation. §4b's reads-times-cost-per-answer is gone.

The consistency window is exactly zero. Every guarantee you had with one model, you still have. Priya sees her own order the instant it commits. There is no lag to monitor, no projector to restart, no rebuild to schedule.

It is one transaction, so there is no outbox, no feed, no checkpoint.

For a very large fraction of systems that think they need CQRS, this is the correct final answer. Say so out loud in design reviews.

🔮 Predict. v1 has zero staleness and acceptable latency. What forces anyone to give that up? There are three answers and only one of them is about performance.

Reveal The three are listed immediately below. If you said "it gets slow", you found the third and least important one. The two that actually force the change are about where the read store lives and what happens when it is down — neither of which is a speed problem.

What breaks it.

  1. The read store cannot always be in your transaction. The moment one of your read models is Elasticsearch, Redis, ClickHouse, or another service's database, there is no transaction that spans it. You would need a distributed transaction, and your two-phase-commit and distributed-transactions labs already established what that costs and why nobody does it here.
  2. Availability is now coupled the wrong way. If the search index is down, checkout fails. You have made the least important component a dependency of the most important one. That is backwards, and it is not fixable within v1.
  3. Latency is coupled. Row 8 above is fine at 21 ms. It is not fine when one read model's update occasionally takes 400 ms because of a lock or a remote call. That 400 ms is now inside Priya's checkout.

Points 2 and 3 are the same shape. Synchronous coupling means the write path inherits every read model's worst day. That is the requirement v2 exists to break, and breaking it creates every remaining problem in this lab.

10.2 — v2: what happens when Dev walks over on a timer?

Write the fact down in the same transaction as the sale, and let Dev pick it up later. Asha's counter is now insulated. And the window is born.

Write the event to a feed inside the write transaction. That is the outbox rule. Then let a separate process apply it later.

-- command side, one transaction
BEGIN;
  INSERT INTO orders ...;
  INSERT INTO events (seq, type, payload) VALUES (17, 'OrderPlaced', ...);
COMMIT;

-- projector, separately, forever
loop every 500ms:
    BEGIN;                                     -- on the READ store
      rows = SELECT * FROM events WHERE seq > :checkpoint ORDER BY seq LIMIT 100;
      for each row: apply(row);
      UPDATE projector_state SET checkpoint = :lastSeq;
    COMMIT;

Now checkout is insulated. Its transaction contains one extra small INSERT and nothing else. The read models can be anywhere, in any technology, and can be down without checkout noticing.

The structure is completely regular. Say the rule in words before the symbols. An order waits for half of Dev's nap. Then it waits while Dev works through whatever piled up ahead of it. Then it waits for the board to be written.

Three of those pieces have short names. The nap is the poll interval. How busy Dev is, meaning his work arriving divided by his capacity, is ρ (rho). The work itself is one apply plus one checkpoint commit.

mean window ≈ (poll / 2) · (1 + ρ) + apply + checkpoint

Three Greek letters run through the rest of this lab. Each is a thing in the shop first, a sentence in words second, and a symbol only third.

In words once more, because it is the formula the whole lab hangs on: half the nap, stretched by how busy the clerk is, plus the time to do the work.

Why is the drain term ρ·poll? Because an event arrives at a uniformly random point in the nap, so it waits poll/2 on average. Then it waits for the projector to chew through the events ahead of it in the same batch. At utilisation ρ, that pile takes ρ·poll to clear.

Check it. At ρ = 0.96 the formula gives 250·1.964 = 491 ms, and the simulator gives 491 ms.

Now read the practical consequence off that formula. At low utilisation, which is what a healthy projector looks like, the window is essentially poll/2 and nothing else.

Your consistency window is a config value you chose. Probably without thinking about it. Probably by copying it from another service.

Visibility is quantised. Because the applies and the checkpoint share one transaction, a whole batch becomes visible at one instant. Dev does not chalk one number at a time; he steps back and the whole board changes at once.

So the window does not shrink smoothly toward zero for lucky events. Events cluster at batch commits. That is why the p50 of 297.9 ms sits almost exactly at the mean, and the p95 jumps to 522 ms. The distribution is closer to a sawtooth than a bell.

10.4 — v4: what breaks the moment there are eight Devs?

Streaming fixed the floor. Capacity is still one clerk. Hire eight and one stall rewrites three orders permanently.

Capacity is still one projector's 487.8 events/s. When Asha's order rate approaches that, the obvious move is more workers.

The obvious way to do it is wrong, and it fails so rarely that it will reach production.

Hand events to N workers round-robin. Now consider one order. OrderPlaced(ord-1) at t. OrderCancelled(ord-1) 50 ms later. Different workers. Normally the placed event is applied first because it arrived first, and everything is fine.

Then one worker stalls. A GC pause, a slow read-store shard, a lock. One second.

🔮 Predict. 20,000 orders over ten minutes, 8 workers, one single 1-second stall. How many orders end up permanently wrong on the board?

Reveal Three. If you said zero, you assumed arrival order survives round-robin hand-off, and it does not survive one stalled worker. If you guessed hundreds, you overestimated: the damage needs two events for the same order to straddle the stall, which is rare. Three out of twenty thousand is 0.015%, and that rarity is exactly what makes it reach production.
Setup Cancelled orders left looking alive Max apply delay
8 workers, round-robin, no stall 0 4 ms
8 workers, round-robin, one 1 s stall 3 989 ms
8 workers, partitioned by order id, one 1 s stall 0 990 ms

Three orders out of twenty thousand, 0.015%, where the CANCELLED event was applied first and the later PLACED event overwrote it.

Those three orders are now permanently shown as active in the read model. Not stale. Not eventually consistent. Wrong forever. And a rebuild recreates the state correctly, so the two disagree and nobody knows why.

The fix is the one your kafka lab already taught. Route by key. All events for one order go to the same worker, so they are applied in order relative to each other. Events for different orders are still parallel.

The third row shows what that does. Zero corruption, and the stall turns into 990 ms of extra latency for that partition.

Partitioning by key converts a correctness failure into a latency failure. That is the whole trade, and it is almost always the right one, because latency failures are visible and correctness failures are not.

10.5 — v5: what happens when Dev is interrupted mid-board?

This is the only step about being right rather than fast, and it cannot be skipped.

The projector dies mid-batch. It has applied 60 of 100 events and has not yet committed its checkpoint. It restarts, reads from its old checkpoint, and applies those 60 events again.

In the shop: Dev is halfway through chalking today's revenue when he is called away. He comes back, sees his pencil mark still at the old entry, and adds the same sales to the board a second time.

Whether that is harmless or catastrophic depends on two independent properties.

🔮 Predict. Two protections are available: writing the board in a way that is safe to repeat, and moving the pencil mark in the same stroke as the chalking. You can have both, one, or neither. How many of the four combinations produce a wrong revenue number?

Reveal Exactly one: neither. If you guessed two or three, you assumed the protections stack. They do not — each one alone closes the hole completely, and the matrix below shows three zeros and one 5.39%.

Here is the full matrix. 200,000 events, batch 100, one crash per 1,000 applies, 210 crashes.

Apply is idempotent? Checkpoint in the same transaction? Revenue error
yes yes 0.00%
yes no 0.00%
no yes 0.00%
no no +5.39%

Either protection alone is sufficient. Having neither reports revenue 5.39% too high. And this is the part that makes it dangerous: nothing is broken, no error is logged, no alert fires.

The number on Meera's board is simply wrong, and it drifts further every time Dev restarts.

The two protections are genuinely different mechanisms, and you should know which one you have.

Idempotent apply means re-applying an event is a no-op. This is a property of how you write the projection, and it is mostly a matter of avoiding accumulation.

-- NOT idempotent: applying twice double-counts
UPDATE daily_revenue SET total = total + 120 WHERE day = '2026-09-01';

-- idempotent: applying twice is the same as applying once
MERGE INTO customer_dashboard USING (...) ON (order_id = 'ord-1')
  WHEN MATCHED THEN UPDATE SET status = 'PLACED', total = 120.00
  WHEN NOT MATCHED THEN INSERT (...);

-- idempotent accumulation: guard with the sequence you have already applied
UPDATE daily_revenue SET total = total + 120, last_seq = 17
  WHERE day = '2026-09-01' AND last_seq < 17;

Upserts keyed by the aggregate's identity are naturally idempotent. Counters and sums are not, and that is where the 5.39% came from. Any projection with a += in it needs the third form.

Atomic checkpoint means the applies and the checkpoint advance commit together, so a crash rolls back both and the batch is simply retried. Dev chalks the board and moves his pencil mark in one indivisible stroke.

This is available only when the checkpoint lives in the read store. You write it to a projector_state table in the same database, in the same transaction. It is the cheaper protection when you can have it, and §13.2 explains the very common production situation where you cannot.

The blast radius is the batch size. With neither protection, the damage scales directly with how much work a crash throws away.

Batch size Revenue error What Meera's board says
1 +0.09% essentially right
10 +0.49% half a percent high, nobody notices
100 +5.39% the canonical setting, visibly high
500 +32.92% a third too high

At batch 2000 the reported revenue is 3.4× the truth. Same crash rate, same events, same code. Only the batch size changed.

And there is a second, subtler batch limit that applies even when you are fully correct. Every replayed event is itself exposed to the crash hazard, so a large enough batch spends most of its time replaying.

Name the quantity in words first: how many crashes you expect inside one batch. That is the batch size multiplied by the chance of crashing on any one event. Written short, it is B·h.

Batch B·h (expected crashes per batch) Wasted replay ÷ useful work
100 0.10 0.05×
500 0.50 0.33×
1000 1.00 0.76×
2000 2.00 2.43×
5000 5.00 27.13×

At B·h = 5 the projector does 27 units of wasted work per unit of progress. It is still perfectly correct, because the atomic checkpoint guarantees that. It just barely moves.

Engineers call this a poison batch: a batch large enough that a crash within it is likely, so it rarely completes, so it retries, so it crashes.

The governing quantity is the dimensionless product B·h, confirmed at two independent hazard rates. Replay ratios were 0.13 / 0.29 / 0.70 / 1.96 at B·h = 0.25 / 0.5 / 1 / 2, against 0.05 / 0.33 / 0.76 / 2.43 at the other rate.

Push it further and the projector stops being a projector. At B·h = 12, with batch 3,000 and one crash per 250 applies, it had applied 1.5% of the feed before a work budget of 60× the useful work ran out. It was not slow. It had no forward progress at all, and it would have stayed that way indefinitely.

Keep B·h well under 1. If your projector crashes once per thousand events, batches of 100 are comfortable and batches of 5,000 are a trap.

Note that this is a self-inflicted failure mode. It is caused entirely by the batch size you chose, and it gets worse exactly when the system is already unhealthy, because that is when the crash rate rises.

  3. Read-your-own-writes by waiting. Return the event's sequence number from the command. Have that user's next query block until the projector's checkpoint passes it. Precise, costs a little latency for one user, and requires plumbing the sequence number through your API.
  4. Route that specific query to the write model. Correct, and a legitimate escape hatch. But if you find yourself doing it for most screens, you have discovered that this subsystem did not want CQRS.

11.2 — THE ONE THAT MATTERS: what happens when a command asks a chalkboard?

This is the failure the entire lab is built around. It is the one that turns a latency property into lost money.

Omar writes an inventory check. It is the obvious thing to write.

handlePlaceOrder(cmd):
    sold = SELECT units_sold FROM product_stats WHERE sku = cmd.sku   -- READ MODEL
    if sold >= stock: reject("out of stock")
    ... accept, write, emit OrderPlaced ...

It reads beautifully. product_stats is fast, it is already there, it is what the product page uses.

And it is a correctness bug. The read model is by construction behind. So every order that arrives inside the consistency window sees a stock level that has already been spent.

In the shop: the cashier glanced at the chalkboard instead of the ledger. The board says one keyboard left. It has said that for three minutes, and eleven people have bought it.

One unit left. Here is how many orders get accepted for it.

Setup Mean window Mean oversells p99 Worst seen
λ=40/s, poll 2000 ms 1160.9 ms 46.66 97 117
λ=40/s, poll 500 ms 297.2 ms 11.95 29 41
λ=40/s, poll 100 ms 65.1 ms 2.61 9 15
λ=40/s, streaming 8.1 ms 0.33 2 5
λ=400/s, poll 500 ms 456.5 ms 182.54 325 385

One unit of stock. Twelve orders accepted on average, forty-one in the worst case.

The expected count is exactly what you would derive by hand. It is the order rate multiplied by the window. At 40 orders a second and a 297 ms window that is 11.87, against 11.95 in the run.

Now read the table for the lesson, because the lesson is not "the window was too wide".

Tightening the window from 2000 ms to streaming took the oversell from 46.66 to 0.33. It never reached zero — and it cannot, because there is always some window. You cannot fix a decision made on stale data by making the data less stale. You can only move the decision.

The fix is to put the check where the truth is. Back to the ledger.

handlePlaceOrder(cmd):
    BEGIN;
      UPDATE stock SET units = units - 1
        WHERE sku = cmd.sku AND units >= 1;   -- write model, conditional
      if rowsAffected = 0: rollback; reject("out of stock")
      INSERT INTO orders ...; INSERT INTO events ...;
    COMMIT;

One conditional write on the write model, and the oversell is exactly zero. At any window, any load, any poll interval.

It is the same conditional-write move your optimistic-vs-pessimistic-locking and idempotency labs kept finding in different costumes.

How to find this in an existing codebase. Grep your command handlers for reads of any projection table. Every hit is either a bug, or a deliberate, documented decision that the staleness is acceptable for that rule. In my experience most hits are neither. They are someone reaching for the convenient table.

The read model may still show stock. Showing is fine. Deciding is not.

11.6 — Why is reordering permanent, and how would you ever see it?

Covered as a mechanism in §10.4. Here is what makes it a failure rather than a design note.

Three orders in twenty thousand end up in a state Asha's ledger does not agree with. Silently. With no error and no retry.

And it is permanent. The read model is not behind. It has converged to the wrong value.

The detection story is the problem. Lag is zero. Error rate is zero.

The only thing that would catch it is a reconciliation job: something that periodically compares a projection against a fresh replay, or against the write model. Almost nobody has one. §13.4 says what to do about that.

11.7 — Why is a crash mid-batch worse than reordering?

Also covered mechanically, in §10.5. What makes it nastier than §11.6 is that it is cumulative.

A reordering corrupts specific orders once. A non-idempotent accumulator over-counts a little on every crash, forever, and the error only grows.

The 5.39% in §10.5 is 210 crashes' worth. Run for a year and the number is unbounded, and there is no point at which anything alerts.

The tell, if you ever suspect it: rebuild the projection into a second table and compare. A correct projection rebuilds to the same numbers. A drifting one does not, and the difference is your accumulated error.

11.8 — What does "just rebuild it" actually cost?

"Read models are disposable, just rebuild them" is the pattern's proudest claim. It is true. And it comes with a bill nobody computes in advance.

Rebuild time is not history divided by throughput. While you rebuild, live traffic keeps arriving and must also be applied.

So say it in words first: your rebuild speed is not Dev's speed. It is Dev's speed minus the rate at which new sales arrive. Written short, with H for the history size:

rebuild time = H / (μ − λ)

Here μ is Dev's capacity in events per second, and λ is the live arrival rate. Replaying 50 million events at a capacity of 487.8/s gives this.

12. What are you actually guaranteed, and what are you not?

In one breath: You are guaranteed the rules are enforced once, in one place, and that every board can be rebuilt. You are not guaranteed that any board is current, that two boards agree, or that the window has a number.

Guaranteed, given the two invariants of §8:

  • Business rules are enforced exactly once, on the write side. This is the guarantee that licenses everything else. The read side may be arbitrarily denormalised and duplicated because it makes no decisions.
  • Commands are atomic. Rejected means no trace anywhere. Accepted means recorded and announced together.
  • Convergence. Stop writing, wait, and every read model reaches exactly the state a from-scratch replay would produce. This holds provided applies are idempotent or checkpoints are atomic (§10.5), and provided per-key order is preserved (§10.4).
  • Rebuildability. Any read model can be recreated from the feed. This is what makes read-side schema change cheap and read-store choice free.

Not guaranteed:

  • That a query reflects the write that just returned. 89.4% of the time it does not, at a 500 ms poll (§11.1). Priya is right and the system is fine.
  • That two read models agree with each other. 100% of the time they do not (§11.5). Meera's list and Meera's total are not one picture.
  • That the window is bounded. It is bounded only while ρ < 1, and ρ is not visible in the lag metric (§11.3). Arjun's graph will not tell him.
  • Anything about write-side scale. CQRS scales reads and shapes. Asha's counter scales by the usual means — partitioning, bigger boxes — and this pattern does not help it. Believing otherwise is §14.4.

The assumptions all of that rests on:

  1. The outbox invariant holds: state and event commit together.
  2. The feed preserves order per key, and the projector respects it.
  3. Applies are idempotent, or checkpoints are atomic, and B·h is well under 1.
  4. No command reads a read model. If this one is false, none of the above guarantees mean anything, because the system is now making decisions from a store that is defined to be behind.

Traded away, permanently: the INSERT then SELECT sees it programming model.

That loss infects UX design. It infects integration testing, because every test now needs to wait for a projector. It infects on-call, because every incident starts with "is the projector behind?".

It does not go away with maturity. It is the price, and §14.7 is about pretending otherwise.

A good answer sounds like "The boards are safe because they only ever get looked at. Nobody uses one to say yes or no to anything. The moment a cashier checks a board before agreeing to a sale, everything changes. The board being a few minutes old stops being a small annoyance. 13. What does this look like in production, and what do you actually operate?

In one breath: The arrow this lab drew is one of three real things, the bookmark may not be yours to keep, and the work that never ends is the monitoring rather than the split.

13.1 Where does the feed actually come from?

This lab drew an arrow between Asha's ledger and Dev. In production it is one of three things.

An outbox table polled or streamed by the application. Simplest, and it keeps the checkpoint in your own database. Which means the atomic checkpoint of §10.5 is available to you. Covered by transactional-outbox.

CDC off the write database's log. Debezium reading the Postgres WAL or the MySQL binlog. No application change at all. Events describe row changes rather than domain facts, which is a real semantic difference your change-data-capture lab covers in depth.

A broker the command side publishes to, usually Kafka. Usually fed by one of the first two rather than written to directly. Writing to the database and the broker separately is precisely the dual-write bug the outbox exists to prevent.

13.2 Why does Kafka take one of your two protections away?

This matters more than it sounds, and it is the most useful production fact in this lab.

🔮 Predict. §10.5 gave you two protections and said either one alone is enough. You move your feed to Kafka. How many do you still have?

Reveal One, and you do not get to choose which. If you assumed both survive, the reason they do not is that one of them requires the bookmark to live in the same database as the boards, and Kafka keeps the bookmark itself.

In §10.5, the atomic checkpoint worked because the checkpoint was a row in the read store, written in the same transaction as the applies. Dev's pencil mark was on the board's own frame.

When your feed is Kafka, the checkpoint is a consumer offset, and it lives in Kafka. There is no transaction spanning your read store and __consumer_offsets. So:

A Kafka-fed projection is structurally the "non-atomic checkpoint" column of §10.5's matrix. Idempotency is not optional for it — it is the only protection you have.

Two consequences. First, if you are on Kafka, audit every projection for accumulation and convert every += to an upsert or a sequence-guarded update.

Second, there is a well-known way to buy the atomicity back. Store the offset in the read store yourself, in the same transaction as the applies, and seek to it on startup instead of using Kafka's committed offset. Kafka supports this through seek() on partition assignment. Flink and Debezium do variants of it, and it is the standard move for exactly-once sinks.

13.3 Which read stores can you actually use?

The freedom to pick a store per question is the practical payoff people actually enjoy. Elasticsearch for search, Redis for hot dashboards, Postgres tables for reports, ClickHouse for analytics. One feed, many stores.

Note the cost of each addition. Every store is another projector, another checkpoint, another lag metric, and another entry in §11.5's disagreement table.

13.4 What should Arjun put on the dashboard?

In priority order, and the first is the one usually missing.

  1. Projector utilisation, busy time divided by wall time. The only leading indicator of §11.3's cliff.
  2. Age of the oldest unapplied event, per projector. Not event count (§11.4).
  3. Time since the projector last made progress. This distinguishes "behind" from "dead". It is your eventual-consistency lab's point exactly: the delivery clause's death produces no event, so you must alarm on the machinery's heartbeat.
  4. Rebuild headroom: capacity minus arrival rate, and the implied rebuild time for your current history size. Compute it quarterly, before you need it.
  5. Reconciliation residual: periodically rebuild a projection into a shadow table and diff it. This is the only thing that catches §11.6 and §11.7. Almost nobody runs it. Everyone who has been burned once does.

13.5 How do real systems give a user their own writes back?

The sequence-number-and-wait approach from §11.1's third option has a standard shape.

The command returns its event sequence. The client sends it back as a header or a cookie. The query layer either waits for the projector's checkpoint to pass it, or routes that one query to the write model.

This is the same mechanism as a session token for bounded staleness in a replicated database. It is the same idea as your causal-consistency lab's session guarantees.

13.6 How do you rebuild at scale — the answer to §11.8?

Three real options, and you should know which one you have.

Blue/green projections. Build v2 into a new table alongside the live v1, from sequence 0, while v1 keeps serving. When v2 catches up, flip reads. This is the standard answer, and it is exactly the snapshot plus delta plus cutover protocol from your change-data-capture and partitioning-and-rebalancing labs.

Parallel replay. Rebuild with far more workers than the live projector uses. The ordering constraint is per key, so replay parallelises well if you partition by key. This is how you turn 6.6 days into hours, and it is why §10.4's keying decision pays off twice.

Reduce the history. Snapshots, or a compacted topic, or simply not keeping ten years of events for a projection that only needs ninety days.

The one thing you must not do is discover during an incident that the first option takes six days.

13.7 Which frameworks do this, and where do you meet it?

Axon for Java, EventStoreDB, Marten and Lagom all package this.

Most production CQRS is not built on any of them. It is command handlers, an outbox, a consumer, and idempotent upserts, exactly as drawn in §8.

If you are on Spring, the honest description of a typical implementation is @Transactional command handlers writing an outbox table, Debezium or a scheduled poller draining it, and @KafkaListener projections doing upserts.

Where you meet it in the wild: order management, where an "orders" page is almost always a projection. Banking and ledger systems. Inventory. And anywhere an audit trail and read scale have to coexist.

13.8 What did this lab deliberately leave out?

  • Event schema evolution. Events outlive the code that wrote them, so rebuilds must be able to read five-year-old events. Versioned event types and upcasting are the standard answers. event-sourcing covers them, and the concern is much sharper there.
  • Multi-tenancy and privacy. Deleting a user's data from a write model is easy. Deleting it from a feed that is replayed forever is not.
  • Backpressure between a fast write side and a slow projector, beyond "the window grows".
  • Exactly how to size a read store. Treated here as a service with a fixed cost per apply, which is a real simplification.

14. Which common wrong models will you meet?

In one breath: Eight sentences people say about CQRS that are not true. Three of them are confusions with a neighbouring idea, and five of them make teams adopt it for the wrong reason.

14.1 "CQRS is event sourcing" — is it?

No, and this is the most common conflation. It is the one that makes people think CQRS is exotic.

CQRS says: read through a different model than you write through. The write model in this entire lab stores current state, normal rows in normal tables, and emits events as a feed. Delete the feed and Asha's ledger is still complete and correct.

Event sourcing says: the events are the state. There is no current-state table to fall back on. You derive state by folding the stream, as your event-sourcing lab covered.

They compose well. Event sourcing needs something like CQRS to be queryable at all. But each stands alone, and most production CQRS is not event sourced.

If you take one thing from this section: you can adopt CQRS on Tuesday without adopting event sourcing, and you probably should.

14.2 "CQRS means two databases" — does it?

No. It means two models.

§10.1's v1 is CQRS with one database, one transaction, and a zero-width consistency window. It is the right answer more often than the full asynchronous version.

The number of databases is a deployment detail chosen later, driven by §10.1's three breaking points.

14.3 "CQRS means eventual consistency" — must it?

Only the asynchronous variant does. v1 is synchronous and has none.

The eventual consistency is a consequence of decoupling, not of splitting the model. Conflating the two makes people reject the whole pattern because they cannot afford the window, when the version without a window was available.

14.4 "CQRS scales writes" — does it help Asha's counter?

No. It scales reads and shapes.

The command side is untouched, and if anything v1 makes it slower. Look again at §10.1's tables: 5 ms to 21 ms of service time for eight inline read models. A team adopting CQRS to fix write throughput has misdiagnosed.

14.5 "The read model is a cache" — is a chalkboard a cache?

Superficially similar, structurally different. And the difference decides how you operate it.

Cache Read model
Population on demand, on miss eagerly, from the feed
Correctness mechanism invalidation (a guess) replay (a derivation)
On a miss falls back to the source there is no fallback; a miss is the answer
Staleness unbounded and unmeasured a window you can measure and alarm on
Recovery re-warm rebuild deterministically

The third row is the practically important one. A cache miss is a slow correct answer. A read model miss is a wrong answer, and that is why projection correctness in §10.5 needs the care it does.

14.6 "Make the window smaller and staleness stops mattering" — does it?

No, and §11.2's table is the counterexample.

Going from a 2 s poll to streaming took oversells from 46.66 to 0.33. That is a 140× improvement that is still not zero, and cannot be.

Narrowing the window changes the probability of a stale-data decision. It never changes its possibility. Only moving the decision to the write model removes it.

This is the same shape as your distributed-locking lab's conclusion about shortening a lease. It reduces the window, not the possibility.

14.7 "We'll adopt CQRS across the service" — should you?

No. CQRS is a per-subsystem decision. You take it where §4b's reads-per-change ratio is high and the staleness is genuinely tolerable.

Applied uniformly, it imposes the window on subsystems that gain nothing. And every one of those is a candidate for §11.2's bug, because the engineer working there has no reason to have internalised why a chalkboard must not be consulted.

Start with CRUD. Adopt v1 where the shape war hurts. Adopt v2 and beyond only where v1's three breaking points actually bite.

14.8 "Splitting the models is the hard part" — is it?

No. The split is a day's work.

The permanent work is everything in §13.4. Lag monitoring that measures age rather than count. A utilisation metric nobody asked for. A rebuild rehearsed before it is needed. And a reconciliation job that catches the two silent corruptions.

Teams that fail at CQRS rarely fail at the split.

Say it back. You have finished the production layer. A colleague says "we should do CQRS, we're getting big". Without using any of §16's vocabulary, say what you would ask them first, and what would make you say no.

A good answer sounds like "I would ask how many times a day the data changes, and how many times a day someone looks at it. If people look far more often than things change, doing the work up front is cheap and worth it. If things change more often than people look, we would be doing more work, not less. And I would say no outright if anything in the code decides whether to accept an order by reading the summary tables — that has to move first."

How were the numbers in this document produced?

Every figure above comes from a discrete-event simulator. The same simulator is embedded in visualization.html, so you can reproduce any figure by opening the page and setting the controls. The document and the page share one engine and agree digit for digit.

The model, stated so you can argue with it:

  • Commands commit to the write store as a Poisson process at rate λ. Each accepted command appends exactly one event, a sequence number and a commit timestamp, in the same transaction as the state change.
  • A projector reads events after its checkpoint, applies each to a read store, then commits the checkpoint. One apply costs applyMs; committing the checkpoint costs ckptMs; one batch holds at most batch events.
  • The applies and the checkpoint share one read-side transaction, so every event in a batch becomes visible at the same instant, at the batch commit. Visibility is quantised, not smooth. This matters in §10.2.
  • Projector capacity is therefore μ = 1000·batch / (batch·applyMs + ckptMs) events per second.

Canonical setup, used wherever a figure does not say otherwise: λ = 40 events/s, applyMs = 2, ckptMs = 5, batch = 100, poll = 500 ms, 15 simulated minutes. That gives μ = 487.8 events/s and ρ = 0.082 — a projector loafing at 8% utilisation, which is what a healthy one looks like.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 A model answer:

Recording a sale and reporting on sales want opposite things. Recording wants careful rules and small, exact updates. Reporting wants totals that are expensive to work out from scratch. If both go through one set of tables, the reports slow the sales down — they hold the database's workers for a long time while a sale needs one for a moment — and the reports stay slow, because the tables are laid out for recording, not for answering. So: keep the strict tables for recording, and keep separate tables that already hold the answers, updated whenever something changes. The answer tables are a little behind. For reporting that is fine.

The first half of that covers the contention; the last three sentences cover the arithmetic. If your version only said "reports are slow", add the contention: at 6 reports/second, checkout's p99 went from 23 ms to 1,590 ms without checkout changing at all.

1.2 Permission is granted by the fact that nothing depends on the read model being right at any particular instant — it makes no decisions.

The smallest change that revokes it: have a command read from a read model. One SELECT in one command handler. That is exercise 6.2, and it turns a latency property into an oversell of 12 units per unit of stock.

1.3 They have CQS — Command–Query Separation, Meyer 1988 — which is a rule about methods, not models. It is genuinely useful: it makes handlers easier to reason about and test. It buys nothing from §1's table, because both classes still hit the same schema on the same connection pool, so the reports still block checkout and the joins still run per query.

1.4 "Read model" suggests a view of the write model — something derived live, like a SQL view or a projection in the mathematical sense. It is not: it is a separately stored answer, maintained by a process, that can be wrong if that process is broken. "Answer table", "materialised answer" or the lab's "chalkboard" all carry that better. The misunderstanding it prevents: that a read model is automatically consistent with the write model, and therefore safe to validate against.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 Formula: mean ≈ (poll/2)(1 + ρ) + apply + ckpt. = 400 × 1.1 + 7 = 447 ms. Anything in 400–460 is a good prediction. The thing to get right is that it is dominated by poll/2 and barely touched by anything else at low ρ.

3.2 (a) The mean window does not change — 8.12 ms at batch 10, 100 and 2000. (b) Capacity rises: 400.0 → 487.8 → 499.4/s.

If you predicted a wider window you made the same mistake I did when designing this lab. The batch is opportunistic — it takes whatever has arrived, which at ρ = 0.08 is about one event, so a bigger limit changes nothing. Batching costs latency only when you wait to fill the batch, and that waiting has a name: it is a linger timer, and a poll interval is a linger timer. That is the entire 297 ms vs 8.1 ms difference between v2 and v3.

3.3 390 → 478/s (ρ 0.80 → 0.98): mean window 445 → 509 ms. A 23% traffic rise bought a 14% window increase — nothing.

478 → 498/s (ρ 0.98 → 1.02): mean window 509 → 8,661 ms. A 2% traffic rise multiplied it by 17×, and the number is no longer meaningful anyway, because past ρ = 1 the window grows with how long you have been over capacity (14.3 s → 29.0 s → 57.9 s → 118.2 s as the run doubled).

The lesson is not "watch out near ρ = 1". It is that lag gives you no warning at all — it is flat across the entire safe range, so you must monitor projector utilisation as a separate signal.

3.4 2-second poll: 46.66 on average, p99 97, worst seen 117. Streaming: 0.33 on average, worst 5.

Both numbers are λ × window: 40 × 1.161 = 46.4, and 40 × 0.0081 = 0.32.

The prediction that matters is the second one. Most people predict zero for streaming. It is not zero, and it cannot be, because there is always some window.

3.5 100%. Identical configuration, identical load, identical code — they disagree on every single sampled page load, with a mean gap of 10.1 events. Independent projectors have independent checkpoints and there is no instant at which both are in the same place. No amount of tuning fixes this; only putting the two answers in one read model behind one projector does.

3.6 Positive, and about +5% (measured: +5.39%).

Positive because the failure mode is re-applying, and re-applying an accumulator adds again. The Kafka detail in the question is the important part: the offset lives in Kafka, so there is no transaction spanning the read store and the offset store, so you are structurally on the non-atomic row of §10.5's matrix and idempotency is your only protection — which += does not provide.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Switch the projector from polling to streaming: mean window 297.2 ms → 8.1 ms, a 37× reduction.

  • What it cost: a notification path (LISTEN/NOTIFY, a broker, a CDC stream) and a long-lived connection to maintain, plus a process that is now event-driven rather than a cron-like loop.
  • What it did not change: capacity, which is 487.8/s either way. Streaming fixes the floor of the window. The ceiling is untouched.

Other single changes that also qualify — cutting the poll to 10 ms — are worse answers: they get you to a 65 ms window at poll 100 ms while multiplying the projector's wake-ups and its load on the feed, and they still leave a timer.

4.2

Quantity 2 ms 10 ms Moves?

Which pages you: almost certainly none of them. A lag alert set anywhere sensible (say 5 s) is nowhere near 465 ms. The quantity that moved most — ρ — is the one nobody graphs. You have gone from 8% to 40% utilised, and you will find out at 100%.

4.3 Catches that the old one missed: the §11.4 slowdown. When applies go from 2 ms to 24 ms, 10,000 events stop being 0.4 minutes of work and become 105.5 minutes — the same count, a 264× different meaning. An age alarm notices because age is measured in the unit your users and your SLA are written in.

Catches later: a sudden burst on a healthy projector. 10,000 events can pile up in a couple of seconds during a spike and be drained in 24 seconds, and the count alarm fires immediately while the age alarm never reaches 60 s. That is usually the correct behaviour — you did not want to be paged — but it is a genuine loss of sensitivity, so if you care about bursts, alarm on both, with the count alarm as a ticket rather than a page.

4.4 You move to the non-atomic checkpoint column — §10.5's second and fourth rows. You lose the atomic checkpoint, so idempotency becomes your only protection.

What to audit: every projection statement for accumulation. Anything of the form SET x = x + :v is now unsafe. The fixes are an upsert keyed by the aggregate's identity, or a sequence-guarded update (SET total = total + :v, last_seq = :seq WHERE ... AND last_seq < :seq).

The alternative worth knowing: store the offset in the read store yourself, in the same transaction as the applies, and seek() to it on partition assignment. That buys the atomicity back and is the standard exactly-once sink pattern.

4.5 At ρ = 0.92 with μ = 487.8/s, a 50 M-event rebuild takes 367 hours — 15 days.

  1. Blue/green with a parallel replay. Build v2 alongside v1 with many more workers than the live projector uses; the ordering constraint is only per key, so replay parallelises. 8× workers turns 15 days into under 2 days. Drawback: the extra workers load the same read store, so real speed-up is less than linear, and you need double the storage during the cutover.
  2. Reduce H. Snapshot the read model as of some sequence and replay only from there, or shorten retention. Drawback: you give up the ability to rebuild from zero, which was one of the pattern's main benefits — now the snapshot is load-bearing state you must also protect.
  3. Rebuild against a replica or a restored copy of the feed, off the production read store entirely, then swap. Drawback: cutover complexity, and the delta accumulated during the rebuild still has to be applied.

The answer that is not available: reducing λ. That is why §13.4 says compute this number quarterly.

The reasoning, step by step:

  • First tick at t = 500. Events committed at or before 500: seq 1, 2, 3 — that is exactly batch, so the batch is full. Cost = 3 × 2 + 5 = 11 ms, so it commits at 511, and all three become visible together.
  • The batch was full, so the projector drains rather than sleeping. At t = 511 nothing new is available (seq 4 committed at 520), so it sleeps to the next tick.
  • Tick at t = 1000. Available: seq 4, 5. Cost = 2 × 2 + 5 = 9, commits at 1009. Not a full batch, so it sleeps.
  • Tick at 1500. Available: seq 6. Cost = 7, commits at 1507.

5.2

Three events, one visibility time, three windows: because the applies and the checkpoint share one transaction, visibility is quantised by the batch — every event in a batch becomes visible at the same instant regardless of when it committed. The window is measured from each event's own commit, so it differs even though visibility does not. This is why the window's distribution is a sawtooth rather than a bell.

60 ms later, 438 ms longer: seq 3 commits at 460 and just catches the 500 ms tick (window 51). Seq 4 commits at 520 and misses it by 20 ms, so it waits for the next one (window 489). The poll interval is a cliff, not a gradient — missing a tick by a millisecond costs a whole interval. That is the mechanism behind the whole poll/2 term.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 — Idempotency is not ordering. Smallest scenario: OrderPlaced(ord-1) and OrderCancelled(ord-1) 50 ms apart, handed to different threads; one thread stalls (GC pause) and the cancel is applied first, then the place overwrites it. Order ord-1 shows as active forever. Measured: 3 orders in 20,000 with one 1-second stall. Violated: convergence — the read model has converged to a state the write model does not agree with. It is not stale; it is wrong, permanently. Fix: partition by key, which converts the corruption into 990 ms of latency.

6.2 — A command reading a read model. Smallest scenario: one unit of stock, two orders 10 ms apart. Both read units_sold before the first order's event is visible, both are accepted. At 40 orders/s with a 50 ms window you oversell about 2; at the lab's 500 ms poll, 12 on average and 41 at worst. Violated: the invariant the write model exists to enforce. Fix: a conditional write on the write model — UPDATE stock SET units = units - 1 WHERE sku = ? AND units >= 1. Note the alert in the quote is irrelevant: a 50 ms lag alert firing correctly still leaves you overselling.

6.3 — The dual write. Smallest scenario: the Postgres commit succeeds, the process dies before the Kafka publish. The order exists and no event describing it ever will, so every read model is permanently missing it and a rebuild does not fix it — the feed has no record. Violated: invariant 1, atomicity at the source. Fix: the transactional outbox — write the event to a table in the same transaction and relay it separately. "We retry until it succeeds" does not help, because the process that would retry is the one that died.

6.4 — Correct, and still broken. The claim is true: with an atomic checkpoint, a batch of 10,000 is perfectly correct at any crash rate. The flaw is progress, not correctness. Every replayed event is itself exposed to the crash hazard, so the governing quantity is B·h. At B·h = 5 the projector wastes 27× the useful work; at B·h = 12 it applied 1.5% of the feed before hitting a work budget of 60× and would have stayed there indefinitely. Violated: liveness. The projector is not wrong, it is stuck. Fix: size the batch so B·h ≪ 1 — and note the trap that h rises exactly when things are already going badly.

6.5 — Two eventually-consistent things are not one eventually-consistent thing. Smallest scenario: the item list shows 5 orders while the total shows the sum of 4, because the two projectors are at different checkpoints. Measured: two projectors with identical 500 ms polls whose timers fire 250 ms apart disagree on 100% of page loads. Violated: nothing formal — each model is individually fine. What is violated is the user's reasonable assumption that the page shows one moment in time. Fix: put anything that must be internally consistent in one read model behind one projector, or timestamp the two facts separately and show them as such.

6.6 — A threshold in units of work. Smallest scenario: the read store slows from 2 ms to 24 ms per apply. 5,000 events was 10 seconds of drain; it is now over 50 minutes. The alarm's threshold did not change and its meaning changed by 264×. Violated: the alarm's relationship to the SLA it was written for. Fix: alarm on the age of the oldest unapplied event, which is in the same unit as the SLA.

6.7 — Statement atomicity is not idempotency. Smallest scenario: the statement commits, the projector dies before the offset is committed, the statement runs again. Revenue is 100 too high, forever, and nothing errors. Violated: effectively-once application — invariant 2. Fix: guard it — SET total = total + :amt, last_seq = :seq WHERE day = :day AND last_seq < :seq — or restructure the projection so the row is an upsert of a computed value rather than an accumulation. "Atomic" answers a different question ("can it half-happen?") from "idempotent" ("what if it happens twice?").

6.8 — Uniformity as a value. Smallest scenario: a subsystem with R/N = 0.1 (§4b's last row) now pays 11.11 CPU-hours/day of projection work to save 1.11 of query work, and has acquired a consistency window it gains nothing from — and an engineer working there, with no reason to have internalised §6's rule, writes exercise 6.2's inventory check. Violated: nothing technical. This is a judgement failure that manufactures opportunities for all the others. Fix: per-subsystem adoption, driven by the R/N ratio and by whether staleness is genuinely tolerable there.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 Ordered by cost:

  1. Render from the command result. The confirmation page already knows the order succeeded; it does not need to query anything. Free, and it removes the problem entirely for the single most common screen. Suits: confirmation pages, "saved" toasts, anything showing back what the user just submitted.
  2. Stream instead of poll. 89.4% → 0.0% at every think time, for a config and deployment change. Suits: everything, and it is the highest-leverage single change available. Caveat: at ρ = 0.92 streaming's own window is 89.6 ms mean / 197 ms p99, so it is not a licence to stop thinking.
  3. Sequence-number wait (read-your-own-writes). Precise, per-user, and it costs a little latency for exactly the user who needs it. Suits: lists and detail pages the user was just redirected to.
  4. Route that query to the write model. Always correct. Suits: rare, high-stakes screens. If you need it for most screens, this subsystem did not want CQRS.

API contract change for option 3: the command response stops being an empty 200 OK and starts carrying a consistency token — the event's sequence number, or an opaque encoding of it. The client returns it on subsequent reads (header or cookie). The query layer either waits until the relevant projector's checkpoint ≥ the token, or routes to the write model, and it must cap the wait and degrade rather than hanging. This is the same mechanism as a session token for bounded staleness in a replicated database.

7.2 Over the next several minutes:

  • The projector resumes from its checkpoint and drains at μ, while λ keeps arriving, so it closes the gap at μ − λ, not μ. Four hours at 40/s is 576,000 events; at 487.8/s capacity that is 576,000 / 447.8 ≈ 21 minutes.
  • The lag graph shows a sawtooth-free straight line down — a linear descent at a constant slope. Age falls at (1 − λ/μ) seconds per second: here, 0.92 seconds of age per second of wall clock.
  • The user-visible symptom is that read models are hours stale and then progressively less so. Crucially, writes were never affected — that decoupling is the feature you paid the window for.

Catch up or rebuild? Let it catch up if (a) the feed still retains everything back to the checkpoint, and (b) μ − λ gives an acceptable time. Rebuild if the retention window was exceeded during the outage — because then the checkpoint points at events that no longer exist, and catching up would silently skip them. That is the check to do first, and it is the one people forget: an expired offset is not a stalled projector, it is a permanently wrong read model.

7.3

  • (a) Is it wrong? You cannot tell from the read model — a wrong number looks exactly like a right one. Rebuild the projection into a shadow table from sequence 0 and diff. This is the only reliable test, and it is why §13.4 lists reconciliation.
  • (b) By how much? The diff gives it exactly, per row. Expect the error to correlate with crash/restart/rebalance history rather than with traffic — which is itself confirmation of the mechanism.
  • (c) Fix without downtime: blue/green. Build the corrected projection alongside, let it catch up, then flip reads. Do not try to "repair" the live table with a correction delta — you cannot compute one reliably, because you do not know how many times each event was applied.
  • (d) Prevent: convert accumulations to upserts or sequence-guarded updates; add reconciliation to the schedule; and if you are on Kafka, treat idempotency as mandatory rather than as good practice, because the atomic checkpoint is not available to you.

7.4 Sketch:

  • The command handler commits, and returns the event's sequence number as a token: {"status":"ok","consistency":"orders:184423"}.
  • The client stores it — cookie for browser flows, header for API clients — scoped to that user's session, and sends it with subsequent reads.
  • The query layer compares the token to the relevant projector's checkpoint. If the checkpoint is behind, it either waits (with a short cap, e.g. 200 ms) or routes to the write model.
  • Projector 30 s behind: waiting is not an option. The query layer must detect that the wait would exceed the cap and immediately fall back — to the write model if the query can be answered there, otherwise to serving stale data with a visible "updated a moment ago" marker. Silently hanging is the worst outcome and the most common implementation bug.
  • Second device: the token lives in the first device's session, so the second device has no token and reads normally — it may not see the write. That is correct and expected: read-your-own-writes is a session guarantee, not a global one. If you need it across devices, the token must be stored per-user server-side, which turns a cheap mechanism into a stateful one.

7.5 With 12 partitions keyed by order id:

  • Guaranteed: events for a single order are in one partition, so their relative order is preserved end to end. Order-level reordering is impossible.
  • Possible: duplicates. A rebalance revokes partitions from a consumer that had applied records but not yet committed its offset. The new owner resumes from the last committed offset and re-delivers them. This is unavoidable — the offset lives in Kafka and cannot join your read store's transaction.
  • Possible: reordering across orders. Partition 3 may be seconds ahead of partition 7. Anything that assumes a global sequence across orders is broken.
  • What the projection must do: be idempotent per event (upserts or sequence-guarded updates), never assume a global order, and never derive a cross-order aggregate that must be internally consistent at an instant. If you need an exact global total, it belongs in one partition or is computed from the write model.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 The arithmetic: R/N = 100,000 / 1,000,000 = 0.1. With J = 40 ms, read-time cost is 1.11 CPU-hours/day and projection-time cost is 11.11 CPU-hours/day. Projections would cost 10× more than the thing they replace, and add a consistency window, a projector to operate, and a rebuild to plan.

CQRS is a bet on a ratio, and this system is on the wrong side of it. "Getting ready for scale" has it backwards: growing this workload makes projections relatively worse, because N grows with it.

What to propose instead: index the specific slow queries; cache if the same answers repeat; and if reads are interfering with writes, a read replica — which fixes contention without adding a projection. Revisit if R/N moves, which it will if you add a dashboard that a million people look at.

8.3 "Rebuildable in 4 hours" is a constraint on spare capacity, since rebuild time = H/(μ − λ). Turning it into requirements:

  1. μ − λ ≥ H / 14,400 s. With H = 50 M that is a required spare capacity of 3,472 events/s — over 7× the lab's whole projector. So either μ must be much larger, or H much smaller, or the replay must be parallel.
  2. Replay parallelism ≥ P, where P is chosen so P × (μ − λ) meets (1). This is only sound if the feed is partitioned by key, so the constraint propagates back into a design requirement on the feed, not just an operational one.
  3. Retention ≥ the rebuild window plus margin, and H bounded by retention or snapshots — otherwise H grows monotonically and requirement (1) silently expires. Add an alert on projected rebuild time, not just on H.

The broader lesson: a rebuild SLO is not an operational nicety, it constrains your partitioning key and your retention policy at design time.

8.4 Smallest realistic system where it loses money: one product, one unit of stock, a checkout that validates against a projection. At 40 orders/second with an ordinary 500 ms poll you accept 12 orders on average, 41 at worst, for one unit. Every one beyond the first is a refund, an apology, and possibly a regulatory problem if you charged for it.

Why "reduce the window" cannot be the fix: the window is never zero in any asynchronous design. Going from a 2 s poll to streaming took the oversell from 46.66 to 0.33 — a 140× improvement that still is not zero, and the remaining 0.33 is not a tuning failure, it is the definition of the design. Reducing the window changes the probability of a wrong decision, never its possibility. Only moving the decision to where the truth is makes it zero.

Which decisions can safely be made from a read model? Precisely those whose correctness does not depend on the read model being current — which is a real and non-empty class:

  • Decisions that are advisory and will be re-validated later ("show this banner", "suggest this product", "pre-fill this form").
  • Decisions on monotone predicates that cannot be invalidated by events still in flight. If a fact can only ever become more true — an account was created, an order was placed at some point — then a stale "no" may be wrong but a stale "yes" cannot be. Note the asymmetry: you may act on the yes and must not act on the no.
  • Decisions where the cost of being wrong is bounded and acceptable, and someone has actually written down what that bound is.

Everything else — anything conserving a scarce resource, anything enforcing a limit, anything the business would call a rule — belongs on the write model.

8.5 With a magic transaction spanning the write store, the read store and Kafka, what disappears:

  • §10.5 in its entirety: no crash matrix, no idempotency requirement, no atomic checkpoint distinction, no B·h.
  • §11.7's silent drift, and §11.6's reordering (a global transaction implies a global order).
  • The outbox pattern itself — §13.1's whole first bullet.

What survives unchanged:

  • The consistency window, if the projection is still asynchronous — atomicity is not synchrony. If you make it synchronous to close the window, you are back at §10.1's v1 and you have re-acquired its latency and availability coupling.
  • §11.2's oversell, if a command still reads a read model. Nothing about atomicity stops you from validating against a store that is behind.
  • §11.5's cross-model skew, unless every read model is in the same transaction as every other — at which point you have one giant synchronous system.
  • §11.8's rebuild arithmetic. H/(μ − λ) does not care about transactions.
  • §4b's economics. The reason to do this at all is untouched.

What you would have paid: the availability and latency of a distributed transaction — your write path now blocks on the slowest and least reliable participant, which is exactly what §10.1's breaking points 2 and 3 were about, and exactly what your two-phase-commit lab priced. That is the honest summary of the whole pattern: CQRS is what you build when you decide that price is too high, and every remaining complication in this lab is change from that decision.

8.6 Runbook for "orders-dashboard projector age > 60 s":

  1. Is it moving? Compare the checkpoint now with the checkpoint 30 seconds ago. This is the first check because it splits the entire problem space: a checkpoint that is advancing means behind; one that is not means stuck or dead, which is a completely different investigation (crash loop, poison batch, lost lease, expired offset).
  2. Is it behind because of input or because of output? Compare λ now with λ an hour ago, and compare the projector's utilisation ρ now with before. λ up and ρ up means a traffic event. λ flat and ρ up means the read store got slower — this is §11.4, and it is the one that surprises people because nothing about your service changed.
  3. Will waiting fix it? Compute μ − λ. If positive, age falls at (1 − λ/μ) seconds per second and you can state an ETA; if λ ≥ μ, waiting will never fix it and you must shed, scale the projector, or accept unbounded staleness. This is the check that turns the page into either "I have an ETA" or "I have an incident".
  4. Is the feed still retaining what the checkpoint points at? If the offset has expired, catching up would silently skip events, and the correct action is a rebuild, not patience. Check this before anyone "restarts it to see if that helps".

Two things deliberately not in the top four: restarting the projector (it destroys the evidence for check 1 and fixes only the case check 1 would have identified) and looking at error rates (all four of this lab's silent failures have an error rate of zero).
