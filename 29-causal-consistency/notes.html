Lab 29 · Why Distribution Is Hard

Causal consistency — never show the reply before the message

Tell a friend: Amira posts a photo of her dog in London. Ben, in Frankfurt, reads it and writes "lovely dog!" underneath. Cara opens the app in Singapore and sees Ben's comment sitting on its own, with no post above it. Nothing crashed and nobody misbehaved — Ben's comment simply took a faster road than Amira's photo, and the message carrying it never mentioned that he had read her post first. This lab is one rule that stops that: never show a change without everything that happened before it. You pay for it in waiting and nothing else, and it is the strongest promise you can keep while every region still answers on its own.

The trainer is visualization.html — The Comment Before the Post. What the lab is made of, where every number came from, and where this sits among your other labs: section 19, at the end.

1. Why can nothing be broken and the product still lie?

In one breath: Your app is fast because each region answers from its own copy and passes changes to the others afterwards. That works perfectly until one change is a reply to another, because the reply can arrive first.

Your product runs in several regions so that people everywhere get fast answers. A region answers immediately from its own copy. It passes the change along to the other regions in the background. This is the design almost everyone ships. For one person changing one thing, it is perfect.

It stops being perfect the moment one change is a response to another.

A comment is a response to a post. A reply is a response to a message. "Publish this photo" is a response to "stop letting my manager see my photos". The two changes then travel by different routes, at different speeds. The second one can arrive first. Somewhere, a user is shown a reply to something that, as far as that region knows, has not happened.

The damage runs from mildly confusing to genuinely serious.

What the user sees How bad it is Why it happened
a comment on a post that is not there confusing; looks like a UI bug the comment overtook the post
a reply quoting a message you cannot find support ticket; nobody can explain it the reply overtook the message
a photo published under the old privacy setting a security incident the photo overtook the setting change

Read the third row twice. That is the one that ends up in a postmortem. And the worst part is the same in all three rows: nothing is broken. Every machine did exactly what it was told.

2. What happens to Amira, Ben and Cara in one afternoon?

In one breath: Three people in three cities, one photo, one comment, and two roads of very different quality. The comment gets there and the photo does not.

Meet the three people this lab follows all the way to the end.

Amira is in London. Ben is in Frankfurt. Cara is in Singapore. All three use the same app, and each one is served by the region nearest to them.

Here is their afternoon, in three steps.

  1. Amira posts a photo of her dog. London shows it to her instantly and starts sending a copy to Frankfurt and to Singapore.
  2. Ben reads the post. The copy reached Frankfurt in a moment. He leaves a comment: "lovely dog!". Frankfurt shows it to him instantly and starts sending copies out.
  3. Cara opens the app in Singapore.

One more fact, and it is the only unusual thing in the whole setup. The road from London to Singapore is having a bad afternoon. The road from Frankfurt to Singapore is fine.

Now hold on to this sentence, because everything else in the lab is a consequence of it:

Ben's comment only exists because he read Amira's post.

A human can see that at a glance. Nothing in the system can see it, because nothing wrote it down.

3. What is the obvious design, and why does everyone ship it?

In one breath: Show every change the moment it lands. There is nothing to wait for, so nothing is ever slow — and that is exactly the appeal.

Asha builds the replication path. Her first version is the one you would write too, and it fits on a line:

When a region receives a copy of a change, apply it and start showing it.

There is nothing to wait for, so nothing is ever slow. Every region ends up with the same content eventually. This is what an ordinary asynchronous replicated store does, and it is what you get by default from almost every system in this family.

Asha ships it. Then Cara opens the app.

4. What does Cara actually see?

In one breath: She sees the comment and no post. Nobody is alerted, because from the system's point of view nothing went wrong — and the reason it cannot go right is hiding in the message, not in the network.

Reveal

She sees the comment "lovely dog!", and where the post should be there is nothing at all. Nobody is told anything. Singapore received one change and applied it, exactly as designed.

Now the deeper problem, which is not about speed at all. The message Ben's region sent carries no trace of the fact that his comment was written after reading Amira's post. It says "set comment to 'lovely dog!'" and nothing else. Singapore could not have waited for the post even if it had wanted to. It had no way of knowing there was anything to wait for.

The information was destroyed at the moment the message was built. You have seen this shape of failure twice before. The crdts lab opens with a whole list being sent where one individual change was meant. The google-docs lab opens with a position being sent where an identity was meant. Every time, the wire format threw away what the receiver needed.

Two things stop this being a one-off curiosity.

It is not rare. It just needs an unequal network. Run the same design at six levels of road slowness and the anomaly rate climbs steeply. The design never changes between rows; only the road does.

Extra delay on the slow road Runs that showed an effect before its cause What the row tells you
none 30 of 300 a calm network hides the bug nine times in ten
20 ticks 214 of 300 the same code, on an unequal network, fails most of the time

A consistency test on a calm network proves nothing. That is the same lesson the linearizability lab taught from the other side.

It is not fixed by the tools you already own. A version vector per key, from quorums-and-replication, keeps one key's copies in order. The post and the comment are different keys. A CRDT merge, from crdts, makes each key settle on one value. Neither says a word about the order between two keys — and this problem lives entirely between keys.

5. What are we really asking for?

In one breath: Two demands. Never show an effect before its cause. And never stop answering. Everything difficult comes from wanting both.

State the requirement in plain words, with no technology in it:

If one change was made after seeing another, then no region may ever show the second without also showing the first.

And, because Asha is not willing to give up the thing that made this architecture worth building in the first place:

Every region must still answer immediately, from its own copy, even while cut off from the others.

Now notice what we are not asking for. We are not asking that everybody see changes in the same order. Only that nobody sees an effect before its cause. Two changes that had nothing to do with each other may appear in either order, on different machines, and that is fine.

That deliberate weakening is the whole idea. It is easiest to see against the models you already know. Read the middle column as "the rule we are giving up".

Model The rule you delete What you keep
Linearizability — one order, and it matches real time
Sequential consistency real-time order one order everybody agrees on
Causal consistency one agreed order order only where one thing caused another
Eventual consistency causal order too nothing about order at all

Each step down buys availability and latency, and sells guarantees. This lab is about the third row. §12 explains why that row is a far more interesting place to stand than it first looks.

6. What is the one rule that fixes it?

In one breath: Make every change carry a note of what its author had already seen. Make a region refuse to show a change until it has applied everything on that note.

Here is the rule, and the rest of the lab is either an implementation of it or a consequence of it:

Make each change carry a record of what its author had already seen, and make a region hold a change back until it has applied all of that.

Two halves, and both carry weight.

The first half is record the reason. Ben's message must say more than "set comment to 'lovely dog!'". It must also say "…and the author had already seen the post". Every design in §10 is a different way of writing that second clause compactly.

The second half is wait rather than show something wrong. Singapore already has Ben's comment sitting in memory. The rule does not make it arrive any sooner. It makes the region decline to show it until Amira's post lands.

That is worth saying flatly, because it is the price tag on the entire lab: consistency here is bought with latency and nothing else. No extra messages. No agreement. No coordination. Just waiting.

7. Which six words carry this lab, and where did each one happen?

In one breath: Six terms do all the work. Each one names something you have already watched happen to Amira, Ben or Cara, so you meet the thing first and the word second.

Which word means "could have influenced"?

Ben read Amira's post, and then he wrote a comment. We cannot know that the post shaped what he typed. We can know that it could have. That "could have influenced" relationship is the only kind of "before" that survives having no shared clock, and Leslie Lamport named it in 1978: happens-before.

Three things make it true, and nothing else does:

  1. Two changes by the same person, in the order that person made them.
  2. A change written after reading another change.
  3. Chains: if the first happened before the second, and the second before the third, then the first happened before the third.

Engineers write it with a small arrow. Amira's post happens-before Ben's comment, written post → comment. Read the arrow aloud as "could have influenced", every time, until it stops needing translating.

Papers write the same three rules with single letters. Here they are with the words kept beside them, so you never have to decode a bare arrow.

Written as In words
a → b change a could have influenced change b
b → c change b could have influenced change c
a → c then a could have influenced c as well — that is the chaining rule
b → a the reverse claim; for Amira's post and Ben's comment it is false

The lab asserts both the reading rule and the chaining rule in Tests.reading_a_write_puts_it_in_your_causal_past and in happens_before_is_transitive_across_three_clients, the second across three clients at once.

Why does this need its own word at all? To keep could have influenced apart from happened earlier on a wall clock. The second is unknowable across machines. It is also actively harmful when you rely on it: the quorums-and-replication and crdts labs both counted a replica whose clock ran 60 seconds fast destroying 1,000 of 1,000 genuinely newer writes.

If the name feels familiar, it should. It is the distributed twin of happens-before in the java-memory-model lab. Same name, and the same job: say which writes a reader must see. One machine instead of many.

What do you call two changes that never heard of each other?

Amira sets the album title to "Dog Photos". At the same moment Ben sets it to "My Dog". In the store those two changes read title = "Dog Photos" and title = "My Dog". Neither author had seen the other's. Neither could possibly have influenced the other.

Engineers call that pair concurrent. Read the notation below aloud as "neither one could have influenced the other", and notice that it is defined entirely by two arrows that are absent.

Written as In words
a ∥ b neither a → b nor b → a holds
post ∥ comment said of two particular changes: this pair has no arrow either way

The important trap: concurrent is a statement about knowledge, not about time. Two changes made an hour apart are still concurrent if neither author had seen the other's. Wall-clock distance has nothing to do with it.

This is why happens-before is a partial order rather than a line. Some pairs simply are not comparable. The model needs a word for that gap, and every design decision in §10 is really a decision about what to do inside it. There are plenty of such pairs: one sweep of 300 workloads throws up 17,163 pairs where neither author had seen the other's change.

What is causal consistency, said precisely?

The plain version you already have from §5: you never see an effect before its cause.

The precise version: every region applies changes in an order that agrees with happens-before. Said fully — for every region there is some single ordering of the changes it has applied that respects "could have influenced" and explains every value it ever returned. Two regions may order a concurrent pair differently. That freedom is the definition, not a loophole.

Why does the property deserve a name? Because it is the strongest guarantee that costs no coordination at all. §12 gives the formal result. The practical version is shorter: everything stronger has to stop answering during a partition.

With the rule switched on, Cara sees neither the post nor the comment, rather than the comment alone. That is the test named Tests.causal_delivery_holds_the_effect..., and it is the one behaviour change the whole lab exists to produce.

If that is the promise, what is the machinery called?

The promise is causal consistency. The machinery that delivers it is causal delivery: a region applies a change only once everything that happened before it has been applied.

Written with letters, and with the words kept alongside:

Written as In words
apply w only when every d with d → w is applied show a change only once everything that could have influenced it is already showing

Both words exist because you can have either without the other. A single-copy store is trivially causally consistent and has no delivery rule anywhere in it. And you can implement the delivery rule perfectly and still not have a system anyone would ship — which is the next term.

You have already met this requirement once, as a cost rather than a subject. In the crdts lab, causal delivery was the price of operation-based CRDTs: deliver an add and a remove out of causal order, and the two copies diverged permanently. This lab is that footnote, promoted to the subject.

Why is causal consistency alone not enough to ship?

Turn the rule on, then have Amira and Ben set the same field at the same instant, neither having seen the other. Causality has no opinion about that pair. So each region keeps whichever copy happened to arrive last — and arrival order differs per region.

The wire empties. Nothing is in flight. The regions still disagree, and nothing will ever fix it. Across 400 runs, that happened 400 of 400 times.

So real systems ship causal consistency plus an agreed rule for the pairs causality has no opinion about. Regions that have received the same changes end up in the same state. The COPS paper coined a name for the combination: causal+, short for causal consistency with convergent conflict handling.

The "+" is exactly where the crdts lab plugs in. Its strong eventual consistency is a convergence guarantee with no ordering. This is an ordering guarantee with no convergence. Together they are the whole package, and that is what production systems actually ship.

Which four smaller promises do users actually notice?

Terry and his colleagues at Xerox PARC, building Bayou in 1994, noticed something practical. Users do not experience the whole store. They experience their own session. So they named four promises made to one user rather than to the system, and gave them names you will meet in every vendor's documentation.

Promise What it says What the user notices when it is missing
read-your-writes a read reflects your own earlier changes Amira edits her bio, reloads, and the old bio is back
monotonic reads you never see the store go backwards Cara refreshes and the comment she just read disappears
monotonic writes your changes are applied everywhere in the order you made them Ben's second edit lands before his first
writes-follow-reads a change you based on something you read is never applied before that thing the comment-before-post story itself

Two of those are nearly free. If a user always talks to the same region, the first two cannot be violated at all. The lab counts 0 violations of read-your-writes and monotonic reads with a sticky client. That holds even on the design with no ordering machinery whatsoever.

And here is the theorem worth memorising: all four together are exactly causal consistency. That is why the list is worth learning. It is the property, taken apart into four pieces you can implement, test and alarm on one at a time.

8. What picture should you keep in your head?

In one breath: Every change is a letter, and on its envelope the author lists the letters they had already read. The post office may not put a letter on your desk until every letter named on its envelope is already there.

What if every change were a letter with a reading list on the envelope?

Every change is a letter. On the envelope, the author writes the list of letters they had already read when they wrote it. A post office may deliver a letter to your desk only once every letter on that list is already on your desk. Otherwise it goes in a tray behind the counter.

Follow the afternoon through, in letters:

  • Amira's letter has an empty list. It can be delivered anywhere, immediately.
  • Ben's letter says "I had read Amira's letter first". Singapore holds it in the tray until Amira's letter arrives.
  • Two letters with each other's names absent from both envelopes have no relationship at all. Either may be delivered first, and nobody can complain.

That is the entire mechanism. Everything in §10 is an argument about how to write the list compactly enough to fit on the envelope.

What is the same picture in engineering words?

Changes form a directed acyclic graph: a set of dots with arrows, where the arrows never loop back. Each arrow is "could have influenced". Every region's set of applied changes must be causally closed — if a change is in the set, everything below it in the graph is in the set too.

Reading is then safe by construction. Whatever a region returns, everything underneath it is already there.

Where does the story lie to you?

Three places, and each one is a whole later section.

The reading list grows forever. A person's list of "letters I have read" only ever gets longer, so the envelope eventually costs more than the letter. After 320 operations it holds 102.5 entries per letter. §10 v5 and §10 v6 are two different fixes.

The post office does not resend. Holding a letter in the tray is not the same as noticing one was lost. A letter destroyed in transit is simply gone, and every letter behind it waits forever. Causal delivery is a when rule and never a whether rule. Resending is anti-entropy's job, from the gossip-protocols lab. The lab asserts both halves: healing a partition delivers nothing, and one anti-entropy round delivers everything.

The reader may walk to a different post office. All of this orders regions against each other. It says nothing at all about a user whose next request is routed somewhere else. When clients roam, 334 of 500 of them fail to see their own change. §10 v8 is that story.

10. How would you build this, one broken attempt at a time?

In one breath: Eight designs. Nothing below is finished. Each version is what a careful engineer would build next, given exactly what just broke.

Asha does not design this in one go, and neither would you. She builds the obvious thing, watches it fail, and fixes the thing that failed. Dev, Arjun and Priya each hand her one idea along the way. Follow the eight steps and you will have derived every real implementation in the field.

v1 — what happens if you show every change the moment it lands?

Already done, already broken. Across 300 random workloads, 160 produce an anomaly. §4 gave the diagnosis: the message carries no record that one change was based on another.

The obvious repair is to put something in the message that says "this one comes later". The next three versions are three different somethings.

v2 — can a wall-clock timestamp put them back in order?

Dev suggests the thing everybody suggests first. Stamp every change with the sending machine's clock reading — in Java, whatever System.currentTimeMillis() returns at that instant. Have each region apply changes in timestamp order. Ben's comment was written after Amira's post, so it carries the larger number, so it is applied second.

🔮 Predict: Does stamping the wall-clock time fix the comment-before-post problem?

Reveal

No, and it fails twice over, for two unrelated reasons.

First, clocks disagree. You already have the number from two earlier labs: a replica whose clock ran 60 seconds fast destroyed 1,000 of 1,000 genuinely newer writes. If Ben's machine is slightly behind Amira's, his comment gets the smaller stamp. The ordering is inverted, with no error reported anywhere.

Second, and more fundamental: a region cannot know when it is safe to apply. Singapore is holding a change stamped 14:32:09. Is something stamped 14:32:07 still in flight? A timestamp tells you where a change belongs in the order. It does not tell you the order is complete. Every design from here on has to answer that second question too, and most of the difficulty in this lab lives there.

If you guessed that better clock synchronisation would rescue this, you fixed only the first reason. The second has nothing to do with clock quality. A perfect clock still cannot tell a region that nothing older is on the way.

This is why the lab ships no wall-clock design for you to run. It fails for a reason you have already seen counted twice, and running it again would only teach you that clocks are still bad.

v3 — what about a counter that cannot lie?

Arjun's turn. Replace the clock with a counter that cannot run backwards relative to a message. Each region keeps one integer. Every change bumps it. Every message carries it. On receiving a message you set your integer to whichever is larger, yours or theirs — in code, max(yours, theirs). Lamport described this in the same 1978 paper, so it is called a Lamport clock.

It has the property the wall clock does not: if one change happened before another, its counter is strictly smaller. So order by the counter, and hold anything that would apply out of order.

There are two problems. One is small. One is fatal.

🔮 Predict: Ordering by a counter that respects happens-before must give you causal consistency, surely?

Reveal

Only if every link delivers in the order things were sent. Here are the two rows side by side:

Lamport order, reordering links      3/300 runs      3 anomalies
Lamport order, FIFO links            0/300 runs      0 anomalies

Here is the leak. A region decides it is safe to apply a change stamped 7 once it has heard from every other region at 7 or higher. But "heard from" means whatever actually arrived. Suppose a later change from London, stamped 9, overtakes an earlier one from London, stamped 4, on the same link. The receiver's evidence now says "London has reached 9". So it releases the change it was holding, while London's change 4 is still in the air.

If you guessed yes because the counter respects happens-before, you were assuming the counter arrives in counter order. Ordering by a single counter is causal only over links that preserve send order. Engineers call such a link FIFO, for first-in-first-out.

That is a real dependency on your transport. It is quietly violated by UDP, by multi-path routing, and most commonly of all by sharding, where two keys travel on different connections. Scene 3 of the narrated run is exactly that case.

Now the fatal problem, which no amount of FIFO repairs. "Safe to apply" means hearing from everybody. A region that goes quiet stalls every other region's queue. It does not matter whether it is quiet because it is idle, slow, or cut off. From the outside, those three look identical.

The exhibit below prices that. "Held on arrival" counts changes that arrived and were made to wait. "Write-ticks hidden" totals how long changes sat invisible. Read the last row against the third.

Twelve times the invisibility of the best causal design, for exactly the same promise. The reason is that a single counter forces a total order. It makes everyone wait for everyone, including for changes they never depended on.

Put a number on that waste. Of the 17,163 pairs where neither author had seen the other's change, a single Lamport counter puts 87.2% of them into a definite order. Every one of those orderings is invented, and every one costs latency.

v4 — what if each change names what it was based on?

Stop inferring. Asha's next version has each change carry the actual set of changes its author had already seen, and a region holds it until every one of them has been applied.

Each change needs a name for this to work. Give it two parts: which region accepted it, and how many changes that region had accepted before. Amira's post is London's first, so its name is R0#1. Ben's comment is Frankfurt's first, so its name is R1#1. Engineers call that pair a dot. The point of it is that any region can mint one alone, with no coordination at all. You used this exact record in the crdts lab.

The whole idea is three lines of Java:

// Cluster.deliverable, case DEPS — the whole idea, three lines
Set<Dot> need = rule.nearestOnly() ? w.nearest() : w.deps();
for (Dot d : need) if (!r.applied.contains(d)) return false;
return true;

This is correct with no assumptions about the network at all: 0 anomalies in 300 runs, no FIFO requirement, and no waiting on silent regions. It is also what COPS does.

v5 — how much of that list can you throw away?

Most of that list is redundant, and the story shows you why. Ben's comment depends on Amira's post. Cara's like depends on Ben's comment. So Cara's like does not need to name the post at all. The comment already implies it, and the comment cannot be applied without it.

Keep only the nearest dependencies, the ones no other dependency already covers.

// Cluster.nearest — a dep is redundant if another dep has it in ITS past
for (Dot other : deps)
    if (allWrites.get(other).deps().contains(d)) { covered = true; break; }

🔮 Predict: Throwing away most of the list must weaken something. What does Cara stop being protected from?

Reveal

Nothing at all. This is a pure saving, and that is the surprise.

The table above is the result: 1.4 entries per change, flat, against 102.5 and climbing. And Tests.full_deps_and_nearest_deps_give_the_same_visibility asserts across over 100 workloads that the two produce identical region states. Not similar. Identical.

If you guessed that some rare ordering would slip through, you were treating the dropped names as extra evidence. They are not evidence, they are echoes. A dropped name is one that some kept name already forces to be present. This is COPS's central optimisation, and it is what makes naming dependencies practical at all.

v6 — can you compress it to one counter per region?

There is a still more compact encoding, and it is the one most people have heard of. Instead of naming individual changes, say how much of each region's history the author had already seen.

Picture a row of three tally marks on the envelope, one per region. Amira's row says "I had seen none of anybody's changes". Ben's says "one of London's, and my own". A zero means "none of that region's changes at all".

Engineers call that row a vector clock: one counter per region, kept together as a vector. Here is the row of marks, first in words and then as notation.

The row of marks In words Written as
London 0, Frankfurt 0, Singapore 0 Amira's post, written before she had seen anything [0,0,0]
London 1, Frankfurt 0, Singapore 0 Ben, having read the post and nothing else [1,0,0]
London 1, Frankfurt 1, Singapore 0 the comment itself, once Frankfurt has accepted it [1,1,0]

The delivery rule then becomes two checks, and both are needed:

// Cluster.deliverable, case VV
int src = w.id().replica();
if (w.vv()[src] != r.vv[src] + 1) return false;      // exactly the next write from its author
for (int j = 0; j < r.vv.length; j++)
    if (j != src && w.vv()[j] > r.vv[j]) return false;  // nothing else it saw is missing
return true;

The first line is the "no gaps" check: this must be exactly the next change from its author, not one further along. The second is the "nothing missing" check: everything else the author had seen must already be here. The lab plants a broken build with the first line removed, to prove that check earns its place.

🔮 Predict: A vector clock is 3 numbers where the pruned dependency list was 1.4 names. Does the vector clock hold changes back for less time, or more?

Reveal

More. Look again at the table above: 16,279 write-ticks hidden for the vector clock against 7,428 for the explicit list. More than double the waiting, for exactly the same promise.

If you guessed less, you were pricing bytes on the wire rather than time on the clock. Smaller metadata is not the same as less waiting, and here the two pull in opposite directions.

The reason is a distinction worth carrying out of this lab. The two designs answer different questions. The dependency list records what the person saw, exactly. A vector clock records what that person's region had already applied when the change was accepted, which includes plenty the person never looked at.

The second is a safe over-approximation. It never under-orders, so it is always correct. But it invents dependencies that were never there. Of the 17,163 pairs where neither author had seen the other's change, the per-region counters still put 68.9% into an order.

what the CLIENT saw   — exact.        0% false ordering.   list, ~1.4 entries
what the REGION had   — safe superset. 68.9% false ordering. 3 counters
a single counter      — total order.   87.2% false ordering. 1 counter

That is the real trade, in one sentence: precision costs bytes, imprecision costs latency. All three rows are correct. None of them ever shows an effect before its cause.

v7 — with the rule working perfectly, do the regions agree?

Turn causal delivery on and leave it on. Now have Amira and Ben set the same field at the same instant, neither having seen the other.

🔮 Predict: Causal delivery is working perfectly. Do the three regions end up showing the same value?

Reveal

Not necessarily, and in this lab's runs, never:

causal only (no convergence rule)     replicas left disagreeing: 400 / 400
causal+, one counter per replica      replicas left disagreeing:   0 / 400

Causality has no opinion about a concurrent pair. So each region keeps whichever change arrived last, and arrival order differs per region. Every message has been delivered. Nothing is in flight. The regions permanently disagree, and no further messages will fix it.

If you guessed yes, you were expecting an ordering rule to be a tie-break rule as well. It is not. Ordering says nothing about the pairs it refuses to order.

The fix is one line, and it is not an ordering rule at all. Resolve concurrent changes the same way everywhere. This lab uses a tie-break on the pair (lamport, replicaId), which in words means "the larger counter wins, and if those are equal, the lower-numbered region wins". A real system might use a CRDT merge instead, which is what the crdts lab built.

// Cluster.wins — the "+" in causal+
if (current.deps().contains(incoming.id())) return false;  // ordered: incoming is the past
if (incoming.deps().contains(current.id())) return true;   // ordered: incoming is later
if (!rule.convergent()) return true;                       // concurrent: arrival order — diverges
return incoming.lamport() > current.lamport() ...;          // concurrent: agreed tie-break

Causal consistency plus that rule is causal+, and it is what every production system actually means when it says "causal".

v8 — what about the user who moves?

Everything so far orders regions against each other. Now move a person.

Amira updates her profile in London. Priya has just rolled out a load-balancer change for better balancing, and Amira's next request is routed to Frankfurt, before the copy of her update arrives.

🔮 Predict: Causal delivery is on and working. Does Amira see her own edit?

Reveal

Usually not:

stays on one replica     reads that did NOT see the client's own write:   0 / 500
roams between replicas   reads that did NOT see the client's own write: 334 / 500

If you guessed that causal delivery covers this, look closely at what is happening. Nothing is out of order. Frankfurt has not shown any effect before its cause. Amira's update is simply still in flight, and Frankfurt is answering honestly from what it holds.

What is missing is that the person has a position in history too, and it did not travel with her.

The fix is to give the client a token describing what it has seen, and to have it present that token with every request. The region then waits until it has caught up to that point before answering. MongoDB calls that token afterClusterTime. Bayou called the general idea session guarantees. Exercise 7.1 builds it.

Now the four promises from §7 have a mechanical shape. Read the right-hand column as "where the ordering has to live".

Guarantee What it needs
read-your-writes the client's own position, carried with it
monotonic reads the client's own position, carried with it
monotonic writes per-author ordering between regions
writes-follow-reads cross-author ordering between regions — the hard one

Read the first and third rows together, and one fact jumps out. Session stickiness gives you the two guarantees users actually notice, for free, with no ordering machinery at all. Row one has no causal delivery anywhere in it and still scores zero on both.

That is why a sticky load balancer papers over so much. It is also why Priya's change, removing stickiness for better balancing, is a consistency change wearing an infrastructure costume.

11. What goes wrong, and what does it look like on call?

In one breath: Ten things break. Some break the guarantee, some break only your patience, and telling those two apart at three in the morning is the skill this section is for.

Each of the ten is a short story about Amira, Ben, Cara or the people who keep their app running. After the ten there is one table that lines them all up.

1 · An effect overtakes its cause. This is Cara's afternoon from §4. She sees a comment on a post that is not there, and on the design with no ordering at all it happens in 160 of 300 random workloads. The message carried no record of the dependency, so Singapore had nothing to wait on. Every region is internally fine and will settle down eventually. What is gone is causal consistency, visibly, to a real person. On call it arrives as a bug report nobody can make happen again: "the reply showed up before the message". On a calm network it hits 30 times in 300 rather than 214, which is why nobody can. The narrated run leaves this line behind:

t=6 cara [CAUSALITY] sees comment=lovely dog! (R1#1) but post still reads
      <absent> — the cause post=photo-of-a-dog (R0#1) is invisible here

2 · The same design, on a calm network. Asha's staging environment has three regions in one datacentre and equal latencies between them. She runs the consistency suite and it passes. At zero extra route delay the anomaly shows up 30 times in 300; at 20 ticks of extra delay, 214 times in 300. Nothing about the design changed between those two rows. Only the network did. This is the single most useful sentence in the lab: a consistency test without injected asymmetry is close to worthless. Jepsen's entire method is built on that observation.

3 · Ordering by a single counter over reordering links. Arjun's Lamport design leaks in 3 of 300 runs when links may reorder, and in 0 of 300 when every link preserves send order. The mechanism is v3's: a later change from a region raises the receiver's evidence about that region past an earlier change of its own that is still in flight. The design is still perfectly correct if every link is FIFO. It stops being correct the moment two keys travel on different connections. This is why "we use TCP, so we're causal" is wrong. TCP preserves order per connection, and a sharded store has thousands.

4 · A quiet region stalls everyone. Singapore goes idle for a while. Nobody there is doing anything. London and Frankfurt still cannot release anything, because the single-counter rule needs news from everybody before anything is safe. The price was in v3's table: 89,509 write-ticks hidden against 16,279 for the vector clock, same guarantee. Correctness is untouched. What is lost is liveness of visibility — and an idle region is indistinguishable from a slow one. Systems that need this design send heartbeats purely so that silence cannot be mistaken for lag, exactly as Raft's empty AppendEntries does.

5 · The dependency list outgrows the data. Nothing is wrong here at all, and that is the point. Amira's causal past never shrinks, so after 320 operations each change is carrying 102.5 dependency entries to set one value, and still growing. Correctness is perfect throughout. This is a bill, not a bug. COPS's nearest-only pruning takes it to 1.4, and past that you switch to a vector clock and pay in latency instead.

6 · One slow shard makes unrelated changes invisible. This is the failure that decided the history of the field, so read the numbers slowly. One route slows down. Everything causally after a delayed change must wait, and dependency chains carry that delay into changes about entirely different keys.

Extra delay on the one slow route Write-ticks hidden Multiple of the calm case

4 1,443 about 4×

Correctness holds all the way down that column. What is lost is visibility latency, and it is lost non-locally: Cara waits for something she has no relationship with. Engineers call this the slowdown cascade, and it is the reason causal stores did not become the default. Facebook's Existential Consistency paper looked at their eventually-consistent store and found it was already causal 99.9%+ of the time. So they declined to pay this bill for the remaining 0.1%.

7 · A client is moved to another replica. Priya's load-balancer change from v8. 334 of 500 roaming clients fail to see their own change. Nothing is out of order; the change is simply still in flight. Causal ordering between regions is genuinely intact, and genuinely not helping. What is lost is read-your-writes and monotonic reads — the two promises users actually notice. The fix is the session token the client presents, which MongoDB spells afterClusterTime. Until that exists, session stickiness is load-bearing, and turning it off is a data-correctness change.

8 · Concurrent changes leave the regions disagreeing forever. Amira and Ben rename the album at the same instant. In 400 of 400 runs, the three regions end up showing different values with the wire completely empty. Causality has no opinion about that pair, and arrival order differs per region. This is fully legal causal consistency, which is exactly why it is dangerous. What is lost is convergence. You never ship this. You ship causal+, with either a timestamp tie-break, which loses updates, or a CRDT merge, which costs metadata.

9 · A partition, and then a heal that heals nothing. Singapore is cut off. Every operation Cara performs still succeeds on stale data: 600 of 600 of them. Availability holds throughout, and so does causal consistency. Then the partition heals, and the changes made during the outage are still missing, because causal delivery is a rule about when a received change may be shown, never about whether it arrives. What is lost is recency, and until repair runs, cross-region visibility itself. The background repair loop from gossip-protocols is not optional. Without it, a causal store is a store that quietly forgets. The suite pins both halves down in Tests.anti_entropy_is_what_repairs_a_partition_not_causal_delivery: healing alone delivers nothing, and one repair round delivers everything.

10 · Per-key causality mistaken for causal consistency. This is the most common misunderstanding in the whole area, so it gets the last slot. Build the rule so it waits only for dependencies on the same key. That is precisely what a per-object version vector gives you, and precisely what the quorums-and-replication lab taught you to build. The lab plants it as the bug named same-key-only. Anomalies appear in 99 of 200 runs, and Cara's comment-before-post afternoon replays exactly. Every individual key's history is intact. The whole property is gone. Causality is a statement across keys. A per-key version vector is a convergence tool, not an ordering one.

12. What exactly is promised, and where does the promise stop?

In one breath: The promise is one sentence long. The list of things it deliberately does not promise is much longer, and every item on it has bitten somebody.

What is guaranteed?

Five things hold, and each one has a count behind it.

The core promise: no region ever shows a change without also showing everything that happened before it. All three dependency-based designs score 0 anomalies in 300 runs.

All four session guarantees hold, provided the client keeps its position: read-your-writes, monotonic reads, monotonic writes and writes-follow-reads, all four at 0 violations for causal plus sticky routing.

Availability is total. Every region answers every read and every change immediately, alone, partitioned, forever — 600 of 600 operations on the isolated side of a partition.

Amira's own changes are never delayed. A region applies its own clients' changes with no waiting at all, which the lab pins down in Tests.a_replica_never_delays_its_own_clients_write.

And with the "+", convergence: regions that have received the same changes end up in the same state, 0 of 400 divergent with a tie-break in place.

What is explicitly not guaranteed?

There is no agreed order for concurrent changes. Two regions may serialise Amira's rename and Ben's rename differently. Without a convergence rule they can disagree permanently, which is failure 8.

There is no recency. A causal read may be arbitrarily stale and still be perfectly legal. The lab's own check, Tests.a_causal_read_can_be_arbitrarily_stale, returns a value 100 ticks out of date with zero violations recorded. There is no bound at all. Compare linearizability, where "already answered" pins a read to real time. Here nothing does.

There is no compare-and-set, no uniqueness, and no global invariant. Omar from the compliance team asks whether the app can reserve a seat, or promise that a username is unique. It cannot. Deciding requires agreeing, agreeing requires coordination, and coordination is the thing we gave up. This is the same wall the crdts lab hit at minus two bags of flour.

There are no transactions. Two changes are ordered if one caused the other. They are never atomic. Cara can legally see the first without the second. COPS's follow-up, Eiger, adds read-only and write-only transactions on top, and that is extra machinery rather than a consequence of causality.

There is no delivery. Causal delivery orders what arrives. Loss needs anti-entropy, which is failure 9.

And there is nothing about the client. Ordering between regions says nothing about a person who moves between them, which is failure 7.

What did you trade away, and what did you get for it?

You gave up agreement on the concurrent pairs, and any relationship to real time. In exchange you keep answering during a partition. That is the whole point, and it brings us to the result that makes this model interesting rather than merely weak:

Causal consistency is the strongest consistency model that can be implemented in a system that remains available under network partition.

Two papers make that precise. Attiya and Welch show that stronger models require communication proportional to network delay, so they cannot be available. Mahajan, Alvisi and Dahlin show causal consistency is the ceiling for always-available, one-way-convergent systems.

So this is not "eventual consistency with extra steps". It is the top of the available half of the cap-theorem picture.

15. What does a real system add, and what does Meera watch?

In one breath: The lab is one JVM and three maps. A real system adds five things, and the on-call engineer watches six numbers — one of which matters more than the other five put together.

What is the chain from this lab to a real system?

This lab is one JVM, three regions, one key-value map each, a tick-driven network, and one design at a time. It exists to make deliverable() visible.

A real implementation adds five things, roughly in this order.

  1. Sharding, and the fact that it breaks FIFO. A real store has thousands of partitions, and two keys travel on different connections. This is why the single-counter design is not usable in practice, and why dependency tracking has to be explicit. See failure 3.
  2. Dependency pruning. COPS's nearest-only rule, plus a "these are old enough that everyone certainly has them" cut-off, plus batching. Without it, experiment 3's 102.5 becomes unbounded.
  3. Session tokens. The client-side half from v8: a compact record of the person's position, presented with each request, so that Amira stays ordered even when Priya's load balancer moves her. MongoDB's afterClusterTime over a hybrid logical clock.
  4. Anti-entropy. Causal delivery orders; it does not deliver. The background repair loop from gossip-protocols does.
  5. A convergence rule. The "+" — a timestamp tie-break, or CRDTs from the crdts lab, chosen per data type rather than globally.

A production architecture is then this. Regions, each internally a sharded and locally-consistent store. Asynchronous replication between regions, carrying dependency metadata. A dependency-check queue on the receiving side. A repair loop underneath. Sticky routing plus session tokens at the edge. And, for the small set of operations that must be able to say no, a completely separate coordinated path.

What is this commonly confused with?

Six near-neighbours, one sentence of separation each.

Against eventual consistency. Eventual consistency promises the regions end up the same and says nothing about what you see on the way. Causal consistency constrains the journey and, on its own, promises nothing about the destination. You want both, which is causal+.

Against a per-key version vector. A version vector orders changes to one key and detects conflicts on it. Causal consistency orders changes across all keys. Confusing the two is the most common mistake in this area, and the planted-bug run recreates it at 99 of 200 runs anomalous.

Against sequential consistency. Sequential consistency requires one order that everybody agrees on. Causal consistency lets different regions order concurrent changes differently. Sequential needs coordination; causal does not.

Against "consistent prefix". Consistent prefix says you see some prefix of a single agreed order. It is stronger than causal, because it implies one order, and it is what a single-master system with async replicas naturally gives you.

Against causal delivery. Delivery is the mechanism: hold a message until its dependencies are applied. Consistency is the property. You can have the property without the mechanism, and the mechanism without a usable system.

Against read-your-writes. Read-your-writes is one of the four session guarantees. It is a quarter of the property, and the quarter stickiness gives you free.

5.1 The blank starts at 2 / 6 (checks 1 and 2 pass trivially for return true). The whole rule:

    for (Dot d : w.deps()) if (!applied.contains(d)) return false;

5.2 A workable shape — count how many held writes are blocked on each id and report the worst:

public Dot oldestBlockedDependency(int replica) {
    Map<Dot, Integer> blocking = new TreeMap<>();
    Replica r = reps[replica];
    for (Write w : r.pending)
        for (Dot d : w.deps())
            if (!r.applied.contains(d)) blocking.merge(d, 1, Integer::sum);
    return blocking.entrySet().stream()
        .max(Map.Entry.comparingByValue()).map(Map.Entry::getKey).orElse(null);
}

At a slow route of 32, one id sits at the head of many queues — which is exactly the signature of the slowdown cascade, and why §15 nominates oldest blocked dependency age as the one alarm to have if you may have only one. A queue that is deep but has many different heads is normal load; a queue that is deep behind one id is a stuck upstream.

5.3 Change allHeardAtLeast to skip a replica you have never received anything from. The new problem is immediate: you can no longer distinguish "that region has nothing to say" from "that region's message has not arrived yet", so you release writes that a slower message would have preceded — you are back to failure 1. This is failure 4 from the other direction, and it is why systems that need a total order send heartbeats: not to carry data, but so that silence can never be mistaken for lag. Raft's empty AppendEntries exists for the same reason (see the raft lab).

Rung 6 — Debug

6.1 It encodes "we use TCP, so messages from a given sender arrive in order, so we are causal." It is nearly true — it does give you monotonic writes, one of the four guarantees — which is exactly why it survives review. Smallest breaking story: Amira writes the post (R0), Ben reads it and writes the comment (R1), Cara's region receives the comment. The comment's only dependency is from a different replica, so the rule waits for nothing. Anomalies in 98 of 200 runs; the deterministic story reproduces it exactly.

6.2 quorums-and-replication teaches you to build precisely this, and it is correct for what it was built for: detecting conflicting updates to one object. It is the most dangerous of the three because it is not an implementation slip — it is a correct tool applied to the wrong question, by someone who can defend it. Anomalies in 99 of 200 runs. The one-sentence corrective: causality is a statement across keys.

6.3 It is rare because it only fires when one author's own writes arrive with a gap — #3 landing while #2 is in flight — which needs that author to have written twice in quick succession over a link that reorders. Two writes by different people never trigger it.

Rarer is worse, for three reasons: it survives every test suite and every staging environment; when it does fire it is unreproducible, so it gets closed as "could not repeat"; and it fires most under load, which is when the consequences are largest. The general rule — the same one experiment 2 teaches — is that in consistency work, a low failure rate is evidence about your test environment, not about your design.

6.4 (a) Clocks disagree. You measured this twice already: a replica 60 seconds fast destroyed 1,000 of 1,000 genuinely newer writes in both the quorums-and-replication and crdts labs. If the commenter's machine is slightly behind the poster's, the ordering inverts with no error. (b) A timestamp does not tell you the order is complete. Holding a write stamped 14:32:09, a replica has no way to know whether something stamped 14:32:07 is still in flight — so it either shows things out of order or waits forever. Fixing (a) with NTP does nothing about (b), which is the deeper of the two.

Rung 7 — Worked design discussions

7.1 The session token

What the token contains: the client's causal position — the set (or, in practice, the vector-clock summary) of everything it has seen or written. In MongoDB this is afterClusterTime, a hybrid-logical-clock timestamp; in Bayou terms it is the session's read-set and write-set summary.

What the replica does when behind: it waits until its own applied state covers the token, then answers. It does not answer from a stale state and it does not error immediately — it converts a correctness problem into a latency problem, which is the same trade the whole lab makes.

Measured, on the roaming workload that scores 334/500 failures today:

token=false   read-your-writes=3759   monotonic-reads=1611   writes-follow-reads=356   anomalies=726
token=true    read-your-writes=0      monotonic-reads=0      writes-follow-reads=0     anomalies=0

and on the isolated read-your-writes test, 334/500 → 0/500. Note the guard is needed on both paths: on reads so the client does not go backwards, and on writes so a write made from a fresh region still carries the dependencies the client acquired elsewhere. Guarding only reads leaves writes-follow-reads broken.

When the replica can never catch up (the write went to a region that is now partitioned away): the wait must be bounded. After the timeout you have three honest options and one dishonest one. Honest: fail the request with a retryable error; route the client back toward a region that can satisfy the token; or degrade explicitly and tell the UI it is showing a stale view. The dishonest one — answer anyway — silently converts a latency problem back into the correctness problem you built the token to fix, and it is what happens by default if nobody chooses.

What the client is told: something actionable and specific — "this view may be a few seconds behind" for the degraded path, not a generic error. Note the irony worth stating in a design review: the client-side half is where causal consistency stops being always-available. The store still answers; the session guarantee is what makes you wait.

7.2 Dependency garbage collection

The fact that must be true: every replica that could ever receive a write naming this dependency has already applied it. Then no receiver can ever need it to decide whether to wait, and it can be dropped from the list.

The mechanism: each replica publishes what it has applied (a vector clock); take the pointwise minimum across the membership — the stable frontier — and prune any dependency below it. This is causal stability, structurally identical to the tombstone-GC problem you solved in the crdts lab, and it needs the same two things: membership and liveness, from gossip-protocols.

What breaks if you drop one too early: a replica that never received the dependency receives the dependent write with nothing telling it to wait, applies it, and shows an effect without its cause — failure 1, reintroduced by the optimisation meant to make it cheaper. This is the same shape as the too-early-tombstone-drop failure in the crdts lab, and it is equally unrecoverable: nothing later will notice.

What stalls it: one unreachable replica freezes the minimum, so the frontier stops advancing and lists grow again. The answer is the same as for tombstone GC — after a timeout, evict the silent member and require it to re-bootstrap. Which means, once again, a coordination-free property leaning on a coordinated membership service.

7.3 The slowdown cascade

Two mitigations that keep the guarantee:

  1. Reduce over-ordering. Half of the waiting in the vector-clock design is for writes the author never saw — 68.9% of concurrent pairs treated as ordered. Switching to pruned explicit dependencies more than halves the hidden write-ticks (16,279 → 7,428, measured). You pay in bytes and get latency back.
  2. Make the slow route not be on the critical path. Deliver each write over more than one route, or have a fast replica forward on behalf of a slow one (COPS-style dependency propagation). The dependency then arrives by whichever path is quickest, and one slow shard stops gating unrelated writes. Measure: oldest-blocked-dependency age (exercise 5.2), per replica.

One that abandons it in a controlled way: cap the hold. If a write has waited longer than N, apply it anyway and record the event. You have chosen to serve an anomaly rather than a stall — which is defensible for a like counter and indefensible for a privacy setting. Measure: the rate of forced releases, alarmed as a correctness metric, and broken out by key class so the privacy keys can be excluded from the policy entirely.

What the user experiences in each case: (1) and (2) are invisible — things appear sooner. (3) is invisible until the day it fires, and then it is failure 1 in production with a metric that tells you it happened, which is strictly better than the silent version but is not "no anomaly".

Rung 8 — Worked design discussions

8.1 A multi-region social product

Needs causal ordering, across keys:

  • post → comment → reply. The whole point; a reply visible before its parent is the bug this lab exists for.
  • privacy setting → post. "Remove my manager from who-can-see-this" then "publish" is two writes to two keys with one obvious order, and this is the COPS example. This is the security item.
  • block → post. Same shape: block someone, then post. If the post overtakes the block, the blocked user sees it.
  • follow → timeline entry, upload → attach, delete account → any subsequent write.

Does not need it:

  • like counts — a CRDT counter; nothing causally precedes a like that matters, and delaying likes for ordering is pure cost.
  • presence / "last seen" — an LWW register; losing a concurrent update is fine and staleness is expected.
  • view counts, analytics — no ordering, no per-user guarantee.

The session token lives at the edge: issued and carried by the client (a cookie or header), presented on every request, and enforced by the region. On a load-balancer failover, the new region reads the token and waits — which is the whole reason to have it, and the reason stickiness alone is not a design. Budget for it: a bounded wait plus an explicit stale-view path, per 7.1.

Must NOT be causal:

  • username / handle uniqueness — needs the ability to refuse. Coordinated store, conditional write.
  • payments, credits, anything counted down — the crdts lab's −2 bags of flour, in a different costume.
  • auth token revocation — this one is subtle and worth arguing in the review: revocation must be fast and fail-closed, and a causal store gives you neither bound. Short token lifetimes plus a coordinated deny-list.

What the user sees when a region is behind: their own writes, always (session token). Other people's, late. Never an effect without its cause. The one thing to design deliberately is the stale-view indicator, because the alternative is a user who does not know whether the feed is empty or just behind.

Three metrics before launch: (1) oldest blocked dependency age, p99 per region — the cascade alarm; (2) session-token wait time and timeout rate — the client-side cost, which is where users feel it; (3) privacy-setting propagation lag specifically, separated from everything else, because it is the item where being late is an incident.

The security item: the privacy/block ordering. Getting the like counter wrong is a bug report. Getting "remove access, then publish" wrong publishes private content to the person it was hidden from, and no amount of eventual convergence undoes the moment they saw it.

8.2 Turning it on in a real stack

MongoDB has supported causally-consistent sessions since 3.6, so the change is to open a session per user request and pass it through every read and write — the driver then attaches afterClusterTime and secondaries wait until they have caught up before answering, which is precisely the fix in 7.1. The cost is latency on reads that land on a lagging secondary, and a new failure mode (waiting, then timing out) that your code must handle rather than ignore. Before and after, measure secondary read latency at p99 and the rate of session waits that exceed your budget — the second number tells you whether replication lag, not the feature, is your actual problem. "Just read from the primary" is the answer if your read volume fits on one node: it gives you a strictly stronger guarantee for less complexity, and the honest version of this proposal says so out loud. It stops being the answer when read volume or geography forces secondaries, and the point of causal sessions is that they let you keep the secondaries without the complaints. One caveat to state explicitly: this fixes the client's view, and says nothing about ordering between two different users' writes across regions — if your product has the privacy-setting problem from 8.1, sessions alone do not solve it.

8.3 Where causal consistency is the wrong choice

Seat reservation (the easy one). Booking seat 14C must be able to refuse, and refusing requires knowing what everyone else has done. Causal consistency never blocks and never refuses, so two regions will both accept 14C and then faithfully converge on a state where both did. It is right for everything around the booking — the seat map, the browsing, the party size, a "requested" state — with a single coordinated write at the moment of commitment. Same boundary as the crdts lab: let the weak model carry the intent, and consensus carry the commitment.

Rate limiting (the interesting one). At first it looks like the seat problem — "at most 100 requests per minute" is a global invariant, so it needs coordination. But your distributed-rate-limiting lab already established that nobody actually pays for that: the limit is a business policy with slack in it, so approximate counting with local buckets and background reconciliation is the correct engineering answer, not a compromise. So the verdict is neither "causal works" nor "causal fails" — it is that the invariant was never really an invariant, and the right question is not which consistency model to buy but how much overshoot the policy tolerates. That distinction — between a rule that must hold and a rule that must mostly hold — is worth more in design reviews than any consistency model on the ladder.
