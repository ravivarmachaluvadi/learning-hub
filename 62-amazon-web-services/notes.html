Lab 62 · Running It In Production

Amazon Web Services — a shop where you rent other people's distributed systems

Tell a friend: Asha runs a small payments company and bought one computer to run it on. That computer cost her the festival, eleven months of paying for idle metal, and one very bad 3am. Amazon's answer is a shop: you rent slices of an enormous pool by the second, Amazon publishes a map of which slices die together, and a guard checks your badge before every call. Everything else in the shop is somebody else's distributed system with a price on it, so the hard part is never picking a service. It is knowing which boundary each copy of your data sits in, and whether getting better means asking Amazon for help while Amazon is the thing that is broken.

The simulator is visualization.html, beside this file; section 9 says what to do with it. What this lab is made of, where its numbers come from, and who is in it are all in section 17 at the end.

1. Why does one working program turn into a second job?

In one breath: You wrote a program and it works. Other people need it at 3am on a Sunday, from another country, while your laptop is in your bag. The moment you accept that, you have taken a second job that has nothing to do with writing programs.

You have written a program. It works on your laptop.

But other people need to use it. At 3am. On a Sunday. From another country. And your laptop is in your bag, closed, and moving.

So the program needs to live on a computer that is always switched on, always connected, and is not yours to carry around.

That sounds like one small decision. It is not. It is a second job, and here is the job description: buy computers. Put them in a room with power and cooling and internet. Replace their disks when they die. Guess a year in advance how many you will need. Be the person who drives to that room at 3am.

The cloud is the offer to stop doing that second job and pay someone else, by the second, to do it instead.

What does it cost you if nobody makes that offer? Three bills, and none of them arrives looking like a computing problem.

You spend six weeks and a large cheque before your first customer. You buy for the busiest hour of your busiest day, then own that hardware for the other 8,700 idle hours of the year. And the first disk failure at 3am is your 3am.

What is this lab not going to give you?

There is a version of "learn AWS" that is a list. EC2 is virtual machines. S3 is files. RDS is a database. Lambda runs a function. SQS is a queue.

That list is correct. It takes twenty minutes to read. And it will not help you at 2am when something breaks.

Why not? Because none of it tells you where your data physically is, what fails together, or what you are still responsible for.

You are also not the audience for that list. You have spent fifty-five labs learning the machinery underneath: quorums-and-replication, consistent-hashing, cap-theorem, pacelc, caching, idempotency, partitioning-and-rebalancing, microservices, redis, networking-for-developers, google-spanner, cockroachdb.

Here is the reframing this lab is built on. Everything after it is a consequence.

AWS is a shop where you rent other people's distributed systems. You already know how to read a distributed system's guarantees. What you do not yet know is the shop — where the shelves physically are, what the receipt actually promises, and which parts of the job stay yours after you have paid.

Which three questions decide whether a system survives its first bad night?

The subtitle of this lab is the failure map, the badge, and the meter. Those are the three things that decide the bad night, and not one of them appears on the service list.

The first is the failure map. Which physical boundary does each copy of your state sit inside, and what dies with it when that boundary dies?

The second is the recovery dependency. Does getting better require you to ask Amazon for something while Amazon is the thing that is broken?

The third is the badge and the meter. Who is allowed to make which call, and what stops the bill running away while you sleep? The meter also covers the damage one mistake can do before anybody notices it.

Everything else, including which of the 200+ services to pick, is a lookup you can do in five minutes once those three are in your head. Section 13 gives you the lookup table anyway, organised by which primitive from your earlier labs each service actually is.

2. What happens when Asha buys one computer?

In one breath: Asha buys one machine for her payments company. It is too small for four days a year and too big for the other 361. And when its disk dies at 3am, the record of every payment dies with it.

No jargon in this section. A ten-year-old could follow it.

Asha runs a small payments company called Marigold Pay. Shops use it to take card payments. The software is one program with one job: a shop sends "charge ₹450 to this card", Marigold talks to the bank, and Marigold answers "paid" or "declined".

It has to write down every charge, permanently. That record is the money.

Asha needs a computer for it. So she buys one.

What does buying one computer actually involve?

She has to decide, today, how big it should be. For a business whose size next year she cannot know.

Too small, and the shops get errors on the busiest day of the year. Too big, and she has spent money on a machine that sits at 6% busy for twelve months.

She picks something in the middle. She signs a cheque for the whole thing up front. Then she waits six weeks for it to arrive.

Then it has to live somewhere. Her flat has power cuts, and the internet connection has one wire to it. So she rents space in a data centre, which is a building full of racks with two power feeds, generators, air conditioning and several internet connections.

She drives over, screws the machine into a rack, plugs it in, installs the operating system, and Marigold Pay goes live.

Now watch three things happen to her.

Three coats, one problem — which one hurts most?

The festival. In October, sales across every shop go up seven times for four days. The machine cannot keep up. Requests queue, then time out, then shops start declining cards.

Asha cannot make the machine bigger. It is a physical object, in a rack, 40 kilometres away, and a bigger one is six weeks out. The festival is four days long. She loses it.

The other eleven months. The machine runs at 6% busy. She paid for all of it. She is still paying for the rack space, the power, and the internet.

The 3am. A disk fails. The machine stops. Marigold Pay is down, and nobody can take a payment anywhere in the country.

Not until Asha wakes up, sees the alert, drives 40 kilometres, works out which disk, finds a spare, and rebuilds. That is four hours if everything goes well. And the record of every charge was on that disk.

Hold on to all three. They are the same problem wearing three coats: capacity was a purchase, not a setting; and everything lived in one place that could stop.

3. Does renting the machine instead of buying it fix this?

In one breath: Stop owning hardware and rent it by the month. The cheque goes away, the drive to the data centre goes away, and the festival still gets lost.

The obvious fix is to stop owning hardware.

Companies exist that already have racks full of machines and will rent you one by the month. This is not a made-up option. It is a real product, sold today as dedicated server hosting and as VPS providers.

Asha signs up. Three things get better immediately.

There is no cheque up front. She pays ₹8,000 a month instead of ₹5,00,000 once.

There is no six-week wait for the first machine. The provider has stock, so a machine can be ready in a day or two.

And there is no driving. When a disk dies, the provider's technician is already in the building. They swap it.

This is genuinely better. For many small businesses it is enough. It fixes the cheque and it fixes the drive.

Before you read on, commit to a guess.

🔮 Predict: The October festival comes round again. Asha is renting instead of owning. What happens this time?

Reveal

She still loses it — just less badly. Renting changed who owns the metal and how you pay for it. It did not change the two things that actually hurt:

  1. Getting a second machine still takes a day or two of human process (an order, a provisioning queue, an OS install, a configuration). The spike is four days long and arrives without warning.
  2. And when the four days end, she is renting a second machine on a monthly contract for a spike that lasted a weekend.

If you guessed "she is fine now, she can just rent more", you have made the most common mistake in this whole subject: you treated cheaper as faster. They are different axes. Renting moved the price. It did not move the clock.

The unit of purchase is still a whole machine, for a whole month, decided by a human in advance. That is the thing that has to break.

4. What did renting not fix?

In one breath: Renting made the repair faster and the bill smaller. It left capacity as a purchase, it left one machine as one machine, and it hid the thing that actually kills Asha: she cannot find out what dies together.

Walk Asha's three pains again, this time against the rented machine.

Capacity is still a purchase. It is a smaller, more frequent purchase now, a month instead of three years. But it is still a decision a person makes ahead of time, in units of one whole machine, with a lead time counted in hours or days.

Marigold's traffic changes in minutes. You cannot track a minute-shaped demand with a day-shaped supply. So you do what everyone did: you buy for the peak and waste the rest. Asha's utilisation is still 6%.

One machine is still one machine. The provider replaced the dead disk faster than Asha could have. But between the disk dying and the technician finishing, Marigold Pay was down, and the record of charges was on that disk.

Renting improved the repair time. It did nothing about the existence of a single thing whose death stops everything.

Asha buys two machines. Why does that not save her?

Now the deeper problem, the one this whole lab is really about.

Ben is the newest engineer at Marigold, and he asks the obvious question: why not just get a second machine, so one can take over if the other dies?

Asha asks the hosting company for exactly that. They give her one. It is a good machine.

🔮 Predict: Asha now has two rented machines instead of one. Two months later, everything goes down anyway — both machines at once. Before you read on, say what could take out two separate computers at the same moment.

Reveal

They were in the same rack. Same power distribution unit, same top-of-rack switch. One power unit failed and both machines went with it.

The provider never told her. She never asked. And crucially, there was no way to ask, because the hosting company sells you machines, not a description of what shares a fuse with what.

If you guessed a network outage or a shared disk array, you are right in spirit and it does not change the lesson: the two things she bought had a shared parent she could not see.

She bought "two machines" believing she had bought two chances. She had bought one.

This is the discovery that reorganises everything that follows:

Redundancy is not a count of machines. It is a count of independent things that can fail. Two copies inside one failure boundary are one copy with extra steps.

And you cannot count independent failures if nobody will tell you where the boundaries are.

5. What does Asha actually need? Four sentences, and everyone skips the last

In one breath: Asha needs capacity she can ask for in a message. She needs a written statement of what dies together. She needs to hand over chores one at a time, and a clear list of what is still hers. The last one is where the accidents live.

Everything from here is the pursuit of four sentences. Asha needs a supplier where:

  1. Capacity is a runtime decision, not a purchasing decision. Ask for a machine with a single message and have it in a minute; give it back in a minute; pay only for the minutes held.

  2. The failure boundaries are published. Not "we have lots of data centres". She needs an actual, named, contractual statement of the form "these two things do not share power, cooling, or a network path, so they should not die together". Only then can "two copies" be made to mean two chances.

  3. The undifferentiated work can be handed over — deliberately, piece by piece. Replacing disks, patching operating systems, running replication, taking backups: each of these should be something Asha can choose to stop doing, at a stated price, with a stated guarantee. Not all-or-nothing.

  4. And she must still be able to tell what is left hers. Whatever the supplier does not do is still Asha's job. She needs to know exactly which jobs those are, because the ones you think someone else is doing are the ones nobody is doing.

Requirement 4 is the one everybody skips. Omar, who runs Marigold's quarterly disaster drill, is the only person on the team who reads it as a sentence about work rather than a sentence about vendors.

He is right to. It is section 11's biggest failure and section 12's sharpest edge. Keep it in view.

6. What are the three inventions underneath all of it?

In one breath: Take an enormous pool of hardware. Cut it into slices you can rent through a web request, by the second. Publish the map of which slices die together. Then put one guard in front of every single call.

Amazon Web Services is one answer to Asha's four sentences. Stripped to its core, it is three inventions stacked on each other:

Take an enormous pool of hardware. Cut it into slices you can rent through an HTTP API, by the second. Publish the map of which slices fail together. Then put one authorization check in front of every single call.

Three parts. It is worth naming them separately, because engineers reliably learn the first, ignore the second, and are eventually taught the third by an incident.

Invention one: what is the pool, and why does an API change everything?

Amazon owns far more hardware than any one customer needs. It cuts that hardware into rentable slices, and every action on a slice is an HTTPS request to a web service.

Create a machine. Delete a database. Read a file. Each of those is a request.

Nothing is a form you post, a person you email, or a wire you plug in. That is the whole of invention one, and it is what turns capacity into a runtime decision, which was Asha's requirement 1.

It has a side effect that matters later. If every action is a request, then every action can be written down as code. Infrastructure as code exists because of this, not the other way round. Section 13.2 and the whole of the terraform lab live in that side effect.

Invention two: what is the map, and why does nobody read it?

AWS publishes its physical failure boundaries as a hierarchy you design against, and commits contractually to their independence.

That is Asha's requirement 2. It is the half of AWS that most tutorials skip entirely. And it is the reason a design that "works" can still be a single point of failure, which is exactly what section 4 did to her.

Invention three: what is the badge, and what is its default answer?

Every one of those API calls is signed and evaluated against a policy before anything happens. The default answer is no.

This is what makes requirement 3 safe to buy. You can hand over the disks without handing over the keys.

It is also what makes requirement 4 knowable, because the badge doubles as the audit trail. Who did what, when, with which permission, is a question with an answer.

So what are the other 200 services?

Everything else in AWS is one of exactly two things.

Some of it is a slice of the pool: rent a machine, rent a disk, rent an IP address.

The rest is somebody else's distributed system with an API in front of it: rent a replicated object store, rent a partitioned queue, rent a Raft-backed coordination service.

That second category is why your fifty-five labs are the right preparation. You are not learning new mechanisms here. You are learning whose mechanism it is, where it runs, and what happens when it stops.

7. Which ten words carry the weight, and what did each one cost?

In one breath: Now the vocabulary, and only now. Each of these ten words was earned by something that already happened to Asha in sections 2 to 6. You cannot reason about an AWS failure without them.

Minor terms get one line at first use. These ten get the full treatment, because each one is a thing that will bite somebody in section 11.

7.1 What is a region, and why does your data not leave one?

Asha's problem was that her single data centre was a single building. Amazon's first structural answer is to have many buildings, in many cities, and to run each city as a separate business.

In plain words: a region is a city-sized place where Amazon has built data centres.

Precisely: it is an independent, geographically separate cluster of data centres with its own API endpoints, its own copy of most services, its own capacity pool, and its own price list. Regions are named like ap-south-1 (Mumbai), us-east-1 (Northern Virginia) and eu-west-1 (Ireland).

Why does the word exist at all? To name the largest boundary that is supposed to be independent.

Two regions are near-separate installations of AWS. They do not share a database. They do not automatically copy your data to each other. And one region can have a very bad day while every other region is entirely fine.

Watch that bite. Asha creates a file store in ap-south-1 for Marigold's receipts. From eu-west-1, that store does not exist in any local sense. A request for it is either redirected or refused. Any copy sitting in Europe is one she set up, and she pays to keep it in sync.

A region is a failure domain, and it is the largest one on the map. It is the whole point of section 4's discovery, drawn at the biggest scale AWS sells.

7.2 What is an Availability Zone, and why is it the answer to section 4?

Remember Ben's question and the two machines in one rack. Asha needed somebody to tell her, in writing, what shares a fuse with what.

In plain words: an Availability Zone, written AZ, is one independently powered wing of a region.

Precisely: an AZ is one or more discrete data centres inside a region, with redundant power, cooling and networking, physically separated from the other AZs in that region. Kilometres apart. Different flood plains. Different power substations.

They are connected to each other by a private, high-bandwidth, low-latency network. Round trips between zones are typically single-digit milliseconds, and often under one. A modern region has at least three of them. They are written ap-south-1a, ap-south-1b, ap-south-1c.

This is the answer to section 4. It is the contractual name for "these do not fail together".

When Asha places one machine in 1a and one in 1b, she has bought two chances. AWS has committed in writing that the rack, the power feed and the switch are not shared. That is exactly the sentence the hosting company could not give her.

Concretely: Marigold Pay runs three application servers, one in each of ap-south-1a, 1b and 1c. A transformer fire takes out 1b. One third of capacity vanishes and two thirds keep serving.

Compare the other arrangement. Three servers, all in 1a, same fire: zero capacity.

Here is the sharp detail most people never learn. The name ap-south-1a is scrambled per AWS account. Your ap-south-1a and Ben's ap-south-1a are probably different physical zones. AWS does this deliberately so that everyone does not pile into "a".

The stable, cross-account identifier is the AZ ID, which looks like aps1-az1. If you ever need to correlate your outage with a colleague's in another account, names lie and IDs do not.

An AZ is the unit you place replicas across. Everything in quorums-and-replication about where the copies go is answered here. The AZ is the "where".

7.3 What is the difference between changing your setup and using it?

You already own this distinction. You met it in distributed-rate-limiting, where the counter store was one thing and the limit configuration was another.

It is the same idea here. In AWS it is arguably the single most operationally important concept in the whole platform.

In plain words: there is a difference between changing your setup and using it.

The engineers' names for the two halves are the control plane and the data plane.

The control plane is the set of APIs that create, modify, describe and delete resources. Calls like RunInstances, CreateBucket and ModifyTargetGroup.

The data plane is the set of APIs and paths that use a resource that already exists. An HTTP request reaching your running machine. A read of an object that is already in the store. A query against a database that is already up.

Why does the distinction earn a name? Because they fail separately, and the control plane fails more often.

A control plane is complicated, stateful and globally coordinating. It must validate, allocate, schedule and reconcile. A data plane is comparatively simple, local and high-volume. AWS deliberately designs data planes to keep working from their last known configuration even when the control plane is unavailable.

🔮 Predict: A large event hits one of Marigold's zones. Priya is on call. Her running machines in the other two zones are healthy and the load balancer is routing to them happily. Her auto-scaling group tries to launch four replacement machines and gets an error every time. Which half is broken, and which half is fine?

Reveal

The data plane is fine. The control plane is degraded.

The running machines keep serving traffic perfectly. The load balancer keeps routing to them. Nothing about using what already exists has stopped.

But RunInstances starts returning InsufficientInstanceCapacity and other errors, so the auto-scaling group cannot launch replacements.

If you said "AWS is down", you have made the mistake this whole section exists to prevent. AWS was not down. The half of AWS that changes things was down, and Priya's recovery plan happened to need that half.

Here is the sentence to carry: a design that survives by already having spare capacity survives. A design that survives by asking for replacement capacity does not.

Hold on to that asymmetry. In section 10.6 it turns into a design principle with a name, and it is the most valuable AWS-specific idea in this lab. Failure F3 in section 11 is what it looks like at 6pm on a Friday.

7.4 What is a service quota, and why does it stop you when nothing is broken?

Arjun looks after Marigold's shared plumbing: accounts, permissions and the numbers nobody thinks about. He is the person who finds this one.

In plain words: a service quota, also called a limit, is a cap on how much of a thing your account may have.

Precisely: it is a numeric limit applied per account, per region, per resource type. Some are adjustable by request and some are hard.

Why does the cap exist? Because the pool is shared. Multi-tenancy needs a bound on what one tenant can grab, both to protect the other tenants and to protect you from a runaway loop. It is a rate limiter and a blast-radius control at the same time.

Here is Arjun's evening. At 6pm on festival day, Marigold's auto-scaling group tries to grow from 12 machines to 30. It stops at 20.

Why 20? Because the account's vCPU quota in ap-south-1 is 80, and the instance type Marigold uses takes 4 each.

Nothing is broken. Nothing has failed over. The system simply will not get bigger, and the error appears in an activity log nobody is watching.

These are the caps this lab names. Every one is a number with a meaning, so read the third column before the second:

The cap The number What that number means
Elastic IP addresses 5 per account per region how many reserved public addresses you may hold
Lambda concurrency 1,000 concurrent executions how many function copies may run at once in a region
vCPU quota, Marigold's account 80 in ap-south-1 the ceiling that stopped Arjun's group at 20 machines
vCPUs per instance, Marigold's type 4 why 80 vCPUs is 20 machines and not 80
API rate quotas X calls per second, per API a separate ceiling that bites during a mass launch

This is distributed-rate-limiting pointed at you, and that lab's "over-admission versus under-admission" framing applies exactly. It comes back as failure F4.

7.5 What does "managed" actually move, and what does it never move?

Asha's requirement 3 was that the chores could be handed over one at a time. AWS sells that, and the word for it is managed.

In plain words: you rent the outcome instead of the machine, and the job splits in two.

AWS states the split formally, and the formal name is the shared responsibility model. AWS is responsible for security of the cloud: the hardware, the physical facility, the virtualisation layer, and the managed service's own software and operations. The customer is responsible for security in the cloud: your data, who may access it, your operating system and patches where you have one, your network configuration, and your encryption choices.

The exact dividing line moves per service, and that movement is the whole point. Read this table as a slider, not as three products:

What Asha rents Who patches the machine Who patches the engine What is still hers
A raw machine Asha Asha everything above the metal
A managed relational database AWS AWS version choice, network, backup retention, who may connect
A serverless store or object store nobody, there is none nobody, there is none data, access, configuration

Now the trap, and it is the most expensive one in this lab.

Asha moves Marigold's database to the managed service and stops thinking about it. Eight months later Dev ships a migration that deletes a table.

AWS has been faithfully replicating that deletion to the standby. It has been faithfully snapshotting the deleted state.

"Managed" moved the disks and the engine patching to AWS. It never moved "is the data correct?" or "can we get back to yesterday?". And the retention window for automated backups has a default that is finite.

That is requirement 4 with money on it. The jobs you think someone else is doing are the ones nobody is doing. It comes back as failure F8.

7.6 Who is allowed to make this call, and what is the default answer?

The badge has a real name: IAM, Identity and Access Management. It is the authorization check in front of every API call, and it has four words worth learning.

A principal is whoever is making the call. A human user, an application, or another AWS service acting on your behalf.

A policy is a JSON document listing allowed or denied triples: an action, a resource, and a condition. For example: read one object, from arn:aws:s3:::marigold-receipts/*, allowed, only from inside our network. That second string names a specific resource, and section 7.10 unpacks its structure. The action itself is written s3:GetObject.

A role is a set of permissions with no permanent password or key. Instead it is assumed. A machine, a function or a container is attached to a role and automatically receives credentials that expire, typically in hours, and are rotated for it.

Temporary credentials are that expiring key set. This is the mechanism that makes it possible to run code in the cloud without a long-lived secret inside it.

Why do these four words exist? Because "who may do what" has to be answerable before every single API call, uniformly, for humans and machines alike.

And because the alternative is worse. A long-lived access key pasted into a config file is the single most common way AWS accounts get compromised. A leaked permanent key works forever, from anywhere. A leaked role credential expires on its own.

Watch it work for Marigold. The payment service runs on a machine with a role allowing exactly three things: write one charge record, read one charge record, and unlock one encryption key.

Written as the actions themselves, that role allows dynamodb:PutItem and dynamodb:GetItem on the charges table, plus kms:Decrypt with one key. Its code contains no credentials at all.

If someone gets shell on that instance, what they can reach is that table and that key. Not the whole account.

Meera reviews these policies before they ship, and the rule she applies is not intuitive, so learn it now.

The default is deny. An Allow somewhere flips it to yes. And an explicit Deny anywhere in any applicable policy flips it back to no, permanently. Deny always wins, and no allow can override it.

That is why a "deny everything not from our network" guardrail written once at the organisation level is safe to apply, even though a hundred teams write their own allow policies underneath it.

7.7 What is elasticity, and why is it not the same as scalability?

Asha's owned server could be made bigger. She could buy another one. In six weeks. Forever.

So it was scalable. It was not elastic, and the difference is the entire commercial argument for cloud.

In plain words: elasticity is capacity that goes up and comes back down, by itself, fast enough to track real demand.

Precisely: it is the ability to acquire and release capacity programmatically in minutes, with billing granularity (per-second or per-request) small enough that releasing it actually saves money.

Elasticity is scalability with a small time constant and a refund. Both halves matter. Scaling up in 90 seconds is useless if you still pay for a year. Cheap per-second billing is useless if provisioning takes a day.

Here is festival evening under elasticity, with the arithmetic that follows:

What happens The number What that number means
Marigold grows for the evening peak 12 → 30 instances in four minutes the time constant half of elasticity
Marigold shrinks again at midnight back to 12 the refund half; without it, nothing is saved
What the spike actually costs 18 machines × 6 hours the extra machines, held only while needed
What the same spike cost when rented 18 machines × 1 month the monthly contract from section 3
The ratio between those two roughly 120× less why anyone moved to cloud at all

And here is where elasticity stops being true, which is the shape of every architecture in section 10. Elasticity is a property of the stateless parts. You cannot elastically double a database that holds 4TB, because the data has to get there.

7.8 How far does one failure reach?

In a shared pool, "will it fail?" is a bad question. It will.

The useful question is "how far does the failure reach?", and engineers call the answer the blast radius.

In plain words: how much breaks when this one thing breaks.

Precisely: the set of resources, customers or data affected by a single fault, a single bad deployment, or a single compromised credential.

Once you have the phrase, a lot of AWS reorganises itself. Multiple zones, multiple regions, multiple accounts, narrow roles, per-service quotas: every one of those is a blast-radius decision, not a reliability decision.

Here is Marigold's version, and it is an ordinary Tuesday, not a disaster. Staging and production run in the same account. A load test in staging exhausts the account's shared vCPU quota in ap-south-1. Production's auto-scaling group then cannot grow during the evening peak.

Nothing failed. The blast radius of "staging" simply included production, because a quota is per-account.

Arjun's fix is section 13.1's multi-account structure. It is the single cheapest blast-radius control AWS offers, and it is organisational rather than technical.

7.9 What is your own private network inside somebody else's building?

"Rent a machine on the internet" would be unusable. Asha needs machines that can talk to each other and not to the world. She also needs the network boundary to be software she can version, rather than a cable somebody moved.

Three words do that job.

A VPC, or Virtual Private Cloud, is a private IP address range that is yours alone inside a region.

A subnet is a slice of that range, and it lives in exactly one AZ. This is the point where the network model and the failure map meet.

A security group is a stateful allow-list attached to a resource. In words: "TCP 443 from the load balancer, TCP 5432 to the database, nothing else."

Marigold's network, concretely. The VPC is 10.0.0.0/16. Three public subnets hold the load balancer: 10.0.1.0/24 in 1a, 10.0.2.0/24 in 1b, 10.0.3.0/24 in 1c. Three private subnets hold the application servers and the database.

Machines in those private subnets have no public IP and cannot be reached from the internet at all. The application's security group allows port 8080 only from the load balancer's own security group.

The detail that matters for this lab: a subnet is AZ-scoped. So "I have three subnets" is only three chances if they are in three different AZs.

That is section 4's mistake, reappearing in network clothing. It will reappear twice more before this lab is finished.

7.10 What does one API call actually look like on the wire?

Three small words make the mechanism in section 10 readable.

An endpoint is the hostname you send an API call to, and it is almost always regional. For example, dynamodb.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com.

Read that hostname again. The region is in the DNS name. There is no "AWS" to talk to. There is ap-south-1's key-value store, which is a different running system from eu-west-1's.

An ARN, or Amazon Resource Name, is the globally unique identifier of any resource. It is worth reading one slowly, because an ARN is the failure map written as a string:

arn:aws:dynamodb:ap-south-1:123456789012:table/charges

The piece What it says Where you met it
aws the partition, the ordinary commercial one 7.2, the outer box on the map
dynamodb which service, and each one is its own system 6, "somebody else's distributed system"
ap-south-1 the region, the largest failure domain 7.1
123456789012 the account, the billing and blast-radius boundary 7.8
table/charges the resource itself 7.6, the thing the policy names

Every policy Meera reviews is written against strings of that shape.

SigV4 is the signing scheme. Every request carries a signature computed over the request's method, path, headers, body hash and a timestamp, using the caller's secret key.

Two properties follow, and both matter. The key itself never travels. And a signature is valid for only a few minutes, so a captured request cannot be replayed tomorrow.

8. What two pictures should you keep in your head?

In one breath: One picture to carry around in conversation, and one to reason with when something is broken. The first is a serviced office building. The second is what is actually true underneath it.

You need both. The building gets you through a design discussion. The machine underneath gets you through an incident.

8.1 What does the serviced office building look like?

Imagine a company that owns enormous office buildings and rents you desks. Asha is a tenant. Here is her tenancy, in six sentences.

Each building is in a different city. Buildings are run separately, so a flood in the Mumbai building has nothing to do with the Dublin one. You must choose which building your team works in, and moving your filing cabinets to another city is a removal job you organise and pay for.

Each building has several wings, and each wing has its own generator, its own air conditioning and its own internet line. The landlord guarantees in writing that the wings do not share these. If you want to survive a generator failure, you put people in two wings.

You get desks by sending a message, not by signing a lease. "Four desks, please" — four desks are ready in a minute. "Done with them" — they are gone in a minute, and the meter stops.

Everything is on a meter. Desks by the second. The photocopier by the page. And here is the one nobody expects: carrying paper out of the building costs money per sheet, while carrying paper in is free.

You badge in for every single action. Not once at the door. Every time you open a drawer. Your badge is checked against a rulebook, and the default answer is no. Staff badges expire every few hours and renew themselves.

You can also rent serviced desks, where the landlord does the filing, the photocopying and the archiving. You still decide what goes in the files and who may read them.

That model is genuinely load-bearing. Building is region. Wing is availability zone. Sending a message is an API call. Badging in for every action is IAM. A serviced desk is a managed service. Paper leaving the building is an egress charge.

8.2 What is actually true, with the building stripped away?

Five statements. Each one is something you can check, and each one decides something later.

An account is a namespace and a billing boundary. Every resource belongs to exactly one account. It is also the default blast-radius boundary, because most quotas are per-account and IAM's reach stops at the account edge unless you deliberately extend it.

A resource lives at a definite point in the hierarchy partition → region → (usually) AZ. Some resources are region-scoped, and AWS spreads them across zones internally: an object store bucket, a serverless table, a managed queue. Some are AZ-scoped and it is your job to spread them: a machine, a network disk, a subnet, a single-zone managed database. Knowing which is which for every resource in your diagram is the job.

Every action is an authenticated, authorized, quota-checked HTTPS request to a regional endpoint of an independent multi-tenant service.

Each service is its own distributed system, with its own consistency model, its own durability claim, its own quotas, and its own control and data planes. There is no shared transaction, no shared clock and no shared lock. Two AWS services are as independent from each other as two vendors.

And only two things in your system cannot be recreated from code: your state and your identity configuration. Everything else (instances, load balancers, networks, DNS records) is a rebuildable consequence of a template.

Read that last one twice. It is why "infrastructure as code" is not tidiness. It is the recovery plan, and section 13.2 is where Omar makes Asha prove it.

8.4 Where does the shop story lie to you?

An analogy left unqualified is a bug with a delay fuse. Here is exactly where the building stops being true. Four places, and all four are expensive.

One: buildings do not have per-tenant quotas that bite at 3am.

If a real landlord has 500 free desks, you can have 500. In AWS your account has a numeric cap per region. It is unrelated to available capacity. It applies to things you are not currently using. And it is enforced by returning an error, not by a person saying "sorry".

So the failure mode is not "the building is full". It is "the building is empty and the door still will not open for you".

Quotas are also per-region. A limit increase Arjun obtained in ap-south-1 does nothing for the eu-west-1 disaster-recovery plan. That single sentence has ruined more than one failover test, and Omar has the drill notes to prove it.

Two: regions are not branches of one company.

In the building story the company is one company, and HR knows about you everywhere. In AWS, regions are much closer to separate installations.

Your data does not travel between them. Your quota does not travel. Even service availability does not travel: a service launched last year exists in us-east-1 and may not exist in ap-south-1 yet.

And a small number of things are genuinely global, with their control plane concentrated in us-east-1. IAM. Route 53's control plane. CloudFront. Organizations. Historically, the billing console.

That is a real, structural dependency. An event in Northern Virginia can affect your ability to change things in Mumbai, even though Mumbai's data planes are perfectly healthy. It is also the honest reason us-east-1 incidents are the ones that make the news.

Three: "serviced" does not mean "supervised".

A serviced office's staff would notice if you set fire to your own filing cabinet. AWS will not.

Deleting a table is a valid, authorized, instantly executed API call. It is literally one call, named DeleteTable, and the replication you are paying for will faithfully replicate the deletion.

Every safety net against your own correct-looking actions is something you switch on. Backups you have actually restored. Deletion protection. Versioning. A separate account holding the copies.

This is requirement 4 again, and it is where the analogy is most dangerously comforting.

Four: the meter has no idle discount, and the direction of travel is priced.

An empty desk costs nothing in a real office. In AWS, a running instance at 0% CPU costs full price. An unattached disk costs full price. An idle NAT Gateway costs by the hour. And an Elastic IP that is not attached to anything is billed precisely because it is idle.

Then there is direction. Omar found this one on a bill, which is how everybody finds it:

Out of AWS to the internet an order of ¢/GB every response to a customer has a price

Read the third row again, because it is the one that surprises people. The architecture that gives you section 4's independence is the one that spreads across zones, and it is also the one that pays for its own internal chatter.

A talkative service mesh spread across three zones can spend more on internal traffic than on compute. Nothing in the office picture prepares you for "talking to yourself costs money".

10. How does one call work, and how does a real design get forced into existence?

In one breath: Two mechanisms, different in kind. First, what physically happens inside one API call, because every single thing you do in AWS is that call. Second, how Marigold's real architecture gets built, version by version, by things breaking.

The first mechanism is small and you can trace it by hand. The second is a story with six versions, and Asha loses something in every one of them.

10.1 What are the seven steps hiding inside one line of code?

Marigold's Java service records a charge. In the AWS SDK that is one line, and that line hides seven distinct things that can each fail differently.

The charge records live in the store that has no zones to choose, no servers and no version to patch — the thing section 8.2 called a serverless table. Its name is DynamoDB, and writing one record into it is called PutItem.

Here is a hand-worked trace with concrete values.

Starting state. The code runs on a machine in ap-south-1b. The machine has an attached role called MarigoldChargeWriter. The table is arn:aws:dynamodb:ap-south-1:123456789012:table/charges. The item is {charge_id: "ch_8812", amount: 45000, status: "captured"}.

Read the trace as seven doors. The request has to get through every one, and each door has its own way of slamming.

Step 1  CREDENTIALS
        The SDK asks the instance's local metadata endpoint for credentials.
        It gets back a temporary triple that expires in ~6 hours:
          AccessKeyId    = ASIA...           (note: ASIA = temporary)
          SecretAccessKey= wJal...
          SessionToken   = IQoJb3JpZ2lu...
        No secret is stored in the application. This is §7.6's role, working.
        FAILS AS: credentials expired / no role attached -> the SDK throws
                  before a single byte reaches the network.

Step 2  ENDPOINT RESOLUTION
        Region ap-south-1 is turned into a hostname:
          dynamodb.ap-south-1.amazonaws.com
        DNS resolves it to an IP inside AWS's network.
        FAILS AS: this is where the networking lab's DNS lesson applies. A
                  5-second resolver stall here looks exactly like "DynamoDB is
                  slow" from the application's point of view.

Step 3  SIGNING (SigV4)
        A canonical form of the request is built - method, path, sorted
        headers, SHA-256 of the body - and signed with the secret key plus the
        date, region and service name. The result goes in a header:
          Authorization: AWS4-HMAC-SHA256 Credential=ASIA.../20260830/
            ap-south-1/dynamodb/aws4_request, Signature=8f42...
        The secret never travels. The signature is time-bound.
        FAILS AS: SignatureDoesNotMatch - and the classic cause is a machine
                  whose clock has drifted more than a few minutes.

Step 4  TRANSPORT
        TLS to the regional endpoint, HTTP POST, connection reused from a pool.
        Everything from the networking lab applies unchanged: connect timeout
        vs read timeout are different bounds, and a slow response is more
        dangerous than a refused one.

Step 5  AUTHORIZATION  (the badge, evaluated)
        The DynamoDB front end asks IAM: may principal
        arn:aws:sts::123456789012:assumed-role/MarigoldChargeWriter/i-0abc
        perform dynamodb:PutItem on .../table/charges ?
        Evaluation, in this order, with deny always winning:
          a. Organization SCP        -> allows dynamodb:*        (pass)
          b. Permission boundary     -> none attached            (pass)
          c. Identity policy on role -> Allow PutItem on charges (ALLOW)
          d. Resource policy         -> none for DynamoDB tables (n/a)
          e. Any explicit Deny?      -> none                     (pass)
        Result: ALLOW. Default was DENY; (c) is what flipped it.
        FAILS AS: AccessDeniedException. Note it fails HERE - after the network
                  round trip, before any data work. An IAM mistake is a
                  100%-of-calls failure, instantly, not a slow degradation.

Step 6  QUOTA / THROTTLE CHECK
        Does this table have write capacity right now, and does this partition?
        FAILS AS: ProvisionedThroughputExceededException. The SDK retries it
                  automatically with backoff, which is why this failure often
                  shows up as LATENCY rather than as errors. See F5.

Step 7  THE ACTUAL DISTRIBUTED SYSTEM
        Only now does anything resemble a database. The key "ch_8812" is
        hashed to a partition; the partition's replicas live in three AZs; the
        write is acknowledged once a quorum of them has it.
        This is your quorums-and-replication lab, running on someone else's
        machines, in three buildings you will never see.
        RETURNS: 200, and the charge is durable across an AZ failure.

Two things to take from that trace, and the first one will change how you read every incident for the rest of your career.

Five of the seven steps have nothing to do with the database. When somebody says "DynamoDB is broken", it is usually step 1, 3, 5 or 6: credentials, clock, policy, quota. That is the shape of almost every AWS incident an application team actually experiences.

Step 7 is the only step that is a distributed system, and you already know how it works. That is the reframing from the top of this lab, made concrete. What was new here was the shop, not the machinery.

10.2 Version 1 — why is one instance worse than the machine she rented?

Asha's first AWS deployment is a direct translation of the rented server. One machine, and everything on it.

She launches one instance of type t3.medium in ap-south-1a. The application and PostgreSQL both run on it. The data sits on the instance's local disk. It has a public IP.

It works. It costs about ₹2,000 a month. And it is, in AWS terms, strictly worse than what she had. It takes one paragraph to see why.

🔮 Predict: Asha stops the instance for ten minutes one evening to change its size, then starts it again. What happens to the payment records?

Reveal

If the data was on instance store, it is gone. Instance store is the physical disk attached to the host machine. Not corrupted: gone, with no error. Instance store is scratch space tied to the specific host your VM happens to be on, and stopping the instance releases the host.

If it was on an EBS volume, it survives the stop. An EBS volume is network-attached storage that lives in the AZ rather than on the host, and this is why it is the default for anything you care about.

If you guessed "nothing happens, it is a computer, stopping a computer does not erase it", you have made exactly the mistake that costs the data. On a rented physical machine you would have been right. Here, the disk was never hers; it belonged to a host she has now given back.

The general rule, and it is the most useful one-liner in AWS storage: instance store dies with the host; EBS dies with the AZ; S3 and DynamoDB die with the region. Three different boundaries, three different levels of "gone". Pick per piece of data, deliberately.

So: version 1, with the data on network-attached disk. Now the instance's underlying hardware fails at 3am, which happens.

The instance stops. Marigold is down until someone launches a new one and reattaches the volume.

We are back at section 2's 3am. It is cheaper and faster than driving 40 kilometres, and it is the same shape.

10.3 Version 2 — what does a load balancer fix, and what does it not?

The obvious fix is two application servers, with something in front to spread traffic and stop sending it to a dead one.

That something is a load balancer. Marigold uses the layer-7 kind, called an Application Load Balancer, or ALB. It gets a public DNS name.

The two machines are registered with it as targets. The ALB runs a health check: it requests /health every 30 seconds, and after two consecutive failures it stops sending that target any traffic.

The database now lives on its own instance, because two application servers cannot share one local disk.

Now the failure test. One instance dies. The ALB notices within about a minute and routes everything to the other one. Customers see a handful of errors during the detection window, then normal service.

This genuinely works. It is the first design in this lab that does.

🔮 Predict: All three boxes (both application servers and the database) were launched in ap-south-1a, because that was the default in the console and nobody changed it. A power event takes out ap-south-1a. How much of Marigold Pay survives?

Reveal

None of it. The ALB survives (it is region-scoped and AWS runs it in multiple zones) and it is now a perfectly healthy load balancer with zero healthy targets, returning 503 to every shop in the country.

This is section 4's discovery, exactly, on new hardware. Asha bought two instances believing she had bought two chances. Redundancy is a count of independent failure domains, and she had one.

If you guessed "two out of three boxes survive, so most of it works", you counted boxes rather than boundaries. That is the same arithmetic Ben used with the two rented machines, and it is wrong for the same reason.

The difference between this and the hosting company is that AWS would have told her — the AZ was a visible, selectable field on the launch screen, and she left it on the default.

Watch this as scenario 2 in the simulator. The instructive detail is the ALB's own state: it is up, it is healthy, its dashboard is green, and it serves nothing.

10.4 Version 3 — how does a system replace its own dead parts?

Two changes, and they are the two halves of every AWS reliability story.

Placement. Application servers go one per zone: 1a, 1b, 1c. Subnets are created per zone, because a subnet is AZ-scoped and three subnets in one AZ is still one chance. That was section 7.9. The ALB is given all three subnets.

Replacement. The instances stop being pets. Asha creates an Auto Scaling Group, or ASG, which is a supervisor that keeps a stated number of healthy machines alive.

She tells it: desired 3, minimum 3, maximum 12, spread evenly across these three subnets, and here is the health check.

Now, when an instance dies, the group launches a replacement automatically, in the same zone, from the same image. The same mechanism handles the festival: a scaling policy says if average CPU across the group exceeds 60%, add instances.

This is the standard shape. It is the picture on the front of every AWS tutorial.

Watch the AZ failure again. 1b dies. One third of capacity vanishes. The ALB stops routing there within a minute. The group launches a replacement in 1a or 1c, and Marigold serves the whole festival with a brief dip.

Except for one box in the diagram.

🔮 Predict: In version 3 the database is still a single PostgreSQL instance, sitting in ap-south-1a, because moving a database felt like a separate project. 1a fails. The application servers in 1b and 1c are healthy and the ALB is happily routing to them. What do shops see?

Reveal

Every payment fails, and here is the cruel part: they fail slowly. The application servers are up, so they accept the request, try to reach the database, and block until their connection times out. A shop that would rather have had a fast "declined" instead waits 30 seconds for a 500.

This is the microservices lab's central result on AWS hardware: a slow dependency destroys a system harder than a dead one, because the healthy tier converts its own health into a queue of stuck threads. All three application servers become useless within seconds of the database going away, regardless of which zone they are in.

If you guessed "two thirds of payments succeed", you assumed the application servers were independent of each other. They are. They are not independent of the one thing they all need.

The lesson that generalises past this example: elasticity is a property of the stateless tier, and it buys you nothing while state has a single home. Auto-scaling three application servers across three zones is decoration if the thing they all need is in one.

10.5 Version 4 — what are the three prices AWS charges for keeping state safe?

State is the hard half, and AWS offers it at three prices. All three are the same trade you have already studied. Only the packaging is new.

(a) RDS Multi-AZ. The managed relational database service is called RDS, and its two-zone arrangement is called Multi-AZ.

One primary database instance sits in 1a, one standby in 1b. Every write is replicated synchronously to the standby before being acknowledged. The standby serves no traffic; it exists purely to take over.

On failure, AWS detects it and flips a DNS name from the primary's address to the standby's. AWS documents this as typically 60–120 seconds for the classic Multi-AZ instance deployment, and faster for the newer Multi-AZ DB cluster form. You change nothing in the application, because it always connects to the same endpoint name.

In cap-theorem terms this is a CP choice. In pacelc terms it is PC/EC. Synchronous replication means the write latency includes a cross-AZ round trip, which is single-digit milliseconds, and that is the price of consistency in the no-partition case — exactly PACELC's "else" branch. During failover the database refuses service rather than serving two primaries.

(b) Aurora. Same API, same engines, with the storage layer rebuilt as a distributed system. AWS calls it Aurora.

The data is held as six copies across three AZs, and the engine writes to that storage layer rather than to a disk. AWS describes the write path as requiring acknowledgement from four of six copies, and reads as satisfiable from three of six.

That is a straight quorum. In your own notation, W=4, R=3, N=6, so W + R > N holds with a margin chosen so the system survives losing an entire AZ plus one more copy and still writes.

Read replicas share that storage instead of copying data, so adding one is fast and adds little write cost.

If that felt familiar, it should. It is quorums-and-replication with the numbers picked so the failure domain, not the individual disk, is the unit being tolerated.

(c) DynamoDB, S3 and SQS. Skip the database-shaped thing entirely. Use a service that is already multi-AZ by construction, with no instances, no failover event and no version to patch.

You do not choose zones for a serverless table. There is nowhere on the screen to make section 4's mistake. The price is that you accept its data model and its consistency menu rather than SQL's.

A useful way to hold the three: (a) moves the failover to AWS, (b) moves the storage layer to AWS, (c) moves the entire idea of a server to AWS. Each step removes a class of mistake and removes a control.

Version 4 is Marigold with an auto-scaling group across three zones and RDS Multi-AZ. It survives an instance failure and an AZ failure. It is a good design.

Now the night it does not work.

10.6 Version 4 breaks — what happens the night the control plane freezes?

This is the section to read twice.

The scenario. A large event affects ap-south-1b. Marigold's design says: lose a third of capacity, the group launches replacements, carry on. Traffic that evening is at festival level, so the group is at 12 instances, four per zone. Priya is on call.

Here is what actually happens, step by step, with concrete numbers. Read the left column as a clock and the right as what Priya can see:

18:04:00  AZ 1b degrades. 4 of 12 instances stop responding.
18:04:30  ALB health checks fail for those 4. It stops routing to them.
          Capacity: 8 of 12. Latency rises; some requests shed.
18:05:00  ASG notices 4 unhealthy, terminates them, calls RunInstances
          for 4 replacements.
18:05:02  RunInstances returns InsufficientInstanceCapacity.
          Reason: every other customer in ap-south-1 whose design also
          depended on launching replacements is asking for the same
          instance type in the same two surviving zones, at the same second.
18:05:02  ASG backs off and retries. And retries.
18:11:00  Still 8 instances. Festival traffic needs 12. Marigold is now
          shedding roughly a third of payments, and has been for 7 minutes.
19:40:00  Capacity gradually becomes available. The group refills.

Nothing in Marigold's design was wrong by the diagram. The application tier was spread across three zones. The database failed over correctly in 90 seconds.

And the system still spent an hour and a half degraded. Why?

Because the recovery plan depended on making a control plane call during the exact minute that control plane was under region-wide stress.

🔮 Predict: What change to the design would have made this a non-event — without adding a fourth zone, a second region, or any new AWS service?

Reveal

Run 18 instances instead of 12, six per zone, and never let the design need a launch in order to recover.

With N = 3 zones and enough capacity in any two of them to serve 100% of peak, losing one zone means going from 150% of needed capacity to 100%. The group's launch call still fails. It does not matter, because nothing was waiting on it. Recovery consisted entirely of the load balancer stopping doing something, namely removing unhealthy targets. That is a data-plane action, and it needs nobody's permission.

If you guessed "use a smaller instance type so capacity is easier to find", you are optimising the odds of a control-plane call succeeding. That is a better bet, not a removed dependency, and on the worst night the odds are exactly when they fail.

This is static stability: designing so that the response to a failure is to keep doing what you were already doing with what you already have, never to acquire something new. AWS names it explicitly in its own Builders' Library, and it is the principle behind why their data planes keep serving from last-known configuration when control planes are unavailable.

The bill is real and you should say it out loud: you are paying for 18 machines to serve 12 machines' worth of traffic — 50% overprovisioning, which is the price of not depending on the control plane. That is the actual trade, and it is a business decision, not an engineering one.

Generalise it into a checklist, because this is the highest-value thing in the lab. For each recovery path in your design, ask one question: does getting better require a successful control-plane call?

Recovery mechanism Needs control plane? Statically stable?
ALB removes an unhealthy target No — data plane ✅
Traffic shifts to already-running instances in other AZs No ✅
ASG launches a replacement instance Yes (RunInstances) ❌
RDS Multi-AZ failover to an existing standby Mostly no — the standby already exists ✅ mostly
Creating a new RDS instance from a snapshot Yes, and slowly ❌
Route 53 health-check failover between existing endpoints No — the health-check data plane ✅
Changing a Route 53 record by hand during an incident Yes (and its control plane is in us-east-1) ❌

10.7 Version 5 — why is a second region a different kind of problem?

A region can have a bad day. If Marigold must survive that, it needs a second one.

This is where AWS stops being able to hide anything from you, because copying state between regions is not a feature you buy. It is a CAP position you take.

Three honest options, and you have already studied all three.

Warm standby with asynchronous replication. Aurora Global Database replicates to a second region with typical lag AWS describes as under a second. Failover is a promotion you trigger.

The number this design is really buying is RPO, the recovery point objective, which is how much data you accept losing. Async replication with one second of lag means an instant region loss can lose up to about a second of committed writes.

For most businesses that is fine. For Marigold, one second of payments is a reconciliation problem with real money in it, and someone in finance needs to agree to that number in advance. Omar is the one who has to get that agreement, in writing, before the drill.

Active-active with DynamoDB Global Tables. Both regions accept writes. AWS replicates both ways. Conflicts resolve last-writer-wins.

That is a deliberate AP choice. It is the eventual-consistency and crdts discussion arriving with a price tag: last-writer-wins silently discards one of two concurrent updates.

Marigold's charges table is the one place where silently discarding a write is unacceptable. Global Tables are excellent for sessions, profiles, feature flags and carts. Think very hard before one holds money.

Synchronous across regions. This is available in effect via google-spanner-style systems, and in the AWS world via things built on similar principles.

The reason almost nobody does it for an interactive path is in your PACELC lab. Cross-region round trips are tens to hundreds of milliseconds, and they are added to every write, in the normal unpartitioned case.

Mumbai to Ireland is roughly 120ms one way. Two round trips of consensus and your payment API has a floor of half a second before it does any work.

There is no fourth option. The reason multi-region is hard is not AWS. It is the speed of light plus the CAP theorem, and AWS's contribution is only that it makes the choice explicit and bills you for it.

Say this before someone spends a year on it. Most businesses should be multi-AZ, statically stable, with tested backups restorable into a second region — and should accept several hours of downtime in a genuine region loss.

Multi-region active-active roughly doubles the infrastructure bill. It more than doubles the operational complexity. It introduces data-consistency failure modes that do not exist in a single region. And its own machinery becomes a new source of outages.

Buy it when the cost of an hour down exceeds all of that, and not before.

10.8 Version 6 — which three things actually take companies down?

Versions 1 to 5 are the infrastructure story. In practice, the incidents that hurt most are not zone failures.

They are these three, and none of them appears on an architecture diagram.

A permission that was wider than it needed to be. A leaked long-lived access key, or a role with *, turns one compromised laptop into an account-wide event. The whole of section 7.6 exists for this. Blast radius is counted in IAM policies, and this is the cheapest thing on the list to fix. Meera's review is the control.

A bill nobody bounded. A misconfigured job, a recursive function, a forgotten cluster left running in a region nobody looks at. The meter never sleeps and AWS will not stop you. Omar finds this one, late.

A backup nobody had ever restored. Multi-AZ replicates your mistakes at the speed of light. This is failure F8, and Dev is the one who triggers it without doing anything wrong.

11. What does it look like when it goes wrong?

In one breath: Eleven failures, in the order a working engineer actually meets them, which is not the order tutorials use. For each one: how to cause it, what Marigold sees, why it happens, what survives, and what a real team does about it.

Every one of these happens to somebody on Asha's team. Priya is on call for most of them. Read them as eleven short nights rather than eleven entries.

F1 — What happens when a single instance dies?

Priya's pager goes at lunchtime, which is the good version of this job.

To cause it yourself: open the simulator, pick any version, and press Kill instance. In reality the causes are dull. Host hardware fails. The operating system kills the process because it ran out of memory. A deploy ships a broken build.

The instance stops answering. The ALB's health check fails twice, on defaults near enough to a 30s interval and an unhealthy threshold of 2. So it is removed from rotation about 60 seconds later. From version 3 onward the auto-scaling group terminates it and launches a replacement, which takes 2–5 minutes to boot, configure and pass its first health checks.

Nothing exotic caused this. Hardware fails. The interesting part is the detection window: for that 60 seconds the load balancer is still sending a share of traffic to a dead target.

Durability survives completely. Anything already written to network-attached disk or a managed store is untouched.

What Priya loses is a slice of requests during detection, plus any request that was in flight on that instance. The in-flight one is what people forget. A payment that was mid-flight has an unknown outcome, which is precisely why idempotency keys are not optional on a write path. The client retries; the server must not double-charge.

In production, health check tuning is a real trade. Aggressive checks detect faster and also eject healthy instances during a garbage-collection pause. Connection draining, also called the deregistration delay, lets in-flight requests finish on a planned removal. And run at least 3 instances, so losing one is a third rather than a half.

F2 — What happens when an entire Availability Zone fails?

In the simulator, press Kill AZ and pick which one. Try 1a when the database is in 1a, then try 1c. The difference between those two runs is the whole lesson.

Every AZ-scoped resource in that zone becomes unreachable at once. Instances, their network-attached disks, the subnet, the NAT gateway in it, and an RDS primary if it lived there.

Region-scoped services keep working. The object store, the serverless table, the queue and the ALB itself are run across zones by AWS for you.

The cause is shared power, cooling or network within the zone, which is exactly the boundary the zone was defined to contain.

The response is layered. The ALB stops routing to the dead zone. RDS Multi-AZ fails over to the standby, typically 60–120 seconds. The auto-scaling group tries to rebalance. Which is where F3 usually joins in.

If Asha is spread across three zones with enough capacity in two, availability survives. Durability of anything in a region-scoped service is untouched.

What is lost is a third of capacity instantly, plus the RDS failover window in which writes fail, plus every open database connection in every application server. Connection pools hold dead sockets and must be evicted before the application recovers, which is the networking-for-developers result appearing again.

In production: three zones, not two, so losing one leaves a majority for anything quorum-based. Never put the only copy of anything AZ-scoped anywhere. And test it — AWS Fault Injection Service can simulate zone loss on purpose, which is how Omar's drill stops being a document.

F3 — What happens when the control plane is unavailable and the data plane is fine?

In the simulator, press Freeze control plane, once with static stability off and once with it on.

Everything currently running keeps running and keeps serving. Meanwhile RunInstances, CreateTable, UpdateService, console operations and API describes become slow, throttled or failing. Auto Scaling cannot replace anything. Deployments cannot proceed.

This is the shape of several of the largest publicly documented AWS events. One is the November 2020 us-east-1 Kinesis event, whose effects reached other services that depended on it. Another is the December 2021 us-east-1 internal network event, where customer data planes were substantially healthier than the control planes and the console.

The cause is structural, not accidental. A control plane does hard, stateful, coordinating work, and it is correlated across the whole region. A data plane is a simple local path designed to keep serving from last-known configuration.

The response here is Asha's, not AWS's. A statically stable system does nothing and is fine. A just-in-time system waits.

The data plane survives: existing capacity keeps serving, and existing data stays readable and writable. What is lost is every form of change — scaling, replacing, deploying, and failing over anything that needs a create call.

In production, apply the section 10.6 checklist. Keep a runbook that assumes the console is unusable. Avoid making the incident response itself depend on control-plane calls. And remember that during a big event you are competing with every other customer for the same capacity.

F4 — What happens when a service quota is reached?

This is Arjun's failure, and it is the quietest one in the list.

In the simulator, press Hit the quota, then raise traffic to festival level.

Scaling stops at a number that has nothing to do with load or available hardware. Nothing crashes. The evidence is a line in the auto-scaling group's activity history and an API error code, and neither is on anybody's dashboard by default.

The cause is the per-account, per-region caps from section 7.4. The classic triggers are three. Never having asked for an increase in a region you only use for disaster recovery. A shared account where a load test consumed the pool. And hitting an API rate quota rather than a resource-count quota during a mass launch.

Correctness survives entirely; everything running is right. What is lost is elasticity, silently. Asha has a scalable design that will not scale.

In production, enumerate the quotas your recovery path needs, in every region you would fail over to, and raise them before you need them. An increase is a support process with a human in it, so it is not something you do at 18:05. Alarm on utilisation at around 70%, not at 100%, using the usage metrics Service Quotas publishes. And this is one of the strongest arguments for separate accounts per environment.

F5 — What happens when one key gets all the traffic?

In the simulator, run festival traffic with a single merchant dominating. In reality: a serverless table partitioned by something with skew.

The table has plenty of unused capacity overall, and yet a subset of requests get ProvisionedThroughputExceededException. Because the SDK retries with backoff automatically, the first symptom is usually p99 latency, not errors, which sends people looking at the network.

This is your consistent-hashing and partitioning-and-rebalancing labs, exactly. DynamoDB hashes the partition key and splits data into partitions with per-partition throughput ceilings, historically documented around 3,000 read units and 1,000 write units each.

A key that is hot sends all of its traffic to one partition. One giant merchant will do it. So will a partition key like date, where everything today shares one value.

DynamoDB's adaptive capacity absorbs a lot of this now. But it cannot make one key be in two places, because the whole point of a placement function is that a key has one owner.

Correctness and durability survive, fully. What is lost is the throughput you thought you had bought, on one key.

In production, fix the key, not the capacity. Write-sharding a hot key into merchant#42#0..9 is the sub-key splitting from your distributed-rate-limiting lab, the same remedy in a new place. Watch ThrottledRequests per table. And if the access pattern is genuinely "one entity is 40% of traffic", a cache in front is the right answer — DynamoDB's own, called DAX, and redis both work.

F6 — What happens when a whole region has an event, and what is the us-east-1 twist?

In the simulator, choose architecture v5 and trigger Region event.

Regional endpoints degrade broadly. If Marigold is single-region, it is down for the duration. There is nothing clever available at that point, because Asha's options were all exercised months earlier.

Now the twist worth knowing, and it is the reason this failure has a second half.

A handful of AWS things are global with their control plane concentrated in us-east-1: IAM, Organizations, Route 53's control plane, CloudFront, and historically parts of billing.

So a us-east-1 event can prevent Meera from changing an IAM policy, or Priya from editing a DNS record, even while the Mumbai workload is entirely healthy. This is why us-east-1 incidents get disproportionate coverage, and it is a genuine structural dependency rather than a myth.

Other regions' data planes survive, along with any data Asha had already replicated elsewhere. What is lost is the region, and possibly her ability to reconfigure anything global.

In production, decide your RPO and RTO as numbers, with the business, in advance. Test the restore, because a backup you have never restored is a hypothesis. Keep at least the ability to serve a static apology page from elsewhere.

And note the split inside Route 53 itself. Health-check-based failover between existing endpoints is a data-plane behaviour and keeps working. Editing records by hand is the control-plane action that may not.

F7 — What does an IAM mistake look like, in both directions?

In the simulator, trigger IAM deny and watch where in the request path it fails.

This failure has two opposite flavours, and Meera cares much more about the second.

Too narrow. A deploy ships a role missing one action. 100% of writes fail instantly with AccessDeniedException. That is a total, immediate, unambiguous outage — which is the good kind, because somebody finds it in ninety seconds.

Too wide. Nothing happens at all, for months. Then a key leaks, or a bug in one service reaches data belonging to another. That is the bad kind.

Why the asymmetry? Deny-by-default plus explicit-deny-wins makes the narrow failure loud. Nothing makes the wide failure loud, because "worked fine" is what excess permission looks like every single day.

In the narrow case everything survives except availability of that one path, and no data is at risk. In the wide case what is lost is potentially the containment property that the rest of the design depends on.

In production: no long-lived access keys in applications, ever — roles and temporary credentials, which is section 7.6. Scope roles to specific actions on specific ARNs. Use organisation-level guardrails, called SCPs, as an explicit deny that no team can override. For example, "no principal in this account may disable logging", or "only these regions may be used". Turn on access analysis to find over-broad grants. And treat a public object-store bucket as a paged incident.

F8 — What happens when a deletion replicates perfectly?

This one cannot be simulated meaningfully. It is a one-line story, and it is Dev's.

A migration drops a column with data in it. A cleanup job's WHERE clause is wrong. Someone deletes the "unused" bucket.

The action is authorized, valid, and executed immediately. Multi-AZ replication copies it to the standby within milliseconds. Aurora's six copies all agree. The cross-region replica receives it in under a second.

Every reliability mechanism Asha bought works perfectly, and works against her, because none of them can distinguish her mistake from her intent.

Replication protects against hardware and infrastructure failure. It is not, and has never been, a backup. That distinction is requirement 4 with money on it.

Availability survives, ironically. The system is perfectly up. What is lost is the data, and possibly the business.

In production, this is the longest list in the lab, and every item is a separate switch somebody has to throw. Point-in-time recovery, with a retention window someone has actually chosen, because defaults are finite and often shorter than the time it takes to notice. Versioning plus MFA-delete on anything irreplaceable. Deletion protection on production databases. Backups in a separate account whose credentials production does not hold, so a compromise of production cannot reach them.

And a restore rehearsal on a calendar. The number Omar cares about is not "do backups exist". It is "how long does a restore take, and does anyone here know how".

F9 — What happens when nobody bounds the bill?

To cause it: leave a p4d GPU instance running in a region you do not check. Or write a recursive function — one that writes to a bucket whose events trigger the same function.

Nothing breaks. The meter runs. The failure surfaces days later as a number, on Omar's desk.

Elasticity has no upper bound by default, and AWS's default posture is to let you have what you ask for. There is no spend limit that hard-stops resources.

Everything technical survives. What is lost is money, and sometimes the project.

In production, set budgets with alerts, and understand what they do: they alert, they do not stop. Make cost-allocation tags mandatory, so a spike is attributable to a team in minutes rather than a week.

Watch the three line items that surprise people most. Cross-AZ data transfer, which is the price of section 7.2's independence. NAT Gateway, billed hourly and per GB. And internet egress.

Keep non-production accounts behind hard guardrails. And know the pricing models — on-demand, Savings Plans and Reserved for a committed baseline, and Spot for interruptible work at a large discount. Choosing them well is usually a bigger lever than any code change.

F10 — Why is a slow dependency worse than a dead one?

In the simulator, throttle the database rather than killing it.

Application threads block. The pool fills. Requests queue.

Now the part that turns a bad hour into an outage. Health checks usually hit a trivial /health that does not touch the database, so they keep passing. The ALB keeps sending traffic to instances that cannot do anything with it.

Then the auto-scaling group, seeing high CPU or high request count, adds more instances. Those instances open more connections to the struggling dependency and make it worse.

This is precisely the microservices result. A slow dependency is worse than a dead one, because a dead one fails fast and frees the caller. Auto-scaling turns it into a positive feedback loop.

Correctness survives. What is lost is availability, with every dashboard green.

In production: timeouts on every call, always shorter going inward, which is the timeout budget from your networking lab. Circuit breakers. Bulkheads, so one dependency's queue cannot consume the whole thread pool. And health checks that are shallow by design — a deep health check that fails when the database is slow will eject the entire fleet and turn a degradation into an outage.

F11 — What happens when failover works and the clients do not notice?

Cause it with an RDS Multi-AZ failover, or any DNS-based failover. Priya has seen this one more than once, and it always looks like AWS lying.

AWS flips the DNS record to the standby in 60–120 seconds. Marigold's application keeps failing for much longer.

Three reasons stack up. The JVM or the connection pool cached the old IP. The pool holds established TCP connections to an address that no longer answers. And isConnected() cheerfully returns true until something tries to write.

DNS-based failover updates a name, and your process resolved that name a long time ago. This is the connection-pool lesson from networking-for-developers with a specific and very common trigger.

The database survives and is healthy and available. What is lost is your application's access to it, for minutes after the platform considers the incident over.

In production, bound DNS caching. In the JVM that is networkaddress.cache.ttl, and historically the default under a security manager was to cache forever, which is exactly wrong here. Use a pool that validates connections and evicts on failure. And test failover as a routine exercise rather than as a theory. RDS lets you trigger one on demand, and the first time you do it you will learn something about your application that no diagram would have told you.

12. What does AWS actually guarantee?

In one breath: Read this section as a contract, because that is what it is. Everything in it was earned by something that happened to Marigold in sections 10 and 11.

Four things are worth separating: what the numbers mean, what the independence claim covers, what each service promises about reads and writes, and what Asha gave up to get any of it.

12.1 Why are durability and availability different numbers?

S3 is described by AWS as designed for 99.999999999% durability of objects over a year. Eleven nines.

Read what that sentence is about. It is a design target for not losing data, achieved by storing redundantly across multiple AZs. It is not a service level agreement, and it is not about whether you can reach your data.

S3's availability is a separate, much smaller number, and that one does have an actual SLA attached: 99.9% monthly for S3 Standard, with service credits below that. EC2's SLA is 99.99% for a region-level deployment across multiple AZs, and a lower figure for a single instance.

🔮 Predict: Omar's request path touches a load balancer, compute, a database and a queue. Each is independently 99.95% available. He tells the board the path is "about 99.95%, call it four hours a year". Before reading on, say whether he is right, and if not, which direction the error goes.

Reveal

He is wrong, and the error goes the expensive way. Availability composes downward, multiplicatively. The path is 0.9995⁴ ≈ 99.80% — about 17 hours a year, from four components that each promised under 5.

If you guessed "it averages out to roughly the same number", you assumed the components take turns failing. They do not. Every one of them has to be up for the request to work, so the probabilities multiply rather than average.

This is the microservices arithmetic, and it is why the answer is never "add more nines to each component". It is "remove the component from the critical path" — cache it, queue it, make it optional, or degrade without it.

Three things follow from those numbers, and each of them catches people.

An SLA is a refund, not a guarantee. Breaching it costs AWS a percentage of your bill for that service. It does not cost them your customers. Never design as though the SLA is a floor on your outcomes; it is a bound on your compensation.

Availability composes downward, multiplicatively, which is the arithmetic in the reveal above.

The multi-AZ number is only yours if you are actually multi-AZ. EC2's 99.99% describes a design across zones. Version 2 of Marigold did not get it, and no amount of reading the SLA would have revealed that.

12.2 What exactly does the independence claim cover?

AWS's structural guarantee is one sentence, and it is worth being able to quote: AZs in a region are physically separated and do not share power, cooling, or a single network path, and are connected by private, low-latency, high-bandwidth links.

That is a claim about infrastructure, and it has held up well.

It is not a claim about three other things, and the gap is where designs fail.

It does not claim that a service's control plane is per-AZ. It usually is not. It is regional, which is F3.

It does not claim that your own dependencies are spread across zones. That is your job, which was section 10.3.

And it does not claim that a bug in a service's software is contained by a zone. Software deploys are the correlated failure that physical separation cannot address, which is why AWS itself deploys in stages across zones and regions.

Read that last one twice. Physical independence is a strong protection against physical correlation. The dominant correlated failure in modern systems is a deployment, and a zone boundary does not stop one.

12.3 What does a successful write mean, service by service?

There is no platform-wide consistency model. Each service is its own system.

Here are the ones you will actually meet, in the vocabulary you already have. Read the third column first in both tables; it is where the money is.

The stores — the things that hold Marigold's state:

The rule to carry away: when you adopt an AWS service, the first question is not what it costs. It is what does a successful write mean here, and what can a subsequent read see?

Every one of your fifty-five labs was preparation for asking that question well.

12.4 What was traded away for all of this?

An honest ledger. Five things were traded away, and Asha does not get any of them back.

Placement control. She chooses a region, a zone and an instance type. She does not choose the rack, the neighbours, or the host. Noisy-neighbour effects and variance are real, and the answer is usually "use a larger instance type or a dedicated one", which means pay more.

Inspectability. When a managed service is slow, she gets the metrics AWS chose to publish and a support case. She cannot attach a profiler to it.

Price coupling. Compute is competitive. Data movement is where the economics bite, and it is deliberately asymmetric: in is free, out and sideways are not. That asymmetry is also the main mechanism of lock-in, and it is more effective than any API incompatibility.

A lock-in gradient, chosen per component. Roughly: EC2 and containers are near-portable. RDS-on-PostgreSQL is portable with effort. DynamoDB, Lambda's execution model, IAM's policy model and the event glue between services are not.

That last one is a legitimate trade, because managed services are genuinely cheaper to operate. Make it consciously, per component, and know which side of the line each of your components is on.

The ability to be sure. Asha is running on someone else's distributed systems. She will have incidents whose root cause is a paragraph in a post-event summary published four days later, and there is nothing she can do about it except have been statically stable at the time.

13.2 Why does click-ops fail, stated properly?

Creating things by hand in the console is fine for learning and fatal in production. The reason is more specific than "it is not repeatable".

Recall section 8.2: only state and identity cannot be recreated.

Your infrastructure code is your disaster recovery plan. If everything but state and identity is a template, a region loss is "apply the template elsewhere and restore the data". If it is 400 console clicks that one person half-remembers, a region loss is a rewrite under pressure.

The value of infrastructure as code is not tidiness. It converts "rebuild our company's infrastructure" from an unbounded task into a bounded one.

The secondary benefits are real too. A diff to review before it happens. Drift detection. And the fact that "who changed this?" has an answer.

That paragraph is an assertion, and this lab does not defend it. The terraform lab is the mechanism behind it, and its own section 13.2 comes back and qualifies the claim properly. Read it next; it is about managing exactly the resources this lab taught you to rent.

13.3 How much of the machine do you want to think about?

That is the genuine decision. Not "which compute service", but how much of the machine you want in your head.

Asha has four answers, and they are four rungs of a ladder.

You get a whole virtual machine. You own the operating system, the patching, the image and the scaling group. The name is EC2, and section 8.3 called it the rented desk.

It is right when you have specific OS, kernel or licensing needs, long-running stateful processes, or an existing estate to lift.

You get a scheduler instead of machines. You hand it containers and it decides where they run. There are two: EKS is Kubernetes with a managed control plane, and ECS is AWS's simpler, non-portable equivalent.

Either is right when you have many services and want one deployment model. Kubernetes is a large operational commitment. A team of five running eight services usually does not need it, and saying so out loud is more useful than the diagram it replaces.

You stop owning the machines under the scheduler. That option is called Fargate, and it costs more per unit and gives up some control.

You get a function invocation and nothing else. No servers, no scaling configuration, pay per request-millisecond, scales to zero. The name is Lambda.

It is right for event-driven work, spiky and low-average traffic, and glue. Its constraints are hard numbers, and the last one is the one that hurts a Java team:

The constraint The number What it means for you
Execution time limit 15 minutes anything longer must be split or moved to a container
Memory ceiling a fixed maximum per function large in-memory work does not belong here
Concurrency quota per region, per account the F4 failure, wearing a serverless hat
Cold start seconds for a Spring Boot app the real one; fix it with SnapStart, a smaller framework, or provisioned concurrency

The honest default for a Java or Spring team: containers on ECS Fargate or EKS for the always-on request path, and Lambda for the event glue and scheduled jobs. EC2 when something forces it.

And a green dashboard with unhappy customers means your health check is shallower than your actual dependency graph. That is F10, and it is the most common way an incident goes unnoticed for twenty minutes.

13.6 What does this look like in Java and Spring?

Since this is the day job, three concrete things. These are illustrative shapes to read, not files to run.

Credentials should be invisible. AWS SDK v2's DefaultCredentialsProvider walks a chain: environment, system properties, web identity token, profile file, then the container or instance metadata endpoint.

On ECS or EC2 with a role attached, that last step supplies temporary credentials automatically. So the client is built with no secret in sight:

// No keys anywhere. On ECS/EC2 the role supplies temporary credentials.
DynamoDbClient ddb = DynamoDbClient.builder()
    .region(Region.AP_SOUTH_1)
    .build();

If you ever find yourself writing .credentialsProvider(StaticCredentials...) with a literal key in a deployed application, that is F7's "too wide" flavour waiting to happen.

Timeouts are yours to set, and the defaults are not your defaults. Every SDK client takes an override configuration. Set an API call timeout and an attempt timeout, so a slow AWS dependency cannot become F10:

ClientOverrideConfiguration.builder()
    .apiCallTimeout(Duration.ofSeconds(3))        // whole operation, all retries
    .apiCallAttemptTimeout(Duration.ofMillis(800))// one attempt
    .build();

The SDK retries for you, which is a feature and a trap. It retries throttling and transient errors with backoff automatically. That is why F5 shows up as latency rather than errors.

It also means a write can be attempted more than once. So the idempotency rule holds: a payment write needs a condition expression or an idempotency key, not a hope.

Which pairs get confused, and what separates them?

Nine pairs, and one sentence each. If you can say the right-hand column without looking, the lab has done its job.

The pair people confuse The sentence that separates them
AWS vs "the cloud" AWS is one vendor's implementation of the idea; GCP and Azure make the same three inventions (pool, map, badge) with different names. GCP's zone and region map cleanly, and Azure's availability zone likewise. The failure-domain reasoning ports; the product names do not
Region vs Availability Zone A region is a city and does not fail over to another region by itself; a zone is an independently-powered wing of that city, and is the unit you place replicas across
Availability vs durability Durability is "the bytes still exist"; availability is "you can reach them right now". S3's famous eleven nines is the first one

Elasticity vs scalability Scalability is "can be made bigger"; elasticity is "made bigger and smaller automatically, in minutes, with the bill following"
Control plane vs data plane Changing your setup versus using it. They fail separately, and the second one keeps working
Managed vs serverless Managed means AWS runs the software, and RDS still has an instance size and an hourly cost; serverless means there is no instance and it scales to zero, as with DynamoDB on-demand, Lambda and SQS
Serverless vs Lambda Lambda is one serverless product; S3, DynamoDB on-demand, SQS and EventBridge are equally serverless and considerably older
An SLA vs a guarantee The SLA is what AWS refunds, not what AWS promises will happen. That is section 12.1

Solutions — Amazon Web Services

Attempt the exercises first. For rungs 7–8 there is no single right answer; what follows is a worked position with the reasoning visible, and the wrong answers named.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1

Renting changed who owns the metal and how the bill arrives. It did not change the two things that actually cost Asha the festival:

  1. Capacity stopped being a purchase and became a setting. With the hosting company, more capacity was an order with a human and a lead time of hours or days, in units of one whole machine for one whole month. On AWS it is a message that returns in a minute, and the meter stops when she gives it back. Demand that changes in minutes can now be matched by supply that changes in minutes.
  2. The failure boundaries became visible and contractual. The hosting company could not tell her whether her two machines shared a rack, a power feed or a switch. AWS names those boundaries — availability zones — commits in writing that they do not share power, cooling or a network path, and puts the choice on the screen. That is what turns "two machines" into "two chances".

(A third, honest answer: she can now rent finished distributed systems — an object store, a replicated database, a queue — instead of building them. But that is a consequence of the first two, not a separate idea.)

1.2

An availability zone is one independently-powered wing of a region: one or more data centres with their own power, own cooling and own network, kilometres away from the other zones, joined to them by a fast private link.

It exists because redundancy is a count of independent things that can fail, not a count of machines. Two servers in one rack, behind one switch, on one power feed, are one server with extra steps — when the feed dies, both die. Your two servers are only two chances if something guarantees they do not share the thing that kills them. The zone is that guarantee, written down.

So "we have two servers" is not the question. "Are they in two zones?" is.

1.3

  • (a) Durability is whether the bytes still exist. Availability is whether you can reach them right now. S3's famous eleven nines is durability; its availability SLA is 99.9%, six orders of magnitude weaker, and they are measuring different things.
  • (b) Replication copies your data — including your mistakes — immediately and perfectly to another place. A backup is a point in time you can return to. Replication protects you from infrastructure; only a backup protects you from yourself.
  • (c) The control plane changes your setup (create an instance, modify a table). The data plane uses it (serve a request, read an object). They fail separately, the control plane fails more often and more widely, and data planes are built to keep serving from their last known configuration when it does.

1.4

Two errors, and a third worth mentioning.

Error 1 — an SLA is a refund, not a promise. Missing 99.99% obliges AWS to credit a percentage of that service's bill. It does not oblige reality to cooperate. It is a bound on your compensation, not on your downtime.

Error 2 — the number belongs to one component, and your system is a chain. If the request path touches an ALB, compute, a database and a queue, and each is independently 99.95%, the path is 0.9995⁴ ≈ 99.80% — roughly 17 hours a year, from four components that each promised under five. Availability multiplies downward. You do not get the best number in your architecture; you get something worse than the worst one.

Error 3 (the one that catches people in practice) — the 99.99% EC2 figure describes a deployment across multiple zones. If everything is in one zone, as in §10.3's version 2, that number was never yours to quote. And none of these figures cover your bugs, your deploys, or your misconfiguration, which is where the great majority of real downtime comes from.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1

Inside a zone: application instances, the database primary and standby, the NAT gateway, the subnets. These are AZ-scoped resources — they die when their zone dies, and placing them is your job.

Outside a zone: the load balancer, S3, DynamoDB, and the control plane box.

  • ALB, S3, DynamoDB are drawn outside because they are region-scoped: AWS runs them across zones for you, and there is no screen on which you choose their zone. Losing one zone does not lose them.
  • The control plane is drawn outside and above because that is exactly where it lives conceptually: it is regional, it is the thing you ask for new resources, and — the whole point of the drawing — it fails on its own schedule, independently of the zones below it.

That layout is the lesson. If a box is in a column, you placed it and you own its failure. If it is above the columns, AWS placed it.

2.2

The panel names the zone (ap-south-1a in v3) and shows standby: none. In v4 it shows a primary in one zone and a standby in another, with the replication marked synchronous. The give-away that v3 is dangerous is that a single box carrying all the state sits in one column.

2.3

The connection line from the load balancer to that instance. A live line means the target is in rotation; a dimmed or cut line means it has been removed. The instance's own colour tells you health; the line tells you routing, and the gap between the two — an unhealthy instance that is still receiving traffic — is the health-check detection window from F1.

2.4

Changed from v3 to v4: the database gains a standby in a second zone with synchronous replication (§10.5a), and the static stability control becomes meaningful (§10.6). The ASG and the three-zone spread were already there from v3 (§10.3). Nothing about the application tier changes — which is itself the point: version 4 is entirely a story about state.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1

Success rate: unchanged at ~100%. Load balancer: healthy. Killing a zone that contains none of your resources does nothing to you. The instructive part is what this proves: an AZ failure is only your problem to the extent you had something in it, which is exactly why the placement decision is the whole game. It also shows why "AWS had an AZ event" and "we had an outage" are different sentences.

3.2

  • Kill 1c: roughly two thirds capacity immediately, recovering as the ASG replaces the lost instance. Payments keep working.
  • Kill 1a: everything fails, and slowly.

The same number of things died — one zone, one instance. But 1a held the single database. The application servers in 1b and 1c are perfectly healthy; they accept requests, try to reach a database that is gone, and block until their connection times out. So shops get 30-second waits ending in 500s, which is worse for them than a fast failure.

The lesson is §10.4's: elasticity is a property of the stateless tier and buys you nothing while state has a single home. Spreading nine application servers across three zones is decoration if the one thing they all need is in one.

3.3

Success rate stays at roughly two thirds — about 660 of 1,000 requests/sec — and stays there. The system is stuck on RunInstances, which returns capacity errors and is retried with backoff.

The precise shape: the ALB does its job (data plane, works fine) and stops routing to the dead zone within about 30 seconds. The database fails over (data plane-ish — the standby already exists). Then recovery stops, because the remaining step needs the control plane to give the ASG four new machines, and the control plane is exactly what is unavailable. Nothing is broken. Nothing is retrying wrongly. The design simply asked for something that is not being handed out tonight.

3.4

Success rate: ~100%, essentially immediately.

What physically changed: you were already running 50% more machines than you needed, so losing a third left you with exactly enough. The RunInstances call still fails — that has not changed at all. It no longer matters, because nothing was waiting on it; recovery consisted entirely of the load balancer stopping sending traffic to dead targets, which is a data-plane action that needs no one's permission.

That is static stability in one sentence: the response to a failure is to keep doing what you were already doing with what you already have.

3.5

Nothing goes wrong, and that is the trap. A single-AZ database at festival traffic performs fine — arguably marginally better, since there is no synchronous cross-AZ write to wait for. The design is now one zone failure away from total loss and every dashboard is green.

This is the general shape of an availability bug: it is invisible until the fault arrives. It is why game days exist, and why "it's been fine for a year" is not evidence about a design's failure behaviour.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1

Availability is lost. Durability, correctness and elasticity are untouched.

The data is still safe (it is in a region-scoped store or on EBS with snapshots), answers are still right, and the ASG still scales perfectly well. The system simply now has a single failure domain containing 100% of the application tier. It will scale beautifully right up until the zone dies, at which point it will serve nothing.

Worth noticing: three of the four properties are fine, so three quarters of your monitoring will say everything is excellent.

4.2

One system recovers by using capacity it already has; the other recovers by asking for capacity it does not have.

That is the whole difference, and it does not appear on any architecture diagram — which is precisely why F3 keeps catching well-designed systems. Two identical boxes-and-arrows drawings, opposite outcomes, and the distinguishing property is a number (how much headroom) rather than a shape.

4.3

Elasticity is lost. Correctness, durability and availability-under-failure are all intact; the system just cannot get bigger. Festival traffic arrives, the ASG dutifully scales to its maximum, and the maximum is the normal-traffic number.

The reason this deserves its own exercise: nothing is red. There is no failed health check, no exception, no alarm. There is a line in the ASG activity history saying it wanted to scale and could not. This is the same failure shape as F4, and it is the argument for alarming on headroom rather than on errors.

4.4

Nothing changes. A quota is only a problem when it is below what you need, and — the useful corollary — you cannot tell whether a quota is a problem by looking at it. You can only tell by comparing it to your peak requirement, in that region, including the requirement your failover plan would create.

That is why F4's production advice is to enumerate the quotas your recovery path needs in every region you would fail over to. A quota that is generous in your primary region and default in your DR region is a plan that works in tests and fails in reality.

4.5

Synchronous cross-region writes make every write slower by at least one cross-region round trip; asynchronous ones do not slow writes at all but introduce a window of possible data loss.

The number from the README: Mumbai to Ireland is roughly 120ms one way, so a consensus round trip is ~240ms and a realistic protocol needs more than one. A payment API with a 400ms budget has spent all of it before doing any work. Against that, asynchronous replication with sub-second lag adds 0ms to writes and costs you an RPO of about one second — up to a second of committed payments that a sudden region loss would take with it.

There is no third option; this is PACELC's "else" branch with the actual speed of light substituted in. Which you choose is a business decision about whether one second of lost payments is worse than half a second added to every payment.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 — the design that passes every review

The flaw: backups retained 7 days, and no mention of where they live or of a restore ever having been performed.

Everything in that description defends against infrastructure failure, and it defends well — the game day proved it. Nothing in it defends against F8: a bad migration, a wrong WHERE clause, a compromised credential issuing legitimate delete calls. Aurora's six copies will replicate the damage to all six.

A concrete run: a migration on a Friday drops a column that a rarely-used reconciliation report depends on. Nobody notices, because the report runs monthly. On day 9 finance runs it, it fails, and the data needed to reconstruct the column is now beyond the 7-day window. The system was 100% available throughout.

The guarantee violated: none of AWS's — every one held. The violated assumption is the customer's, and it is §7.5's: replication is not backup, and "managed" never moved the question "is my data correct?"

Why the game day could not find it: the game day tested a failure mode that was designed for. The retention window is a bet about detection time, and no infrastructure fault exercises it.

What else is thin, secondarily: min 9 / max 30 with three zones means losing a zone leaves 6 instances and requires launching 3 more — F3 territory. And there is no mention of the backups living in a separate account, so a credential compromise that can delete the database can probably delete the backups too.

6.2 — the disaster recovery plan

Reason 1 (§7.4 — quotas are per-region). The eu-west-1 account has never run production. Its vCPU quota, its RDS instance quotas, its Elastic IP quota are all at defaults. terraform apply will get partway through and start returning limit errors, and a quota increase is a support ticket with a human on the other end, measured in hours to days. Your one-hour RTO is now a multi-day RTO, discovered during the incident.

Reason 2 (a plan is not a rehearsal). Restoring an RDS snapshot is not instant — for a database of meaningful size it is tens of minutes on its own, before the DNS change, before connection pools recover (F11), before caches warm. Nobody in the plan has ever measured it. "One hour" is an aspiration someone typed, and the first measurement of it will be taken under maximum pressure with an audience.

The generalisable rule: an untested RTO is not a number, it is a wish. The minimum credible version of that plan includes a restore rehearsal on a calendar and the measured duration written next to the target.

(A third, if you found it: snapshots every 5 minutes is not what an RPO of 5 minutes requires — you also need the copy to eu-west-1 to have completed, which adds its own lag. The true RPO is snapshot interval plus copy time.)

6.3 — the idempotency table

Flaw 1 (F5 — the partition key is a hot key by construction). Partition key date means every charge in the entire system on a given day shares one partition key. That is not skew; that is a design that guarantees a single partition takes 100% of write traffic. It will hit the per-partition write ceiling and throttle, and because the SDK retries with backoff, the first symptom will be p99 latency on the payment path rather than errors.

The fix is the sub-key splitting from your distributed-rate-limiting lab: make the partition key the charge_id itself (naturally uniform), or if you need date-range queries, date#<shard 0..N> with the charge id hashed into the shard.

Flaw 2 — "exactly-once payments" is the wrong claim. A conditional write gives you at-most-once execution of the effect for a given key, which is idempotency, and that is genuinely valuable. It does not give exactly-once, because the step after the conditional write — actually calling the bank — can still fail after the key is claimed, leaving a key that says "done" for a charge that never happened. Real designs store the outcome against the key and make the whole sequence resumable, so a retry of a half-finished charge completes it rather than skipping it.

The correct sentence is: "duplicate requests do not produce duplicate charges", not "exactly once". Exactly-once delivery does not exist; exactly- once effect is something you construct.

6.4 — the health check

The run: the database gets slow — not dead, slow. Say a bad query plan after a statistics refresh doubles latency.

  1. SELECT 1 starts taking longer than the health check timeout on some instances.
  2. The ALB marks those instances unhealthy and removes them.
  3. Remaining traffic concentrates on fewer instances, which now issue more queries each to the already-struggling database.
  4. Those instances' health checks start failing too.
  5. The ASG terminates the "unhealthy" instances and launches replacements — which boot, connect to the same slow database, fail their first health checks, and are terminated in turn.
  6. Every instance is now unhealthy, the target group is empty, and the ALB returns 503 to everything.

A dependency that was degraded has become a total outage, and the mechanism was the recovery system. The database was never down.

The rule: a health check answers "should this instance receive traffic?", not "is the whole system well?". Make it shallow — process alive, can accept a connection — and detect dependency problems with monitoring and circuit breakers, which degrade rather than amputate. A deep health check converts a partial degradation into a fleet-wide, self-inflicted outage, and it does so fastest exactly when the dependency is under most stress.

6.5 — the permission

Two things, at least.

One (data): s3:* includes s3:DeleteObject and s3:PutObject. The attacker can not only read every receipt, they can delete or overwrite them all, and dynamodb:* includes dynamodb:DeleteTable. The author was thinking about read access and wrote a wildcard that authorises destruction. This is F8 with a hostile operator.

Two (not about data at all): dynamodb:* includes dynamodb:UpdateTable — the attacker can raise provisioned capacity, and on other services the equivalent wildcard authorises creating expensive resources. More generally, wildcards routinely include the management actions of a service, not just its data actions: changing replication, disabling point-in-time recovery, altering encryption configuration, removing a resource policy. Turning off PITR before deleting is the difference between an incident and an extinction event.

Three, if you want it: the reasoning "it's scoped to two resources so the blast radius is small" measures blast radius in resources when it should measure in actions. s3:GetObject on one bucket and s3:* on one bucket are the same resource scope and wildly different blast radii.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 — making retries harmless

What the client sends: an idempotency key it generates once per logical charge attempt and reuses across every retry of that attempt — typically a UUID in a header. The crucial property is that the key is generated before the first send and does not change on retry; a key generated per HTTP request is not an idempotency key, it is a request id.

What the server stores, and where: a record keyed by the idempotency key, in a store that is strongly consistent and supports conditional writes. DynamoDB with a conditional put is the natural AWS answer; the row holds the key, a state (in_progress / succeeded / failed), the request fingerprint (a hash of the important request fields), the resulting charge id, and a TTL.

The conditional write, conceptually: "insert this row only if no row with this key exists" — DynamoDB's attribute_not_exists(idempotency_key). Exactly one writer wins.

Two simultaneous retries: one wins the conditional write and proceeds. The other gets the condition failure, reads the existing row, and finds state in_progress. It must not proceed and must not return success. The correct response is to wait briefly and re-read, and if still in progress, return a retryable status (409, or 202 with a location to poll) so the client tries again shortly. Returning success for work that has not finished is the subtle bug here.

A request fingerprint matters: if the same key arrives with a different amount, that is a client bug or an attack, and the right answer is a 422, not a silent replay of the original.

When the idempotency store is unavailable — fail open or closed? For a payments API: fail closed. Reject the charge with a retryable error. The reasoning is asymmetric cost. Fail-open means processing charges with no duplicate protection, during an incident, which is exactly when clients are retrying most aggressively — the failure mode is double-charging real customers with real money, which is a regulatory and trust problem you cannot undo with a deploy. Fail-closed means some payments do not happen for a few minutes, which is revenue you recover when the shop retries.

Note this is the opposite of the usual advice for a rate limiter, where fail-open is often right — and it is the same reasoning both times: ask what the wrong answer costs. This is the "fail-open/fail-closed chosen per limit, never once for the whole system" rule from your rate-limiting lab, applied to a different resource.

7.2 — surviving F3 without 50% overprovisioning

Option A — pre-provisioned warm capacity that is already running but not serving. Keep instances launched and registered but weighted to near-zero traffic, or in a second target group. On failure, shift weight — a data-plane action. Cost: you are still paying for the machines, so this is 50% overprovisioning wearing a different hat unless you use the spare capacity for something interruptible (batch, analytics) that you are willing to evict. Then the marginal cost is genuinely lower, and the price is operational complexity plus the risk that the batch workload does not actually yield fast enough.

Option B — four zones instead of three. With N=4, surviving N−1 requires only 33% overprovisioning rather than 50%. Cost: not all regions have four usable zones, and every cross-AZ chat now has more paths (F9's transfer charges). This is the cheapest option where it is available and it is underused.

Option C — degrade instead of overprovision. Accept that a zone loss means reduced capacity, and make the reduction graceful rather than random: shed low-value traffic first (reporting, analytics, non-critical merchants), keep the payment path whole. Cost: you must have built prioritised load shedding, which is real work and must be tested; and you have accepted that some customers get a worse experience during the event. This is often the right answer and it is the least fashionable, because it requires admitting in advance which traffic matters less.

Option D, mentioned to reject it: "use a different instance type as a fallback in the ASG's mixed-instances policy". This genuinely helps — capacity shortages are usually per-instance-type — and it costs almost nothing, so do it. But it is a probability improvement, not a structural one: it makes RunInstances more likely to succeed rather than making you independent of it. Take it as a cheap supplement, never as the plan.

7.3 — why the app stays broken after failover

Layers holding the stale endpoint, outermost first:

  1. JVM DNS cache. networkaddress.cache.ttl — historically the JVM cached successful lookups forever under a security manager. Set it to something like 5–30 seconds for any application that depends on DNS failover.
  2. The OS / local resolver / any caching resolver in between, each with its own TTL, none of which respect your intentions.
  3. The connection pool. This is the big one. Even with perfect DNS, the pool holds established TCP connections to the old address. It will happily hand out a socket to a machine that is gone; isConnected() returns true until something writes. Fix: a validation query on borrow (or at minimum on idle), a bounded max connection lifetime so connections are recycled routinely, and fast failure detection so a broken connection is discarded rather than retried.
  4. Socket-level timeouts. Without a read timeout, a thread blocked on a dead peer waits for the OS TCP retransmission timeout — minutes. Set an explicit read timeout.
  5. The application's own retry policy. Retries against a cached bad address just multiply the damage. Retries should re-resolve.

The meta-point, from your networking lab: DNS failover updates a name, and your process resolved that name a long time ago. Every layer between the name and the socket is a place the old answer can hide. And the only way to find out which of the five layers is biting you is to trigger a failover on purpose, which RDS lets you do on demand.

7.4 — the guardrail against the honest mistake

The constraint is the interesting part: the person had legitimate access and made an honest error, and must keep being able to do their job. So "remove their access" and "be more careful" are both non-answers.

A layered answer, cheapest first:

  1. Make destruction reversible by default. S3 versioning on anything irreplaceable — a delete becomes a delete marker, and the object is still there. Point-in-time recovery on databases with a retention window chosen against detection time (how long until someone notices?), not against a default. This is the single highest-value item and it is a checkbox.
  2. Make destruction slower than a keystroke. Deletion protection on production databases and tables; prevent_destroy in Terraform. Neither stops a determined operator; both stop an accidental one, which is who we are defending against.
  3. Put the backups where production's credentials cannot reach them. A separate account, with a role that production cannot assume. This is the one that also defends against a compromised credential, and it is the reason the account boundary is the cheapest blast-radius control AWS sells.
  4. Make the dangerous action visible before it happens. Migrations that drop data go through a review that specifically asks "is this reversible?"; a Terraform plan showing a destroy is a blocking review, not a scroll-past.
  5. Change the shape of the action. Two-phase deletes: rename or mark deprecated_ and wait a fortnight before dropping. This one costs almost nothing and catches the largest class of these mistakes, because the honest error is nearly always about something else still using it.

Note what the list has in common: none of them add a human check to the moment of the action, because humans are unreliable at exactly that moment. They all either make the action reversible, slow, or contained.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 — Marigold goes multi-region

Region pairing. ap-south-1 (Mumbai) primary, ap-south-2 (Hyderabad) secondary. Reasoning: Marigold's customers are all in India, and Indian payments data is subject to data-localisation expectations — putting the secondary in Ireland would be a regulatory conversation before it is a technical one. Same-country pairing also keeps the inter-region round trip in the tens of milliseconds rather than 240ms, which makes some designs viable that would not be transcontinentally. (Verify current region availability and the applicable regulatory position — this is a design assumption, not a fact I am asserting.)

Where the write path lives: one region at a time. Active-passive. Every write goes to Mumbai. Hyderabad runs the full stack, warm, serving nothing (or serving reads only). This is the position to defend hardest, because "active-active" sounds better in a board slide and is the wrong answer here.

Why not active-active: the charges table is money. Multi-active replication resolves conflicts, and every available resolution — last-writer- wins in DynamoDB Global Tables, or anything else automatic — can silently discard one of two concurrent writes. There is no acceptable version of "silently discarded" for a payment. To do active-active safely you would need partitioned ownership (each merchant's writes always go to one region, so there are no concurrent writes to the same row) — which is achievable, is what sophisticated shops actually do, and is a much bigger programme than the board is currently authorising. State that as the upgrade path, not as v1.

RPO and RTO, as numbers.

  • RPO: 0 for the authorisation record, ~1 second for everything else. The trick that makes this affordable: not all data has the same value. The charges table gets synchronous cross-region durability on the decision record only — a small, append-only write — accepting the added latency on the one write that must never be lost. Everything else (merchant profiles, settlement staging, receipts in S3, analytics) replicates asynchronously with ~1s lag, RPO 1s. If even that is too expensive at Marigold's latency budget, the fallback is RPO ~1s for everything plus a reconciliation process against the bank's own records, which is the answer the payments industry has actually used for decades: the bank is a second source of truth, and you reconcile.
  • RTO: 15 minutes, achieved by having Hyderabad already running with sufficient capacity (static stability across regions, not just zones) so failover is a promotion plus a traffic shift, not a build.

How failover is triggered, and by whom: a human, with a runbook, on a documented decision. Not automatic. Automatic cross-region failover is a machine that can start a split brain over money on the basis of a network blip, and the blast radius of a false positive is larger than the cost of ten minutes of human latency. The runbook must assume the console is unusable and must not require any us-east-1 control-plane call it can avoid; Route 53 health-check-based failover between existing endpoints keeps working, so pre-create the records and the health checks rather than planning to edit DNS during the incident.

What I refuse to do, and why:

  • Refuse synchronous cross-region for the whole write path — the latency budget cannot absorb it and it makes the normal case worse to improve a rare one.
  • Refuse last-writer-wins on money.
  • Refuse automatic failover (above).
  • Refuse to call it done before a live failover test. An untested failover is a hypothesis. Schedule it, quarterly, in business hours, with the board informed.

The sentence for the board: "Surviving the loss of a region roughly doubles our infrastructure bill and adds a class of data-consistency risk we do not have today. I recommend we buy it, because a full-day outage would cost us more than that in a single day and would probably cost us the licence conversation as well — but I want us to agree the number we are protecting before we spend it, and I want a quarterly test in the budget, because an untested failover is not protection."

8.2 — the account structure

The accounts, each justified by blast radius:

Account Why it exists
Management / root Owns the Organization and nothing else. No workloads. Its credentials are the keys to everything, so it should have almost no reason to be logged into
Security / audit Centralised logs (CloudTrail, config) that other accounts can write to and not delete. Survives a compromise of any workload account
Log archive / backups Backup copies that production's credentials cannot reach — this is 7.4 item 3, made structural
Production The workload. Its quotas, its IAM, its blast radius, alone
Staging Separate specifically because quotas are per-account: a load test here must not be able to consume production's capacity headroom (§7.8)
Development / sandbox Per-team or per-engineer, with hard spend guardrails. People learn by doing dangerous things; give them somewhere it is safe
Shared services Networking, CI/CD, artefact registries, DNS. Things production depends on but should not own

Three SCP guardrails first:

  1. Region restriction — deny all actions outside the regions you actually use. Prevents both cost surprises in unwatched regions and a whole class of crypto-mining abuse, and it is one policy.
  2. Deny disabling security controls — no principal in any workload account may stop CloudTrail, delete log buckets, disable GuardDuty or turn off default encryption. This is the guardrail that survives a compromised admin, because explicit deny cannot be overridden by any allow (§7.6).
  3. Deny root user actions — the account root user should never be used for day-to-day anything; deny its use outside a break-glass procedure.

The thing that will actually be hard, and it is not technical: ownership and the migration of shared resources. One VPC, one database, one S3 bucket that four teams use, all created before anyone thought about accounts, all with IAM policies naming principals that will cease to exist. Deciding which account owns each shared thing means deciding which team owns it, and that is an organisational negotiation that will take longer than the Terraform. Start there, not with the accounts. The usual accelerant is to create the new accounts, put all new workloads in them from day one, and migrate the old estate opportunistically — a big-bang migration of a live estate is rarely worth it.

8.3 — choosing the compute

Traffic: 200 rps average, 2,000 rps peak for 40 minutes twice a day. p99 400ms. JVM start 6 seconds.

The case for ECS Fargate (my choice). The 10× peak is real but predictable and scheduled, which means scheduled scaling handles it without any cold-start question at all — scale out 10 minutes before each peak. Fargate removes the instance fleet without removing the container model, so the 6-second JVM start happens at scale-out time, not on the request path. No Kubernetes to operate at 40 people. The 400ms p99 budget is comfortable for a warm JVM.

The case against my own choice — and it is a real case:

  • Fargate costs meaningfully more per unit of compute than EC2. At a sustained 200 rps baseline running 24/7, that premium is paid every hour of every day for the convenience of not managing instances. EC2 with an ASG and a Savings Plan for the baseline plus Spot for the peak could be substantially cheaper, and at sufficient scale that difference funds an engineer.
  • If the company is already running Kubernetes elsewhere, adding a second orchestration model is a real cost — one deployment pipeline, one set of runbooks, one thing to learn is worth a lot, and consistency can beat local optimality.
  • Fargate's task startup is slower than an already-warm EC2 host, so an unpredicted spike — a marketing email, an outage at a competitor — is handled less gracefully than by a statically-stable EC2 fleet. Since we learned in §10.6 that headroom beats scaling, the design that carries headroom is the more robust one, and headroom is cheaper on EC2.

Lambda, rejected with reasons: 200 rps sustained is not a spiky workload, it is a steady one — that is the regime where per-request pricing loses to per-hour pricing. And a 6-second Spring Boot cold start against a 400ms p99 budget means you are buying provisioned concurrency, which is Lambda pretending to be a server at a worse price. Lambda is right for the event glue around this service, not for the request path.

8.4 — the bill doubled

Investigation, in order, and why this order.

  1. Cost Explorer, grouped by service, daily granularity, last 60 days. This takes two minutes and answers the only question that matters first: is it one thing or everything? A step change on one date in one service is a different investigation from a gradual rise across many.
  2. Then group by the same service, by usage type. "EC2" doubling could be instance hours or it could be data transfer, and those have entirely different causes. This step is where most investigations actually end.
  3. Then by region. Spend appearing in a region nobody uses is either a forgotten resource or a security incident, and you want to know which within the hour.
  4. Then by cost-allocation tag / account. This is where having mandated tags pays for itself: with them, "which team?" is a filter. Without them, you are correlating resource ids by hand for a week — which is itself the finding, and the recommendation writes itself.
  5. Then correlate the step date against CloudTrail for creation events, and against the deploy history. "No deploy anyone remembers" is not evidence; CloudTrail is.

The three most likely patterns, from F9:

  • Data transfer, not compute. Cross-AZ chatter (a service moved, or a new replica landed in a different zone, and now a hot path crosses a zone boundary on every call), NAT Gateway per-GB processing, or internet egress from something newly chatty. This is the most common invisible doubling because no resource was created — a path just changed.
  • Something that scaled and never scaled back. An auto-scaling group whose scale-in policy is broken or whose cooldown is wrong; a DynamoDB table switched to on-demand during an incident and never switched back; a log volume increase (a debug log level left on in production is a classic, and it shows up as CloudWatch Logs ingestion, which people forget is metered).
  • An abandoned resource. An idle cluster, unattached EBS volumes, old snapshots, an environment spun up for a test in a region nobody watches. This is the boring one and it is very often the answer.

The fix that outlasts the investigation: mandatory tagging enforced by SCP, a budget alert per account, and an anomaly-detection alert. The goal is that the next time this happens you find out on day two, not on the invoice.

8.5 — reading a real postmortem

(This is a reading exercise; the value is in doing it. What follows is what to look for, not a substitute for reading the source.)

Which failure mode: both the November 2020 us-east-1 Kinesis event and the December 2021 us-east-1 event map primarily to F3 — control plane unavailable while data planes are substantially healthier — with strong elements of F6 (the us-east-1 global-service dependency, since services whose control planes concentrate there were affected far outside that region's workloads) and of the dependency cascade pattern in F10 (one internal service's degradation propagating to the many services that depended on it).

Which customer designs were unaffected: ones that were already running enough capacity, already had their configuration in place, and needed nothing new from AWS during the event. Statically stable designs (§10.6) had a quiet night. Designs whose recovery, deployment, or scaling depended on control-plane calls did not — and notably, so did designs whose incident response tooling depended on the console.

The sentence that maps to §7.3: look for the paragraph distinguishing what kept serving from what could not be changed — the postmortems are usually explicit that existing resources continued operating while creation, scaling and describe operations were impaired. That sentence is §7.3 in AWS's own words, and it is the justification for everything in §10.6.

A worthwhile extra question to ask yourself while reading: how long was it between customer impact starting and the status page reflecting it? That gap is why the personalised AWS Health Dashboard and your own monitoring matter more than the public page.
