Lab 71 · Whole Systems

Google Docs — how a hundred people type into one sentence without losing a word

Tell a friend: Alice and Bob edit the same shopping list from two cities, and both change it at the same moment. Simple designs either lose one person's letter, or leave the two screens showing different text with nobody told. The fix is to stop sending the document and to send the change instead: "put an S at position 3". Every machine then rewrites that instruction before running it, so it still means what its author meant on a page that has moved on. One product decision forces all of this. Your keystroke appears on your own screen before any server has agreed to it, and everything else in this lab is the bill for that decision.

Play with it: visualization.html — Two Cursors, One Sentence.

1. Why does emailing the document round lose somebody's work?

In one breath: When everyone edits their own copy, someone has to merge the copies later. Somewhere in that merge, one person's work quietly disappears. Nobody is told.

Alice and Bob work on the same team. They are writing a plan together, and the plan goes round by email.

Three people edit their own copy on the same afternoon. Then somebody spends an evening merging four files, one of which is called plan_final_v2_REALLY_FINAL.docx. You have almost certainly lived this.

Somewhere in that merge, an edit is lost. Not deleted with a warning. Just gone, because a later save overwrote an earlier one.

Now change the product. Put every one of those people inside one document at the same time. Show them each other's cursors. Let their letters appear on each other's screens as they type.

That is Google Docs. The entire engineering problem is three words from the sentence you just read: at the same time.

Everything else is ordinary web engineering you have built before. Storage, sharing, comments, version history: none of that is new. The concurrent editing is new, and it is where this lab spends almost all of its time.

What happens if nobody solves it? You ship one of two bad products.

The first locks the document, so only one person may type. That is a fancier shared drive. The second lets two people type at once, and silently produces text that neither of them wrote.

This lab builds the second product properly. Alice and Bob carry the story the whole way. Carol joins in section 11 with a terrible Wi-Fi connection, and Meera takes the pager in section 15.

2. What should the fridge list say when Alice and Bob both change it?

In one breath: Two people, one three-letter list, one change each. Nearly everybody agrees on the right answer. Delivering it is the hard part.

Alice and Bob share a shopping list stuck to a fridge. It says:

HAT

It is a strange shopping list. It is also short enough that you can follow every single character, which is exactly why we are using it.

Alice thinks it should say CHAT. Bob thinks it should say HATS.

They are in different cities. Each is looking at their own photo of the list. They both make their change at the same moment.

🔮 Predict: What should the fridge say afterwards? Commit to one word before you read on.

Reveal Almost everybody answers CHATS: Alice's C at the front, Bob's S at the end, both there, nothing lost. If you answered CHAT or HATS, you picked a winner between two people who were not even arguing. Picking a winner is the failure this whole lab exists to avoid. Hold on to CHATS. It is the specification for everything that follows, and it is surprisingly hard to deliver.

3. What does a reasonable person try first, and what does it cost?

In one breath: Everybody edits their own copy and sends the whole document back to a server. Whoever saves last wins, and the other person's letter is gone with no error at all.

This is not a strawman. It is exactly how a file on a shared drive works. It is also how the first generation of document-sync products worked.

   Bob saves                server: "HATS"

🔮 Predict: Alice's C is gone. What does Alice's screen show at this moment, and when will she find out?

Reveal Her screen still says CHAT — her own copy was never touched. She finds out the next time she reloads, or never. There was no error, no conflict, no prompt. The system did exactly what it was told: store the most recent document it was given. This failure mode has a name we will keep using: a lost update. Two people read the same starting value, both changed it, and one change vanished under the other.

If you guessed her screen would jump to HATS, you are imagining a system that pushes the truth back to her. Nothing in this design does that. Nobody told her anything, because nobody noticed anything.

A lost update is the worst shape a bug can take. It is silent, it is unrecoverable, and the only person who could notice is the one who is no longer looking.

4. Why does sending just the change break too?

In one breath: Locking and merging both fail for a text editor. So you send the change instead of the whole document. That breaks for a new reason: the change was written for a page that has since moved underneath it.

The lost update is the visible failure. Two familiar fixes both fail as well, and watching them fail is what uncovers the real problem.

The first fix is a lock. One person edits, everybody else is read-only. Microsoft Word did this for years, with a message reading "Locked for editing by Ravi". It works, and it produces a product nobody wants. The whole point is that four people can work on one document during a meeting.

A lock also inherits the blocking problem you already met in the two-phase-commit lab. If the lock holder's laptop dies, the document is stuck until something times out.

The second fix is to merge on save, the way Git does. Send both versions, compute a diff, merge them.

That is a real answer for source code. Merges happen a few times a day there, and a human is standing by to resolve conflicts. In a document, edits happen several times per second per person. Nobody is going to resolve a three-way merge conflict between keystrokes.

So the deeper problem is not "how do we merge?". It is this:

We have to merge continuously, many times a second, and with no human involved. We also have to do it without anybody ever waiting, because a text editor that pauses after each letter is not a text editor.

Now try the honest version of that. Instead of sending the whole document, send only what changed: "put an S at position 3."

That is far less data. It also looks like it should merge naturally, because two different instructions do not obviously overwrite each other. Run it:

Reveal Neither is right, and that is the point. Alice's screen says CHAST, which nobody typed and nobody wanted. Bob's says CHATS, which is the answer we want but arrived by luck. If you guessed that the server would referee the disagreement, look again: the server itself holds CHAST. Everybody followed the rules and the rules were wrong.

Here is the whole bug in one sentence. Bob wrote "put an S at position 3" while looking at HAT, where position 3 was the end of the word.

By the time that instruction reached Alice, her own C had pushed every letter one place to the right. Position 3 now sat between the A and the T.

The instruction was correct when it was written and wrong when it was run, because the document underneath it had changed.

Notice the second thing, which is worse than the first. Alice and Bob's screens now show different text, and neither of them will ever be told. There is no error to catch. The system is confidently wrong.

5. What exactly must the system promise?

In one breath: Four promises. Your letter appears instantly, everyone ends up with the same text, what each person meant survives, and nobody ever waits for anybody else.

Every design decision in the rest of this lab exists to keep one of these four. Read the last column first: it says what Alice or Bob would actually experience if that promise were dropped.

# The promise In plain words What its absence feels like
1 Instant local response your letter is on your screen before any server hears about it a 200 ms stutter on every single keystroke
2 Convergence when the typing stops, every copy holds identical text Alice reads CHAST, Bob reads CHATS, forever
3 Intention preservation Alice's C lands at the front, Bob's S at the end the merge is tidy and the sentence is not what anyone wrote
4 Nobody waits no locks, no turn-taking, no "your edit is pending approval" one person types and three people watch

Promise 1 is the product decision that creates the whole problem. A round trip to a server is 50 to 300 ms. You would feel that on every letter, so the letter has to be drawn before the server has agreed to anything.

Promises 2 and 3 are separate, and the difference matters more than it looks. A design can satisfy one and fail the other. Design 2 in section 4 failed promise 2 outright. A design that resolved every conflict by blanking the document would keep promise 2 perfectly and destroy promise 3.

You need words for that difference, which is what section 7 is for.

Promise 4 is invisible until it breaks. It rules out the lock from section 4, and it rules out every scheme where the server has to approve an edit before you may see it.

6. What is the one key idea?

In one breath: Send the change, not the document. Then, before you run somebody else's change, rewrite it to account for everything it was written without knowing about.

Two sentences carry the whole lab.

Send the change, not the document — an instruction like insert 'S' at position 3, so two people's work can be combined instead of overwriting each other.

Before running somebody else's instruction, rewrite it to account for the changes it was written without knowing about. Bob's "S at 3" becomes "S at 4" once you know a character was inserted in front of it.

That rewriting step is the invention. Everything else in this lab is bookkeeping in service of doing the rewriting correctly, in the right order, on every machine. The servers, the numbering, the queues and the retries are all bill, not idea.

7. Which words do you now need, and what does each name?

In one breath: Ten words carry the rest of the lab. Every one of them names something that has already happened to Alice and Bob, so nothing here is new. Only the labels are.

This is the whole vocabulary you need to read a real paper or a real codebase on this subject. Each word arrives at the moment you have already felt the thing it names.

What is an operation, and why is it not a document?

Bob typed an S at the end of HAT. There are two ways to tell the world about that.

Design 1 sent the result: the four characters HATS. Design 2 sent the description: put an S at position 3. Engineers call that description an operation.

Precisely, an operation is a small immutable value describing one edit. In this lab there are exactly two kinds, insert(position, character) and delete(position). An operation can be sent over a network, stored in a log and applied to a document. It can also be rewritten, which is the ability the rest of this lab is built on.

The word exists to keep the description of a change apart from the result of a change. Results can only overwrite each other. Descriptions can be combined.

Bob's keystroke becomes the operation insert(3, 'S'), which is roughly 30 bytes on the wire instead of the whole document.

You have made this exact move before. In the event-sourcing lab you stored what happened rather than what is. A document here is a fold over its operations, in the same way that an account balance is a fold over its transactions.

What does it mean to apply an operation?

To apply an operation is to actually carry it out on some text. Written as code it is a pure function: apply(document, operation) → document. In this lab it is nine lines of String surgery in src/Ot.java.

Purity is doing real work here. The function looks at nothing but its two arguments. So the same operation applied to the same document gives the same answer on Alice's laptop, on Bob's laptop and on the server, always.

That is what makes the whole design testable. Every disagreement between two machines must come from a difference in the inputs, never from the function.

What is a revision number really counting?

A revision is how many edits the document has been through. It is an integer counter, and nothing more.

The server assigns revision n to the n-th operation it accepts. Each client remembers the revision it has caught up to, and stamps every operation it sends with that number. The stamp reads: "this was written against revision 4."

The word exists to turn a vague question into a number. The question is "what did this person know when they wrote that?", and the number is the input to the rewriting rule.

Watch it happen. Bob sends insert(3,'S') stamped revision 0. The server's log is already at revision 1, because Alice's C arrived first.

The gap between those two numbers, revisions 0 to 1, is exactly the set of edits Bob had not seen. It is also exactly what his operation must be rewritten against. The whole protocol lives in that gap.

Why is the log the document?

The log is the official list of everything that has happened to this document, in order. It is an append-only sequence of operations, held by the server.

The document is defined as the log replayed from the beginning. Revision numbers are simply positions in it. There is no other definition of "the truth" anywhere in the design.

This is the event store from the event-sourcing lab again, right down to the reason snapshots exist. Nobody wants to replay a million operations just to open a document, so the folded-up result is stored from time to time. Section 15 does that properly.

If you find yourself thinking "this is just an event log with a projection", you are correct.

Why does "everybody agrees" need two words, not one?

Because two very different things can go wrong, and you need to be able to say which one did.

Convergence means everybody's screen ends up the same. Once every operation has been delivered everywhere, every copy of the document is byte-identical, no matter what order things arrived in.

The word exists because "consistent" is overloaded to the point of uselessness. Convergence says nothing at all about what the final text is. It only says there is one of it. Design 2 failed convergence: CHAST on one screen, CHATS on the other.

Intention preservation means what you meant still happened. Precisely: the effect of each operation in the final document matches the effect it would have had on the document its author was looking at when they wrote it.

That word exists exactly because convergence alone is not enough. Here is the cheapest proof. "When two people edit at once, delete the whole document" converges beautifully. It is also useless.

Intention preservation is much harder to state formally than convergence, which is why real papers state convergence as a theorem and intention as a design goal.

Look at our two candidate answers through both words. Alice meant "a C at the front". In CHATS there is a C at the front, and Bob's S is at the end: both intentions preserved. In CHAST, Bob's S is not at the end, so his intention was lost even though nothing crashed and nobody complained.

What does it mean to transform an instruction?

To transform is to rewrite an instruction so that it still does the right thing on a document that has moved on.

Written as code it is a function, transform(a, b) → a′. It answers one question: operation a was written for a document that does not contain b, so what must a become to have the same effect on a document that does contain b?

The word exists because "merge" implies combining two results. This combines nothing. It produces a modified instruction, which is then applied completely normally.

Bob's edit is the worked example. transform(insert(3,'S'), insert(0,'C')) is insert(4,'S'). In words: something was inserted in front of me, so I shift right by one.

The whole family of techniques built on that function is called operational transformation, usually shortened to OT. When you see "OT" in a paper about editors, this is it. Its rival, which gives every character a permanent name instead of rewriting positions, is the CRDT family, and section 15 argues the two against each other properly.

Who goes first when Alice and Bob type in the same spot?

Somebody has to, and every machine has to pick the same somebody. That rule is the tie-break.

For two concurrent inserts at exactly the same position, transformation orders them by a value that is identical on every machine. Here it is the site id, the per-client name, and "Alice" < "Bob" decides it.

The rule is arbitrary. It must not be random, and it must not be arrival time, because those differ from machine to machine.

It matters far more than it looks. Delete this one rule and the system diverges in 493 of 500 random runs. That experiment is src/BrokenOt.java, and it is exercise 6.

What does each client keep in its pocket?

Two things: the edit it has sent and not heard back about, and everything it has typed since.

The first is called the outstanding operation. Each client holds at most one of them in flight to the server, ever.

The second is the buffer: operations typed while waiting, in order. When the confirmation for the outstanding operation arrives, the next queued one is sent.

This rule is not a design flourish. It was discovered by failure, and you will watch it be discovered in section 10. With two operations in flight, the client and the server disagree about what each one was written against, and transformation is then applied against the wrong history.

Who decides the official order?

One place, and only one. That place is the sequencer.

A single server owns a given document, and it is the only thing that appends to that document's log. "Concurrent" edits become "ordered" edits the moment they pass through it.

Why does it have to be one? Suppose two servers both accepted edits for one document. There would be two logs and two revision-4s. The sentence "written against revision 4" would stop having a meaning, and every guarantee in this lab is stated over one log.

Section 15 covers what that costs. It is the single most important architectural constraint in the whole design, and most of the production complexity is its bill.

8. Which two mental models should you carry, and where does the story lie?

In one breath: Picture a room with a noticeboard and a clerk who corrects every slip of paper before pinning it up. That picture is right about almost everything, and it lies to you in three specific places.

What does the room look like?

There is a noticeboard at the front of a room. Everyone has their own copy of the list on their own desk.

When you make a change, you write it on a slip of paper. The slip says something like "add an S after the T". You post it to a clerk who stands at the noticeboard.

The clerk does three things with every slip, always in this order:

  1. Looks at what has been pinned up since the slip was written.
  2. Corrects the slip so that it still means the right thing now. "After the T" might have to become "the fifth character".
  3. Pins the corrected slip up, numbers it, and posts a copy of the corrected slip to everybody.

Meanwhile you never wait for the clerk. You made your change on your own desk copy the instant you wrote the slip.

That last sentence is promise 1 from section 5, and it is the reason the clerk has any work to do at all.

What does each machine actually hold?

Now the same room in real names. Each client holds four things.

What the client holds In words
doc its own copy of the text
revision the last server revision it has incorporated
outstanding the one operation sent and not yet confirmed, or none
buffer operations typed since then, in order

The server holds two things: doc, and log, the ordered list of operations. Its revision is simply the log's length.

Where the story lies to you

Three places. Each lie hides a real property of the system, so each one is worth replacing carefully.

The clerk corrects the slip once. In the system, every desk corrects it again. In the room, the clerk's corrected slip is final. In the system, a client that receives that operation may have to transform it a second time, against its own outstanding and buffered edits, which the server has never seen. Transformation happens on both ends, for different reasons.

There is no queue at the noticeboard. Slips do not politely line up. They cross in the post, arrive out of order relative to other people's slips, get lost, and sometimes arrive twice. The clerk sees whatever arrives, in whatever order it arrives.

Everyone posting to one clerk is a design constraint, not a convenience. In a real room, a second clerk would obviously help. In this system, a second clerk for the same noticeboard destroys the design. Section 11 shows why, under F8, and it is why the whole architecture is shaped around one document with one owner.

10. How does the mechanism get invented, one broken design at a time?

In one breath: The finished protocol has four rules that look arbitrary until you watch each one get invented. So we build it wrong four times, and each time the breakage hands us the next rule.

v1 — what if the server does the rewriting?

The obvious home for transformation is the server. It is the one machine that knows the official order, so let it correct every slip.

Rule 1. Every edit is stamped with the revision it was written against. When the server receives an edit, it rewrites it against every log entry from that revision onwards, then appends it, numbers it, and broadcasts the rewritten version to everyone.

Here is that rule doing its job. The document is HAT, the log is empty, and the revision is 0. Read the last two columns as the server's own memory after each step.

step what happens server log server doc
1 Alice types C at 0. Her screen: CHAT. She sends insert(0,'C'), based on revision 0. — HAT
2 Bob types S at 3. His screen: HATS. He sends insert(3,'S'), based on revision 0. — HAT
3 Alice's edit arrives first. Nothing logged since revision 0, so no rewriting. Logged as revision 1. r1: insert(0,'C') CHAT
4 Bob's edit arrives, based on revision 0. The log has moved to 1, so it is rewritten against r1: something was inserted at 0, which is before 3, so insert(3,'S') becomes insert(4,'S'). Logged as revision 2. r1, r2: insert(4,'S') CHATS
5 Everyone is sent the rewritten insert(4,'S') and Alice's insert(0,'C'). CHATS

Both screens end at CHATS, which is the answer everybody wanted back in section 2. Run it yourself with java src/Demo.java, third block.

So we are done? Change one thing: the order in which the two edits reach the server.

The server broadcasts Bob's insert(3,'S') untouched — because when it processed that edit, Alice's C did not exist anywhere it could see. Reveal CHAST — the exact corruption from section 4, back again. The server did nothing wrong: it correctly rewrote Bob's edit against everything it knew, and it did not know about Alice's C. The missing piece is that Alice knows something the server does not — her own un-confirmed edit. Nobody but Alice can do that rewriting.

If you guessed CHATS, you assumed the server could see Alice's screen. It cannot, and promise 1 is the reason: her C was drawn before anyone else heard about it.

That reveal is worth pausing on. Promise 1 is what created the gap. Alice's screen contains a letter that exists nowhere else in the world, so Alice's screen is the only place that particular correction can be made.

v2 — so the client rewrites too, in both directions

Rule 2. When an edit arrives from the server, the client transforms it against its own outstanding and buffered edits — and transforms those against it. Both sides move.

That second half is easy to miss, so say it slowly. Alice's incoming insert(3,'S') becomes insert(4,'S') because of her C. Her own C, once it reaches the server, will need to have been transformed against Bob's S.

v3 — one edit in flight, everything else queues

The stamp "written against revision r" is only true under one condition. The client's document must be exactly the log up to r, plus at most one un-confirmed edit that the server is about to see.

Two edits in flight breaks that arithmetic, which is what you just watched.

Rule 3. A client keeps at most one edit in flight. Anything typed while waiting goes into a local buffer and is sent when the confirmation arrives — after being transformed against everything that arrived in the meantime.

This is why the visualization shows "waiting for confirmation: 1" and "queued behind it: 3". Typing fast does not send faster. It makes the queue longer.

Real editors turn that into an advantage. They compose the queue into one bigger operation before sending it, which is why a burst of typing arrives as one message rather than twenty.

One case is still undecided, and it is the one that looks harmless.

🔮 Predict: Alice and Bob both type at exactly the same position — Alice an S at position 3 of HAT, Bob a ! at position 3. Neither is "before" the other in any meaningful sense. What has to be decided, and what happens if each machine decides it for itself?

Reveal Somebody's character has to come first, and every machine must pick the same somebody. If each machine simply keeps its own edit where it is, Alice ends at HAT!S and Bob at HATS! — forever. Run java src/BrokenOt.java: that one missing rule diverges in 493 of 500 random runs. The fix is one line: order concurrent inserts at the same position by site id. It is arbitrary — Alice's letter goes first because "Alice" < "Bob" — but it is identically arbitrary everywhere.

If you guessed "whoever reached the server first wins", look at what each machine can see. Alice's laptop never learns the arrival order of its own un-confirmed edit, so it cannot use one.

Rule 4. Concurrent inserts at the same position are ordered by a tie-break. That tie-break is a fixed function of the operations themselves. Never arrival time, and never a random number.

11. Which nine failures should you be able to name?

In one breath: Nine things go wrong. Four of them turn out to be nothing much, which is the design working. The other five are where the real engineering is, and two of them corrupt the document silently.

Every one of these is runnable, except the last two. Each is told the same way: what happens to Alice, Bob or Carol, what you would see, why it happens, and which of the four promises from section 5 bends.

F1 — what if the edit never reaches the server?

java src/Experiments.java lost-request

Alice types C, and the network eats the message. She keeps typing, and her queue grows. The experiment prints Alice pending edits: 2.

Her screen is fine and self-consistent. The server and Bob simply never hear from her.

Convergence is not violated. It is postponed. Nothing is corrupted, because a client that never gets a confirmation never advances its revision and never sends its queue. The design is stuck rather than wrong, which is the good kind of broken.

The fix is a timeout and a retry, which the experiment then runs: Alice heard nothing back and re-sent insert('C' at 0), and everything converges to CHHATS.

In production, this same event happens on every laptop that closes its lid.

F2 — what if the edit arrives but the confirmation is lost?

java src/Experiments.java lost-ack

This is the dangerous one, and it is the reason retries are not free.

🔮 Predict: Alice's edit reached the server and was applied. Only the confirmation was lost, so Alice re-sends the same keystroke. What is in the document now, and is it the same on every screen?

Reveal Her single keystroke happened twice, and the screens disagree about it. The run is printed below, with duplicate suppression off and then on. If you guessed the retry was harmless because the network lost something, notice what the server lost: nothing at all. From the server's side, "Alice typed C" and "that C again" are the same bytes. 
duplicate suppression OFF : server="CCHAT"  Alice="CHAT"  Bob="CCHAT"   converged=false
duplicate suppression ON  : server="CHAT"   Alice="CHAT"  Bob="CHAT"    converged=true

The fix has three parts, and all three are needed. Each client numbers its edits. The server remembers the last number it saw from each client, and ignores anything at or below it. Then the server re-sends the original confirmation, because the same edit must not get a second revision number.

That is an idempotency key in its smallest possible form. You have met the same mechanism in the transactional-outbox and saga labs, where at-least-once delivery forces every consumer to be idempotent. Same physics, different content.

F3 — what if messages arrive out of order?

You caused this one yourself in section 10. java src/Experiments.java out-of-order throws insert at 4 but doc is only 3 long.

Here is the distinction worth carrying out of this lab. The guarantee the protocol assumes is per-client ordered delivery. The guarantee it provides is convergence, and it provides that only while the assumption holds.

Two consequences follow directly. The transport is one persistent ordered connection per client. And reconnect logic must resume from a revision number, never from "start listening again".

F4 — what if a client goes away and comes back?

java src/Experiments.java offline-burst

Bob edits on a plane while 200 edits land without him. Read the third row: it is the price of the healing, not the size of the document.

   server log length      : 220
   Bob's offline edits    : 20

   converged              : true, document length 223

Nobody was asked to resolve anything. Nobody saw a conflict prompt. But look at the cost, which is roughly (edits you missed) × (edits you made).

Twenty edits against two hundred is 8,200 transformations, which is nothing. Twenty edits against a million is not nothing. The guarantee holds. The liveness does not scale.

That is why real clients that have been away too long throw their local state away and re-download the document. It is also why servers keep a bounded log tail plus snapshots, which section 15 builds properly.

F5 — what if the server confirms an edit it has not written down?

java src/Experiments.java stale-server

   before the crash: server="CHATS!?" ... revision 4
   after the restart: server="CHATS" revision 2 but Alice still believes she is at revision 4
   server="CXHATS"  Alice="CXHATS!?"  Bob="CXHATS!?"   converged=false

Follow what happened. The server acknowledged revisions 3 and 4 while they were still only in memory. Then it restarted from a snapshot that had two entries.

Now every client's revision number refers to a history that no longer exists. Their edits are transformed against the wrong set of operations. The corruption is silent, and it is permanent.

The rule that prevents it: never confirm an edit you have not durably written down. A confirmation is a promise about the log, so the log write has to come first.

You have arrived at this rule from three other directions already. The WhatsApp lab reached it as "never acknowledge what is not yet durable". It is the same discipline as a write-ahead log in the b-tree/LSM lab, and as appendfsync in the redis lab. "We accepted it" and "it survives a crash" are two different moments. Users forgive you for conflating them only until the first crash.

F6 — what does one slow client cost everybody else?

Carol joins the document from a café with terrible Wi-Fi. Her connection stalls.

java src/Experiments.java slow-client

   edits made while Carol was stalled : 100
   messages queued for Carol alone    : 100

Everyone else keeps working. The design has no shared lock, so one slow participant cannot block anybody. Promise 4 holds even for Carol's neighbours.

But somebody is holding Carol's backlog, and that somebody is the server. Put 200 people in a document, each on their own flaky café Wi-Fi, and that is 200 backlogs in one process's heap.

Production answers with a cap on the per-client queue. When it overflows, the server disconnects that client and makes them reload the document from a snapshot. That is backpressure, from the io-multiplexing and communication-protocols labs, in its document-shaped form.

F7 — what happens when everybody edits the same word?

A hundred people in one document, all typing in the same paragraph, is not a correctness problem. The tie-break rule handles it exactly as it handles two.

It is a hot key problem, and this is the shape a hot key takes here: one document, one owning process, one CPU.

You cannot shard a document by user, because every edit must pass through the same sequencer. That is the constraint from section 7 collecting its bill.

Google's answer is a hard limit. Docs stops accepting new editors past a documented cap, and read-only viewers are served from a different path altogether. The general lesson is the one from the distributed-rate-limiting lab: sharding spreads keys, and it does nothing at all for one hot key.

F8 — what if two servers own one document?

This is the one failure the lab does not simulate, because the fix is architectural rather than algorithmic.

If two servers both accept edits for the same document, there are two logs and two revision-4s. The phrase "written against revision 4" stops having a meaning. Every guarantee in section 12 is stated over one log.

So the document's owning process must be unique. That turns out to be the central architectural constraint of the whole system. Section 15 pays for it, with routing by document id, sticky sessions, careful handover on deploy, and a fencing mechanism.

The fencing mechanism deserves its own sentence. A server that thinks it still owns a document, but has been replaced, must not be able to keep appending.

You met that exact hazard in the redis lab with expiring locks, and it needs the same answer here. A fencing token, checked by the thing being written to, not by the thing that believes it holds the lease.

F9 — which conflict can nothing fix?

Alice changes "shall" to "must". Bob deletes the sentence.

The system converges on a sentence that does not exist, containing an edit that no longer means anything. No transformation rule can help. The conflict is in the meaning, not in the positions.

This is the honest limit of the whole approach. The product answer is social rather than technical: you can see each other's cursors, so you do not type in the same sentence.

12. What is guaranteed, what is not, and what did it cost?

In one breath: Five things are true no matter how the network behaves. Five things are explicitly not promised, and one of them surprises people. Four costs were paid to get here, and you can see all four in the architecture.

What is true, always?

These five hold whatever order messages arrive in, and whoever is offline.

One order. There is exactly one log per document, and its order is the definition of what happened. Everything else is derived from it.

Convergence. Once the network is quiet, every replica's text equals the log replayed from the start. The test suite runs 3 clients making 30 edits each, over 1,000 different random delivery schedules, and finds 0 divergences (java src/Tests.java). The naive design, under the identical harness, diverges in 500 of 500 runs (java src/Experiments.java raw-vs-ot).

Intention preservation, for the operations we support. An insert lands between the same two characters its author saw. A delete removes the same character its author saw, or nothing at all if it is already gone. This is not a theorem here. It is a design property that the tests check case by case.

Local responsiveness. No operation ever waits for a network round trip before appearing on its author's screen. That is promise 1, and it is the one that never bends.

At most one edit in flight per client. That is what makes the revision stamps arithmetically true, and it is Rule 3 restated as an invariant.

Those two numbers deserve to sit next to each other, because the gap between them is the whole argument for this lab.

Design Divergences Out of What the number means

What is explicitly not guaranteed?

Any particular final text when two people type at the same spot. You get a deterministic answer, ordered by site id. You do not get the "right" one, and there is no right one.

Meaningful merges. That is F9. Positions merge. Meaning does not.

Real-time ordering. "Alice typed first" by wall clock has no standing here. The log order is the arrival order at the server, which is a race, and the system is correct either way. If you need wall-clock fairness, this is the wrong architecture.

Undo as the inverse of your last operation. Once other people's edits have been transformed into your document, "undo my last edit" is a fresh operation that must itself be transformed. Selective undo in a collaborative editor is a research-grade problem, and real editors implement a restricted version of it.

Availability during a partition. The document has exactly one owner. If you cannot reach it, you edit locally and cannot see anyone else. That is the CAP trade made per document: CP for the shared document, and AP for your own copy. That combination is only possible because the read everyone cares about is their own screen.

What did it cost to get here?

Four bills, and every one of them shows up again in section 15.

A single point of ordering per document. Simple, and the source of F7, F8 and every deployment complication in the production section.

Transform work proportional to how far behind you are. That is F4's 8,200 transformations, and it is why snapshots and forced reloads exist.

Every client needs the algorithm. The rewriting is not a server feature. It is a protocol, so browser, Android, iOS and the export pipeline must all agree, forever, including for documents written years ago.

A case matrix that grows as the square of the number of operation types. Two types here means four cases. Add "apply bold to a range" and "insert a table", and the matrix is where real OT implementations earn their reputation for being hard. This is the single strongest argument for the alternative in section 15.

Which lines actually carry the design?

Everything load-bearing is in transform. Here is the case where both people insert, which is Alice's C meeting Bob's S:

if (a.kind == Kind.INSERT && b.kind == Kind.INSERT) {
    if (b.pos < a.pos) return a.withPos(a.pos + 1);   // something appeared before me → shift right
    if (b.pos > a.pos) return a;                      // it appeared after me → I am unaffected
    // Exact same spot. Somebody has to go first, and BOTH sides must pick the same
    // somebody or the two copies end up with the letters in opposite orders.
    return a.site.compareTo(b.site) < 0 ? a : a.withPos(a.pos + 1);
}

Three lines, three situations: before me, after me, and the dead heat that needs Rule 4.

Now the case people forget, where both people delete:

if (b.pos < a.pos) return a.withPos(a.pos - 1);
if (b.pos > a.pos) return a;
// We both deleted the SAME character. It is already gone; deleting again
// would eat an innocent neighbour. My op becomes nothing at all.
return Op.noop(a.site);

That noop is not tidiness. Without it, two people deleting the same letter of CHATS produce CHA. The T disappears as well as the S, because the second delete removes whatever slid into the vacant position.

The test named ot_two_people_delete_the_same_character is what catches that.

On the client, four lines implement Rule 2, and they are worth reading slowly:

if (outstanding != null) {
    Ot.Op newOutstanding = xform.apply(outstanding, incoming);
    incoming = xform.apply(incoming, outstanding);
    outstanding = newOutstanding;
}

Note the temporary variable. Transforming incoming first, then using the new incoming to transform outstanding, would transform against an operation that never existed in that form. Both results must be computed from the originals.

That is the smallest bug in this lab and one of the hardest to see.

What does each piece actually do?

The browser editor holds the document model, the view, the local undo stack, and the client half of section 10. That is outstanding, buffer and transform, exactly as Alice's laptop held them. It composes rapid keystrokes into one operation before sending, typically one message per 50 to 200 ms of typing rather than one per key.

The edge layer terminates TLS (tls lab) and holds one long-lived WebSocket per open tab. It is the same connection-server tier you met in the WhatsApp lab: sockets, no durable state. Its scaling problem is idle connections, which is the io-multiplexing lab exactly. A million connections that are silent 95% of the time is that lab's whole subject.

The session router maps a document id to the one process that owns it right now. That is the placement problem from the consistent-hashing lab, with one extra requirement: ownership here is exclusive, not merely a preference.

You get exclusivity from a lease in a strongly consistent store, of the etcd/Chubby/Spanner class, with Paxos underneath. Two properties matter. Only one owner at a time, and a fencing token so that a replaced owner's writes are rejected by the store rather than merely discouraged. F8 is the failure this prevents.

The document session server holds the document in memory, appends to the log, transforms, broadcasts, and tracks who is connected and where their cursors are. Everything is per-document and single-threaded per document, which makes the whole section 10 algorithm race-free without a lock.

That is the trick the redis lab taught. The fastest way to make concurrency correct is to not have any.

The document store holds the log and periodic snapshots. This is the storage pattern from the event-sourcing lab, for the same reason. Replaying a million operations to open a document would be absurd, so you fold them into a snapshot and keep only the tail.

Version history is free here, because the log is the history. "Restore to yesterday 4pm" is a position in it. That is also why the log is trimmed by snapshotting rather than by deletion.

The metadata store holds the title, the owner, the sharing rules and the folder location. It has a different access shape, queried by user rather than by document, so it is usually a different store from the document content. The search index is projected off it, which makes it a CQRS read model.

The client receives {text, revision}, then opens a WebSocket. Every later message is a delta, which is why the connection is worth its cost.

Typing. That is section 10, with one addition. The server writes the operation to durable storage before confirming it, batching writes across operations for throughput. F5 is what the addition buys.

Reconnecting. The client says "I am at revision 812". If the server still has 812 in its log tail, it replays 813 onwards. If not, because the client slept for a week or a snapshot has moved past it, the answer is "your revision is too old". The client then discards its local state and re-opens. That is F4's cost turned into a product decision.

What is the other design, and why did Google not pick it?

There is a second answer to the same problem. It is worth knowing precisely, because it changes the architecture rather than only the algorithm.

A CRDT, short for conflict-free replicated data type, gives every character a permanent identity at the moment it is typed. The identity is a counter plus a site id. An edit then refers to that identity instead of a position: "put 'C' after the character named 3@Alice".

Identities never shift. So there is nothing to rewrite, and therefore no need for a central server to order anything. The CRDT lab builds this properly, including the sequence type that a text editor needs.

Read the last two lines together. A hundred visible characters cost a thousand stored cells, because 900 of them are tombstones: deleted characters that must be kept so later operations can still refer to them.

🔮 Predict: Same product, but offline-first on a phone, and a user has been away for a week. Which design would you choose, and what is the bill?

Reveal The CRDT, and the bill is memory. OT has to transform a week of your edits against a million operations you missed, which is F4's cost with the numbers turned up until it stops being trivial. A CRDT merges them with no replay at all. If you guessed OT because it is what Google Docs uses, notice the difference in the requirement: Docs assumes you are online and connected to the one owner, and the offline-first product assumes you are not. Exercise 8.2 is exactly this argument.

The trade, one line each:

operational transformation CRDT
needs a central sequencer yes — one owner per document no; peer-to-peer works

what it is used by Google Docs, Google Wave, ShareDB, Etherpad Yjs, Automerge, Figma's multiplayer, Apple Notes, Redis CRDTs

Note what the CRDT does not remove. It still needs causal delivery, because an insert whose anchor has not arrived must wait, and that buffering is in Crdt.java. A hosted product also still puts a server in the middle, for persistence, access control and fan-out.

What it removes is the requirement that the server be the only place ordering is decided. That is why offline-first and peer-to-peer products reach for it.

So why does Google Docs use OT? Two reasons, and neither is about elegance.

History: Docs predates practical sequence CRDTs by years. Cost: a document that costs 30× its text in memory is a real bill at their scale, and they already had to run a central server for sharing and persistence. The sequencer was free.

What is this commonly confused with?

Five pairs, each separated by one sentence.

Operational transformation against event sourcing. Both are append-only logs of changes. Event sourcing replays operations that were written against the state they are replayed onto; OT replays operations that were written against a different state, which is why it needs transform and event sourcing does not.

Convergence against consistency. Convergence says every copy ends up the same. It says nothing about when, or about what any single read returns, so a system can converge and still show you stale text for a second.

OT against CRDT. Both guarantee convergence. OT rewrites operations and needs a single ordering authority; a CRDT makes operations commute by giving every character an identity, and needs none.

Collaborative editing against multiplayer game state. Games mostly need the latest value of each thing, so last-writer-wins is often fine. Documents need every intermediate operation preserved, because the text is the accumulation of them.

A lost update against a conflict. A lost update is silent and unrecoverable; a conflict is at least visible. Design 1's failure is the worse kind.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Something like: "When we email the file, each person edits their own copy. When two copies come back, somebody has to pick one — and whatever was only in the other one disappears. Nobody gets an error; the change is just gone. Google Docs avoids that by never having two copies: everyone types into the same document, and each keystroke is sent as its own small change, so two people's changes can both be kept instead of one replacing the other."

1.2 Because an edit is written against a particular state of the document, and by the time it is applied somewhere else that state has changed. "Insert S at position 3" was correct when Bob typed it into HAT and wrong when Alice ran it against CHAT. Order alone does not fix it: applying edits in a consistent order still applies the wrong edits. What is needed is to rewrite each edit for the state it is actually landing on.

1.3 Because a network round trip is 50–300 ms and you would feel it on every single letter — the product would be unusable. Applying locally first means each person's screen temporarily contains edits nobody else has seen, which is precisely why a client must transform incoming edits against its own un-confirmed ones (§10, Rule 2). The whole difficulty of the design is the price of that one product decision.

1.4 Convergence: when everything has been delivered, every copy shows the same text. Intention preservation: each edit still does what its author meant. A system with the first and not the second: "when two edits conflict, blank the document." Perfectly convergent, useless. A subtler one: last-writer-wins on the whole document — every copy agrees, and one person's work is gone.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 The server panel holds the official order — the numbered log. "Waiting for confirmation: 1" means that client has sent an edit the server has not acknowledged, so its own screen contains something the rest of the world has not seen yet. Bob: insert 'S' at 4 proves the rewriting happened: Bob's finger pressed S at position 3, and the log records the position his edit had to become once Alice's C was in front of it.

2.2 Tie-break: return a.site.compareTo(b.site) < 0 ? a : a.withPos(a.pos + 1); in Ot.transform. One-in-flight: if (outstanding == null) { ... } buffer.addLast(op); in Collab.Client.edit. Retry safety: if (dedup && seq <= lastSeq.getOrDefault(from, 0)) in Collab.Server.receive.

2.3 Any feed line reading "(their own editor rewrote it to …, against their un-confirmed edit)". It needed rewriting twice because two different machines knew two different things: the server knew the operations already in the log, and the receiving client knew its own edits that the server had not yet seen. Neither could do the other's half.

2.4 "Queued behind it" rises; "revision" does not move at all, because revision only advances when the server tells you it has. What is sitting in his browser is one edit that has been sent (or is stuck), plus a queue of edits that exist nowhere else in the world. Close that tab and they are gone.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 !AT on every screen. Alice's delete removes the H; Bob's ! was meant to go after the H, and with the H gone it lands at the front. Both delivery orders give the same answer — verified: whichever edit reaches the server first, server="!AT" Alice="!AT" Bob="!AT".

Note what happened to intention: Bob meant "after the H". There is no "after the H" any more. The system picks the nearest surviving meaning rather than asking anybody, which is the whole product philosophy in one character.

3.2 Eight. Each pending operation costs two transforms — one to rewrite the incoming edit past it, one to rewrite it past the incoming edit — and she has four pending (1 outstanding + 3 buffered). General formula: 2 × (pending count) per incoming edit, and on the server side, one transform per log entry the sender had not seen. That second one is what makes F4 expensive.

3.3 It converges, and nobody sees a conflict prompt — ever. That is the guarantee. What can happen to the meaning is F9: both sets of edits land correctly and the resulting sentence is nonsense that neither person wrote. No algorithm fixes that; visible cursors and presence are the product's answer.

3.4 500 of 500. This is the most useful number in the lab. The naive design is not "risky under load" — it is broken on essentially every run with two active writers. Anything that appears to work in testing is a scheduling accident.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Safety. Progress never stops — every edit is accepted, every message is delivered, the system stays responsive. What breaks is the guarantee that nothing wrong appears: the copies permanently disagree (HAT!S vs HATS!) and no amount of waiting heals it. A liveness bug makes the system stop; this one makes it lie.

4.2 Verified: the test run dies with IllegalStateException: insert at 5 but doc is only 4 long: "HATS" inside ot_typing_a_burst_faster_than_the_round_trip. The message points at the symptom — a position beyond the end of the document — and says nothing about the cause, which is that two edits both claimed to be written against revision 0 while the second was typed on a screen that already contained the first. That gap between symptom and cause is exactly why the rule is worth stating explicitly rather than discovering.

4.3 Nothing changes because no test in the suite ever retries. Duplicate suppression only does anything when a duplicate arrives, and the suite never produces one — the retry paths live in Experiments.java instead. The lesson generalises well beyond this lab: a green suite proves things about the paths it exercises and nothing whatever about the paths it does not, and idempotency is almost always in the second category.

4.4 ot_two_people_delete_the_same_character catches it, and the document becomes CHA instead of CHAS: the second delete, having no idea its target is already gone, removes whatever slid into that position — the innocent S.

Rung 5 — Implement

The four cases:

// 1. insert vs insert
if (b.pos < a.pos) return a.withPos(a.pos + 1);
if (b.pos > a.pos) return a;
return a.site.compareTo(b.site) < 0 ? a : a.withPos(a.pos + 1);

// 2. insert vs delete
return b.pos < a.pos ? a.withPos(a.pos - 1) : a;

// 3. delete vs insert
return b.pos <= a.pos ? a.withPos(a.pos + 1) : a;

// 4. delete vs delete
if (b.pos < a.pos) return a.withPos(a.pos - 1);
if (b.pos > a.pos) return a;
return Ot.Op.noop(a.site);

5.2 The two most commonly wrong are the boundary in case 3 (<=, not < — an insert at the position of the character you are deleting still pushes it right) and the no-op in case 4. Both are invisible in a two-person demo and appear immediately under random schedules, which is why the harness runs 1,000 of them rather than four examples.

Rung 6 — Debug

6.1 ot_same_position_inserts_land_in_the_same_order_everywhere catches it directly, and ot_converges_under_1000_random_schedules catches it in bulk. It violates convergence. It does not violate local responsiveness, and arguably not intention preservation — each person's letter did land where they meant it; the two copies simply disagree about which came first. That is what makes it such a good bug: every individual operation looks correct.

6.2 A crash is a bug that announces itself, in one place, with a stack trace. This one produces two documents that both look like something a person might have typed, on two screens that will never be compared, in a system with no mechanism that ever notices. The users find it days later as a sentence nobody remembers writing — and there is nothing to debug from, because by then the divergence has been built on by hundreds of later edits.

6.3 Verified: the test passes today (random 40-edit run, log of 40 entries, replay equals the server's document). It is worth writing anyway, because it asserts something none of the other ten do — that the server's state and the server's history still agree. Every existing test compares copies to each other, so a bug that corrupted the log and the document in the same way would pass all ten. It is also the property that everything downstream depends on: version history, "restore to yesterday", crash recovery from a snapshot, and the whole of F5 assume the log is the truth.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 Automatic retry

Give Client a counter of deliveries since it last sent, and re-send outstanding — with the same sequence number — when it exceeds a threshold. Two tests:

  • heals a dropped request: drop the ToServer message, let the timeout fire, assert everything converges.
  • does not duplicate on a dropped confirmation: deliver the edit, drop the ToClient confirmation, let the timeout fire, assert the document contains the character once.

The second test only passes if server.dedup is on. That is the real answer to "what must be true of the server": it must be able to tell this again from another one, which needs (a) a per-client sequence number that a retry does not increment, (b) memory of the last number seen per client, and (c) memory of the confirmation it sent, so the retry gets the same revision number back rather than a new one. Without (c) the client's revision jumps to a value that does not describe its document.

Timeouts should be jittered. A server hiccup that stalls 5,000 clients for two seconds will otherwise produce 5,000 simultaneous retries the instant it clears.

7.2 "Your revision is too old"

Keep the last K log entries; on a request based on an older revision, reply with {snapshot text, revision} instead of a replay. The client must throw away everything local: its document, its buffer, and its outstanding edit — which means the user's un-sent typing is discarded. That is a genuine data loss, so in a real product the client would first try to send its queue while it is still within the window, and only reload if that fails.

What the user loses: unsent edits, cursor position, and undo history. What the system gains: an upper bound on the work of joining, which turns F4 from unbounded to O(K). Pick K by the reconnect window you want to support — 30 seconds of typing, not a week's.

7.3 Backpressure

Cap the per-client outbound queue; on overflow, disconnect and require a fresh open (which then hits 7.2's snapshot path).

This breaks promise 2 (convergence) for that one client, temporarily — they are cut off and must reload — in order to protect promises 1 and 2 for everyone else, and to protect the server's memory. It is the right trade because the alternative is unbounded memory growth caused by the worst connection in the room: one person's café Wi-Fi becomes everybody's outage. It is the same call as evicting a slow consumer from a broker, or shedding load rather than queueing it (distributed-rate-limiting).

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 Three datacentres, 80 ms apart

Survives unchanged: the algorithm. Transformation does not care about latency; it cares about ordering, and ordering is decided in one place.

What must move: the document's owning process lives in exactly one region — the one where most of its editors are, decided at open time and re-evaluated rarely. Everyone else's edits pay a cross-region round trip for confirmation only. Their own keystrokes still appear instantly (promise 1 is local by construction); what they feel is other people's letters arriving ~80 ms later, which is barely perceptible in a document and would be fatal in a game.

What must not be attempted: one owner per region with replication between them. That is F8 with a longer cable. Two logs means two meanings for "revision 4", and no amount of replication repairs it after the fact. If you genuinely need multi-region write ownership, you have chosen the CRDT architecture and should say so explicitly rather than arriving there by accident.

Storage: the log must be durable before a confirmation, so a cross-region synchronous write would put 80 ms on every keystroke's confirmation. Realistic answer: durable in-region with fast local replication, asynchronously replicated across regions, and accept that a region loss can lose the last few operations of documents it owned. State that trade out loud; it is the CAP choice, per document.

Monitor: confirmation latency split by client region vs owner region — one number hides the whole problem. Also ownership migrations per minute, and cross-region replication lag.

8.2 Offline-first on a phone

Choose a CRDT. A week of offline edits against a log that has moved a million operations is exactly the case where OT's replay cost (F4) and the "your revision is too old" fallback (7.2) both fail: you cannot ask the user to discard a week of work, and you cannot cheaply transform against a million operations.

The strongest argument against: memory and storage. Every character carries an id and a parent, and every deletion leaves a tombstone (java src/Crdt.java: 1,000 cells for 100 visible characters). On a phone, for a document edited daily for years, that needs real garbage collection — which is only safe once you know every replica has seen the deletion, which means tracking every replica, which is its own distributed problem.

Storage: the local device holds the full CRDT state, not a text snapshot, because operations must be applicable against it. Sync becomes "exchange the operations you have that I don't" rather than "replay the log from revision N".

Version history: no longer free. There is no single ordered log to point at, so "yesterday 4 pm" has to be materialised deliberately — periodic named snapshots, or a separately maintained server-side ordering.

8.3 Two hundred editors in one document

First thing to fall over: fan-out, in the owning process. Every accepted edit is broadcast to 199 others, so 200 people typing at 5 operations/second is 1,000 in and ~200,000 out per second, from one process, with per-client queues (F6) for every one of them.

Mitigations, in order:

  1. Batch broadcasts — send accumulated operations every 50–100 ms instead of one message per operation. Cuts message count by an order of magnitude and costs 50 ms of perceived latency, which nobody notices in a document.
  2. Split the roles — most of 200 people are reading. Serve read-only viewers from a replicated stream that is allowed to lag; only actual editors hold a session on the owner.
  3. Fan-out tier — the owning process broadcasts to a small number of relay processes, each of which serves a subset of clients. The sequencer stays single; only the shouting is distributed.
  4. A hard cap, honestly communicated in the product.

Also worth doing at twenty editors: batching (1) and per-client queue caps — they are pure wins. (2) and (3) are scale hacks that add moving parts and should wait until measurements demand them.

8.4 A Figma-style canvas

Rules that survive: all four, in spirit. One owner per file, edits stamped with a revision, transform on both ends, deterministic tie-break.

What changes: the operation set. Objects have identity — object 17 — so most edits are set(object 17, x, 340) rather than positional. That makes the common conflicts easier: two people moving different shapes never interact at all, and two people moving the same shape is a last-writer-wins on one field, which is a legitimate answer for a position in a way it never is for text.

What gets harder: structural relationships. Z-order is an ordered sequence, so it has the text problem again. Grouping, parenting and "move this shape into that frame" can produce cycles or orphans when two people restructure at once, and no positional rule saves you — you need explicit rules like "a delete of a group wins over a move into it" or a CRDT tree with conflict rules.

Does it change the OT/CRDT answer? Yes. Per-object last-writer-wins registers plus a sequence CRDT for z-order is a natural, small CRDT design with no positional transformation at all — which is why Figma's multiplayer is built that way rather than as text-style OT. The text case is the one where OT's efficiency really pays.

If the effect is committed and the record is not, the retry does it twice; that is F5 and the transactional-outbox lab's dual-write problem, in a third costume.
