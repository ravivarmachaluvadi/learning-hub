Lab 40 · Copies and Agreement

Raft — how five people keep one order book

Tell a friend: Ana, Ben, Cara, Dev and Eve run a pizza shop, and each keeps an order book. All five books must say the same orders in the same numbered slots, even though people vanish mid-shift and shouts across the kitchen get lost. Raft's trick is to put one person in charge for a numbered stretch of time, let only that person write, and call an order "settled" the moment most of the five have copied it down. The only people allowed to take charge next are those whose book already holds everything that was settled, which is why a settled order can never be lost. Every rule in Raft that looks odd is a scar from one specific way a simpler version of the shop broke.

The interactive version is The Raft Log Grid, also saved as visualization.html. How to run the Java, where this lab sits among your other labs, and how the numbers were made are all in section 19 at the end.

BEGINNER MODEL

1. Why does this thing exist?

In one breath: Anything important is kept on more than one machine, because one machine dies. But copies can disagree about what happened and in what order, and nothing rings an alarm when they do.

Anything important lives on more than one machine. Why? Because one machine dies. The moment you have copies, you have a new problem, and it is much nastier than losing a machine. The copies can disagree about what happened, and about what happened first.

Here is the shape of it. A balance of £100 has two changes in flight: "add £50" and "take 20%". Apply them in one order and you get £120. Apply them the other way and you get £130. Now imagine two copies of your database apply them in different orders. Both copies are internally consistent. Both look healthy. And they disagree by £10 forever. Nothing crashes. No alarm fires. You discover it when a customer does.

Order the changes were applied Balance What it means
add £50, then take 20% £120 one copy's honest answer
take 20%, then add £50 £130 the other copy's honest answer
each copy picked a different order a £10 gap, forever neither copy is "wrong" on its own; the group is

Raft exists so that a group of machines can agree, permanently, on one ordered list of changes. It keeps that agreement while machines crash and restart. It keeps it while the network loses, delays, duplicates and reorders messages. Every system that has to keep exactly one answer has one of these underneath it: which node owns which shard, who holds the lock, what the current config is, where the leader lives.

2. What is the problem? The pizza shop

In one breath: Five people take phone orders and each keeps an order book. All five books must end up with the same orders in the same numbered slots, even though people leave without warning and shouts get lost.

A pizza shop takes orders by phone. Five people work there: Ana, Ben, Cara, Dev and Eve. Each keeps their own order book. Every book has to end up with the same orders written in the same numbered slots. Why so strict? Because the orders refer to each other.

Slot What is written Why the order matters
1 "table 4 wants a margherita" the pizza everything else is about
2 "table 4 — make that extra cheese" changes slot 1
3 "table 4 — cancel" undoes slots 1 and 2

Apply those in a different order and you either deliver a pizza nobody wanted or throw away one someone is waiting for.

The awkward parts:

  • People leave without warning. Ana's shift ends. Ben's phone dies mid-call.
  • Nobody can see anyone else's book. They can only shout messages across a noisy kitchen. Shouts get lost, arrive late, or arrive twice.
  • You cannot wait for everyone. Suppose the rule is "write it down only when all five have confirmed". Then one person in the walk-in freezer stops the shop.

The job: get all five books to hold the same orders in the same slots, forever, even though people vanish mid-sentence.

🔮 Predict: Why is "just have everyone write down whatever they hear" not good enough?

Reveal

Because two phones can ring at the same time. Ana hears "margherita" and Cara hears "pepperoni", and both write into slot 1. Neither did anything wrong. Their books now differ at slot 1 and at every slot after it. And there is no rule anywhere that says which one was "really" first. In a system with no shared clock there is no such thing as really first.

If you said "whoever picked up the phone first wins", ask: first by whose watch? Ana's kitchen clock and Cara's phone clock disagree, and nobody can prove which one rang first.

3. Why not just have everyone write down what they hear?

In one breath: The obvious design has no coordination at all. Whoever answers the phone writes the order in the next free slot and shouts it. It is simple and fast, and it is about to break.

The naive solution, and the one that needs no rules: whoever answers the phone writes the order in the next free slot and shouts it to the other four. The other four copy it into the same slot.

It is simple. It needs no coordination. It is fast, because nobody waits for anybody. This is not a stupid design. So what is wrong with it?

4. What goes wrong when two phones ring at once?

In one breath: Two people fill slot 1 with different orders at the same moment. Now every later slot means different things in different books. And the obvious patch, "ask first", fails too.

Ana takes "margherita" and writes it in slot 1. At the same moment Cara takes "pepperoni" and writes it in slot 1. Each shouts. Ben hears Ana first and writes margherita. Dev hears Cara first and writes pepperoni.

Now the shop has two different slot 1s and no way to pick. Worse, everything after slot 1 is built on top of a slot that means different things to different people. "Table 4, extra cheese" in slot 2 modifies a different pizza depending on whose book you read.

Here is the shop one second after the two phones rang. Read the last column: it is what each person honestly believes.

Book Slot 1 What that person believes
Ana margherita table 4 gets a margherita
Cara pepperoni table 4 gets a pepperoni
Ben margherita Ana's shout arrived first
Dev pepperoni Cara's shout arrived first
Eve whichever shout arrived first a coin toss

🔮 Predict: The obvious patch: before writing, ask the other four "is slot 1 free?" and only write if they all say yes. Does that fix it?

Reveal

No, and it fails in two ways at once. If Ana and Cara ask at the same moment, both can hear "free" from everyone, and both write. And if two people are away, you never get four answers. So the shop stops even though three perfectly good people are standing there.

If you said "well, it fixes the first problem at least", look again. Asking is just another shout. Two questions can cross in the air exactly like the two orders did.

The deeper problem this failure reveals. This is not really about who writes slot 1. A group with no shared clock has no notion of "first". And any rule that needs everyone to answer is hostage to its slowest member. You cannot patch your way out. You need a rule that manufactures an ordering, and that only ever needs most of the group.

5. What do we actually need?

In one breath: Five plain promises. One order per slot, the same order everywhere, keep working with two people missing, catch up automatically, and refuse rather than guess.

Say it in plain words, because everything after this is the pursuit of exactly these five sentences. This is the real requirement:

  1. One order per slot, forever. Once anyone has been told "slot 3 is settled", no book may ever hold anything else at slot 3.
  2. Same order everywhere. Any two books that both have slot 3 filled in agree on slots 1 and 2 as well.
  3. A minority can be away. The shop keeps working with two of five people missing.
  4. Coming back is automatic. Someone who returns after an hour catches up with no human involved.
  5. Being wrong is never allowed; being stuck is allowed. If too many people are away, the shop must refuse orders rather than guess.

Point 5 is the trade, and it is worth stopping on. You already met it in the cap-theorem lab as the CP corner. When the group is split, the side that cannot reach a majority refuses to answer instead of answering possibly-wrong. Raft is that choice made concrete. Ana would rather send a customer away than promise a pizza the shop might not agree on.

6. What is the key idea?

In one breath: Put one person in charge for a while. Only they write. An order is settled when most people have copied it. And you may only take charge next if your book is already as complete as most people's.

Here is the invention, in four sentences:

Elect one person to be in charge for a while. Only that person writes into the books, so slots have exactly one meaning. An order counts as settled the moment most people have copied it down. And you are only allowed to become the next person in charge if your book is at least as complete as most people's.

That is the whole of Raft. Every remaining page is either an explanation of one of those sentences, or a repair to a version of one of them that broke.

The last sentence is the clever one and the easiest to skip past. Hold onto it: being in charge is a privilege you have to qualify for by already knowing everything that was settled. Eve cannot take over the shop just because she shouted loudest. She has to show that her book is as complete as most people's first.

7. Which words do you need now?

In one breath: Every Raft word is a thing in the shop you have already seen: the book, its slots, the shift, the person in charge, "most of us", and "settled". Here each one is earned in the story, then collected in two small tables.

You have met two of these ideas before, in other labs. The rest are new. This is the lab's terminology, but each word arrives the same way: the thing in the shop first, then a plain sentence, then the engineer's name for it.

The order book, its lines, and the line numbers

Ana's order book is a numbered list of changes. Engineers call the whole book the log. One line in it is an entry: a command plus the shift it was written in. The line number is its index, counting from 1. The log is append-only: you write at the end, never in the middle.

Why a separate word for the book, rather than "the pizzas"? To separate the record of changes from the result of applying them. Two servers may hold the same log and be part-way through applying it. The log is the truth; the result is a replay of the log. This is exactly the event stream from event-sourcing: an immutable append-only sequence folded into current state. Raft is that idea with the replication problem solved.

Here is Ana's book at one moment, in the shop's words and in the code's notation. Read the "in words" column first.

Ana's book In the code In words
slot 1: margherita, written in shift 1 margherita@t1 index 1 holds margherita, created while term 1 was in force
slot 2: extra cheese, written in shift 1 extracheese@t1 index 2 holds extracheese, created while term 1 was in force
slot 3: cancel, written in shift 2 cancel@t2 index 3 holds cancel, created in term 2
the whole book [margherita@t1, extracheese@t1, cancel@t2] Ana's log, oldest entry first

The pizzas: what the book is instructions for

The book is not the pizzas. The book is a list of instructions; the pizzas are what you get by following it. Engineers call the thing that follows the instructions the state machine. It is a deterministic program fed the committed entries in order. Same log, same starting point, same result, on every server.

This is why Raft is formally a replicated state machine protocol. It does not replicate data. It replicates the sequence of changes, and each server derives its own copy. Why bother with the distinction? It makes the correctness argument tiny. If the state machine is deterministic, then agreeing on the log is sufficient. You never have to compare the resulting states at all.

The log Applied to Result on every server
[set x=1, set y=2, set x=3] an empty map {x:3, y:2}, always

That is the fold in event-sourcing. Identical idea. Raft supplies the part that lab assumed: getting every replica the same stream.

The shift: "whose turn is it to be in charge?"

The shop runs in shifts. Each shift has a number, and at most one person is in charge during it. Engineers call a shift a term. A term is a whole number that only ever goes up. Every attempt to become leader starts a new term. Every message carries the sender's term. And a bigger term always wins: any server that sees one immediately becomes a follower and adopts it.

Watch Cara do it. Cara is on term 3 and receives a message stamped term 5. She does not argue. She does not check anything else. She sets her term to 5 and becomes a follower.

Why does the word exist? Because a term is a logical clock that costs one integer. Distributed systems cannot order events by wall clock. But they can order them by "which leadership attempt was this part of". Term comparison replaces time comparison everywhere in Raft.

You met the same job in paxos as the proposal number: a total order over attempts. Raft spends it on shifts rather than on individual values. That is why one term covers thousands of writes.

The three things a person can be

At any moment each of the five is one of three things.

  • A follower is the default. Followers answer messages and never start anything.
  • The leader is the only server that accepts client writes.
  • A candidate is a follower that got bored of waiting and is asking for votes.

Dev has heard nothing for 200 ms. He becomes a candidate for term 4 and asks the other four to vote for him.

Why three states and not two? The middle state exists so that "I am trying to be leader" is a real, visible condition with its own rules. It is not a half-finished leader. A candidate has no authority at all.

"Most of us"

More than half: 3 of 5, 2 of 3, 4 of 7. Engineers call it a majority, or a quorum. You already know why this number. The quorums-and-replication lab is entirely about one fact: any two majorities must share at least one member. That overlap is the load-bearing fact in Raft too, and it is used twice. Once so two people cannot both win the same term. Once so a new leader cannot be missing a settled entry.

That lab drew a distinction, and it matters here. A consensus quorum decides; a read/write quorum merely intersects. Raft's majority is the first kind. The read/write formula from that lab is not what is happening here.

Formula In words Is this Raft?
R+W>N a read set and a write set large enough that they must overlap no; it only guarantees two sets touch
majority vote more than half agree, so any two such groups share a member yes; a majority decides

"Settled, and you can say so out loud"

An order is settled once enough people have written it down that it can never be undone. Engineers say the entry is committed. Precisely: an entry is committed once the leader has stored it on a majority and the entry was created in the leader's own current term.

Why a word for this? To name the exact boundary between "in the leader's book" and "safe to tell the customer". Before it, the entry can legally vanish. After it, no future leader may remove it. Almost every real Raft bug lives at this line.

Each server keeps one number for how far the settled region reaches. In words: the highest index it knows is committed. In the code it is the commit index, commitIndex. Read the table row by row.

The leader's book Its commit index In words
[a, b, c] commitIndex = 2 a and b can never change; c might still disappear

Where does the second half of the rule, "created in the leader's own term", come from? Stage 10.4. It is the least obvious rule in Raft, and it exists because of one specific disaster.

"Is anyone in charge?"

How does Ben know Ana is still there? She keeps shouting "still here!". If Ben hears nothing for a while, he assumes she is gone and asks to take over.

The wait is the election timeout: how long a follower waits, hearing nothing, before declaring itself a candidate. It is randomised per server, per attempt. The shout is the heartbeat: an empty write the leader sends on a fixed interval, purely so followers keep resetting their timers.

Setting This lab In words
election timeout 150–300 ticks a fresh random wait, picked every time the timer resets
heartbeat every 50 ticks an empty "still here" from the leader

Why do these exist? They are Raft's entire failure detector. There is no health check, no monitor, no separate protocol. Silence is the failure signal, and the heartbeat is how a living leader keeps proving it is alive.

The only two things Raft ever says

Across the whole shop only two kinds of shout exist: "can I take over?" and "write this down". Engineers call them two remote calls, each with a reply. RequestVote runs elections. AppendEntries does everything else: replicating entries, repairing a wrong log, telling followers what is committed, and, with an empty entry list, the heartbeat. That is the entire protocol surface.

Where the story lies to you

Four places. Read this before you trust the shop.

  1. The shop has one room; Raft has no room. Ana cannot see that Cara is holding up her hand. Every fact one server has about another arrived as a message. That message may be minutes old and describe a world that no longer exists. This is why every message carries a term. It is the only way to notice you are acting on stale news.
  2. Nobody hands over. In the shop you imagine Ana saying "Ben, you're in charge". That never happens in Raft. Ana usually does not know she has stopped being leader. Two people genuinely believe they are in charge at the same time, routinely. Raft is fine with that, because the older one can never assemble a majority. "Only one leader" is false. "Only one leader per term, and only the newest can commit" is true.
  3. Copying is not one-directional. A follower whose book is wrong does not politely append. It deletes its own entries to match the leader. The shop analogy makes this feel like vandalism. In Raft it is legal precisely because only uncommitted entries can ever be deleted.
  4. There is no "settled" ceremony. Nobody announces a commit. The leader just notices that a majority replied, and mentions the new commit point on the next ordinary message. Followers find out later, and that lag is normal.

10.1 — Elections, v1: what if the first to notice takes over?

The rule: a follower that has heard nothing for a while declares itself candidate, adds one to the term, votes for itself, and asks everyone else. A majority of votes makes it leader.

Concretely, five servers boot at once with empty logs. Nobody is leader, so nobody sends heartbeats, so eventually somebody's timer fires. Here is the trace from java src/Experiments.java election. Read S0 as Ana, S1 as Ben, S4 as Eve.

t=172   S0 starts an election for term 1 (its log ends at index 0, term 0)
t=173   S4 steps down to follower, term 0 -> 1
t=176   S1 steps down to follower, term 0 -> 1
t=180   S0 WINS the election for term 1 with 3/5 votes [0, 1, 4]

In words: Ana's timer fires first, at tick 172. She asks for votes in term 1. Eve and Ben hear a bigger term and adopt it. Eight ticks later Ana has three of five votes, her own plus Ben's and Eve's, and she is leader.

What the run shows Count What it means

messages delivered 8 enough of them arrived for three votes; the rest were not needed

🔮 Predict: Everyone boots at the same instant and everyone's timer is the same length. What happens?

Reveal

Nobody is ever elected. All five time out on the same tick. All five become candidates for term 1. All five vote for themselves. Each has exactly 1 of 5 votes; nobody reaches 3. They all time out again, together, in term 2. And term 3. Over 6000 ticks with identical timeouts: term 40 reached, leader elected: NEVER, safety violations: 0. The cluster is perfectly correct and completely useless.

If you said "one of them wins eventually", you are assuming something breaks the tie. Nothing does. Same timers, same tick, same result, every term.

The fix: randomise the timeout. Each server picks a fresh random wait in a range (150–300 ticks here) every time it resets. One server almost always wakes noticeably before the others. It wins before anyone else even starts, and its heartbeat resets everybody.

Here is what 200 cold starts look like each way, from java src/Experiments.java split-vote. Read the last column for what each row proves.

identical timeouts random 150–300 What it means
leader elected never, in 6000 ticks 200 / 200 randomness is the only thing breaking the tie
terms burned 40 and climbing 1 in 199 runs, 2 in 1 run one collision in 200, and it healed itself
time to first leader — 155 / 174 / 332 ticks (min/median/max) roughly one timeout plus one exchange
safety violations 0 0 the broken cluster was never wrong; it was only stuck

Note what randomisation does not do. It does not make split votes impossible. It makes them self-correcting. Each retry re-rolls the dice, so the chance of n consecutive collisions vanishes. One of the 200 runs did split, and resolved on the next term.

This is your first sighting of the distinction that runs through the rest of the lesson. The identical-timeout cluster never did anything wrong. It just never did anything. Safety and liveness fail in completely different ways.

10.2 — Replication, v1: what if the leader just sends each new entry to everyone?

Ana (the leader) appends the client's command to her own log and sends it out. A follower appends what it receives. When a majority have it, Ana marks it committed and tells the client "saved".

This works beautifully, right up to the first server that misses a message.

🔮 Predict: Cara was switched off while entries 1, 2 and 3 were written. She comes back. Ana, who has no idea Cara was away, sends her entry 4. Cara appends it. What is in her book?

Reveal

Entry 4 is sitting in slot 1. That was Cara's next free slot, so that is where "append" put it. Her book now says slot 1 is entry 4. Every other book says slot 1 is entry 1. Nothing has crashed, no error was returned, and Ana believes Cara is fully caught up. The two books will never agree again.

| Cara's book | In words | |---|---| | [_, _, _, entry4] is what Ana thinks Cara has | three entries then entry 4 | | entry 4 at index 1 is what Cara actually has | one entry, in the wrong slot |

If you said "she has a gap at 1, 2 and 3", notice that a log cannot have gaps. Append means "next free slot", and Cara's next free slot was 1.

The fix: never accept an entry without proving the prefix already matches. Every shout now names the slot just before the ones being sent: its number and the shift it was written in. In the code these are the previous log index and the previous log term, prevLogIndex and prevLogTerm. The follower accepts only if its own log has that exact (index, term) pair. Otherwise it rejects.

Why is one comparison enough for the whole prefix? This is the Log Matching Property, and it is the most elegant thing in Raft:

If two logs contain an entry with the same index and the same term, then the logs are identical in every entry up through that index.

It holds by induction. A leader never changes an entry it has already written. So an (index, term) pair identifies one specific entry, created once, by one specific leader. And that entry was only ever accepted by a follower that had already proved its predecessor matched. So agreement at index i certifies agreement at i−1, which certifies i−2, all the way down. One integer comparison certifies unlimited history. No checksums, no diffing, no Merkle trees.

When you turn this check off (Bugs.noLogMatchCheck), the follower says yes to everything. It tells the leader "I'm at index 3" while holding nothing, and the leader believes it. Read S2 as Cara, S1 as Ben.

with the prefix check ON   : S2 log = [w1@t1 w2@t1 w3@t1 w4@t1 w5@t1 w6@t1]   (repaired)
with the prefix check OFF  : S2 log = [d@t2]                                   (a hole, forever)
                             LOG MATCHING broken: S1 and S2 agree on slot 1 but differ...

In words: with the check on, Cara ends up with all six writes. With it off, Cara holds one entry from term 2 and nothing else, and the checker reports that Ben and Cara claim to agree on slot 1 while holding different things.

10.3 — Voting, v1: what if a higher term wins the vote?

A candidate has a bigger term than you, so you step down and vote for it. Simple, and it is what a term is for.

🔮 Predict: Ana and Ben have committed "transfer £500". That is a majority of three, so the customer has been told it succeeded. Ana then dies. Cara, who was switched off for the whole thing and has an empty book, comes back and asks to be leader with a higher term. Ben votes yes. What happens to the £500?

Reveal

It is deleted. Cara becomes leader with an empty log. The first entry she writes goes into slot 1. Ben's slot 1 currently holds the transfer, with a different term. That is a conflict, so Ben deletes his own entry and takes Cara's. A transaction that was acknowledged to a customer no longer exists on any machine.

With the check disabled, the lab prints two lines. Read S1 as Ben.

| What the lab prints | In words | |---|---| | S1's log afterwards: [overwrite@t2] | Ben's book now holds only Cara's term-2 write | | STATE MACHINE SAFETY broken: index 1 was committed as committed-write@t1 | slot 1 was settled as the transfer, and is now something else |

If you said "Ben keeps the transfer because he already committed it", remember lie number 3 from section 8: followers delete to match the leader, and nothing in v1 stops Cara from being that leader.

The fix: a term is not a qualification. Before granting a vote, compare the candidate's last log entry with your own, and refuse if yours is more complete:

boolean candidateUpToDate(int candLastIndex, int candLastTerm) {
    if (candLastTerm != lastLogTerm()) return candLastTerm > lastLogTerm();
    return candLastIndex >= lastLogIndex();
}

In words: later term wins; if the terms tie, the longer log wins. Term is compared first. A short log ending in term 5 beats a long log ending in term 4, because term 5 happened later and everything in it supersedes.

Why this one function is the whole safety argument. Try to build a leader that is missing a settled entry. The settled entry sits on a majority: say Ana, Ben and Dev. A candidate needs votes from a majority, so three of the five. You cannot pick three people without picking at least one of Ana, Ben or Dev. There are only five. That shared person still holds the entry, and the rule says they refuse anyone whose book is behind. So the candidate cannot win.

That paragraph is the proof. Two majorities always overlap, the fact from the quorums-and-replication lab, so at least one voter holds the committed entry, and that voter refuses anyone who does not. Therefore every leader that ever wins already has every committed entry. This is the Leader Completeness Property, and it is why Raft never needs to copy anything backwards from followers to a new leader.

10.4 — Committing, v1: is "a majority has it" enough?

The last of the four, and the one nobody guesses. The rule looks self-evident: count how many servers have index N; if it is a majority, index N is committed.

🔮 Predict: Entry OLD was created in term 1. It is now sitting on four of five servers, a clear majority, and the current leader can see that. Is it safe to tell the client OLD succeeded?

Reveal

No. This is Figure 8 of the Raft paper, and it is the single most surprising thing in the algorithm. A fifth server that was offline can hold a different entry at that same index, created in term 2. Because term 2 is later than term 1, that server's log counts as more up to date. So it can still win an election, and when it does, it overwrites the index on all four others. "A majority has it" and "it can never be removed" are not the same statement.

If you said "yes, four of five is more than enough", you are counting copies. The up-to-date check does not count copies. It compares terms, and a single copy with a later term beats four copies with an earlier one.

java src/Experiments.java figure8 drives that exact history by hand. With the naive count-the-replicas rule, the lab prints this. Read S0 as Ana, S1 as Ben, S4 as Eve.

  S4 LEADER    term=4  commit=1  log=[NEW@t2]  applied=[NEW]
  !! STATE MACHINE SAFETY broken: index 1 was committed as OLD@t1 but S1 has committed NEW@t2 there
  !! STATE MACHINES DIVERGED: at position 0 S0 applied "OLD" but S4 applied "NEW"

In words: Ana is dead, still believing slot 1 is OLD and having made that pizza. Ben now has NEW in slot 1 but already made OLD. Eve, the leader, has NEW and made NEW. Four servers were told OLD was permanent. It is gone.

The fix, in five words: only commit your own term. A leader may only mark index N committed when the entry at N was created in the leader's current term. In the code, log[N].term == currentTerm:

boolean ownTerm = log.get(n).term() == currentTerm;
if (count >= majority() && ownTerm) { commitIndex = n; }

Why does that close it? An entry from the leader's current term can only be overwritten by a leader of a later term. Any such leader needed a majority of votes. A majority contains someone holding this entry, and the up-to-date check makes that someone refuse. The old-term entry had no such protection, because a competing entry at the same index could carry a higher term and still look fresher.

And old entries are not stranded: they get committed for free, underneath. The moment the new leader commits one entry of its own, everything below it in the log is committed by the Log Matching Property. In practice a real leader appends a no-op entry immediately on winning, precisely to trigger this.

With the same history and the rule switched on:

own-term commit rule ON (real Raft)
  S1 FOLLOWER  term=4  commit=0  log=[NEW@t2]  applied=[]
  S4 LEADER    term=4  commit=0  log=[NEW@t2]  applied=[]
  violations: none

Nothing was committed, so nothing was lost. Ben and Eve hold NEW, nobody has made a pizza, and no promise was broken. OLD vanishing is now correct: nobody was ever told it succeeded.

10.5 — Repair: how does a wrong log get fixed?

Ana (the leader) keeps two numbers per follower. One is what she knows: the highest index that follower definitely has, matchIndex[i]. The other is what she will try next, nextIndex[i]. On winning an election she optimistically sets every next-index to her own log end.

Then she probes. Each rejection means "we don't agree that far back", so she takes one off the next-index and tries again. Once the follower says yes, she sends everything above that point, and the follower deletes any of its own entries that conflict.

The leader never diffs the logs. The follower's yes/no on the previous slot, the (prevLogIndex, prevLogTerm) pair, is an oracle. The leader walks it backwards until it hits agreement.

🔮 Predict: Two followers need repair. Cara is merely behind: she missed six writes, and the leader holds all six. Ana holds five entries that were never committed, left over from a shift that ended badly. Which repair costs more rejections?

Reveal

Cara's, by six to zero. Being behind costs one rejection per missing entry, because the leader's guess starts at its own log end and walks back one slot at a time. Ana's five ghost entries were removed with zero rejections, because the leader's guess for her was frozen at election time, when its own log was short. One conflicting entry made Ana truncate her whole tail in one message.

If you said "Ana's, because five wrong entries must each be found and deleted", you are imagining a diff. There is no diff. One mismatch deletes everything above it.

10.6 — Why does this look like a bug and isn't?

After a leader change, a follower's leftover uncommitted entries are not deleted straight away. They survive until the new leader appends something of its own. Tests.noOpLessonTest demonstrates it:

an_uncommitted_entry_is_NOT_deleted_just_because_a_new_leader_appeared   PASS
that_divergence_is_safe_because_nothing_was_ever_committed_there         PASS
the_new_leaders_first_own_write_is_what_finally_deletes_it               PASS

Two logs genuinely disagree, for a while, and it is fine. Nobody was ever told those entries were safe. But it is untidy, and it delays the commit of everything inherited from the previous term. Which is the second reason real systems append a no-op on election.

12. What is guaranteed, and what is not?

In one breath: Five promises about the books, each one already earned above. Safety never depends on timing; liveness always does. And four things people wrongly expect from Raft.

The five properties, each earned above

Every promise the shop makes is one of these five. Read the last column: it is the rule that enforces each one.

Property What it says Enforced by
Election Safety at most one leader per term one vote per term (votedFor)
Leader Append-Only a leader never overwrites or deletes its own entries clientRequest only appends
Log Matching same (index, term) ⇒ identical prefixes the prevLogIndex/prevLogTerm check

Cluster.checkSafety() asserts all five after every tick of every test and experiment in this lab. In the shop's words: no shift ever had two people in charge; Ana never crossed out her own line; two books agreeing on one line agree on everything above it; whoever takes charge already has every settled order; and no two people ever made different pizzas for the same slot.

Safety versus liveness: the distinction to take away

Safety means nothing wrong ever happens. Raft's safety does not depend on timing at all. Clocks can be arbitrarily wrong. Messages can be arbitrarily delayed, reordered, duplicated or lost. Safety holds. Every scenario in section 11 confirms it, including the ones where the cluster does nothing useful for 6000 ticks.

Liveness means something eventually happens. This does depend on timing, and Raft makes no promise about it. It cannot. FLP, which you met in the paxos lab, proves no protocol can guarantee both in an asynchronous network with even one crash. Raft's answer is the same as Multi-Paxos's: be always safe, and be live whenever the network behaves.

The cleanest demonstration in this lab is the pair of identical-timeout runs. Turn off randomisation and liveness dies with safety untouched. Additionally turn off the one-vote-per-term rule and you get five leaders in one term. Safety in ruins, while liveness looks great.

What Raft explicitly does NOT give you

🔮 Predict: Ana is cut off on the small side of a split and still believes she is leader. A client asks her, not for a write, but to read the current balance. Is her answer correct?

Reveal

Not necessarily. The big side may have elected Cara and committed new writes that Ana has never heard of. Ana's state machine is honest about what Ana knows, and Ana knows stale news. A read from a leader is only correct if the leader first proves it is still the leader.

If you said "yes, she is the leader and leaders have everything", that is Leader Completeness, and it is about committed writes at election time. It says nothing about a leader who has since been replaced without knowing.

  • Fast or even correct reads, for free. Reading from the leader's state machine can return stale data, because a partitioned old leader does not know it has been replaced. Linearizable reads need an extra mechanism: route the read through the log, or confirm leadership with a heartbeat round first (ReadIndex), or hold a time-based lease. Reading from a follower is stale by default. This is the single most common misunderstanding of Raft in production.
  • Protection against lying servers. The failure model is crash-stop and network faults. A server that sends deliberately false terms or logs breaks everything. Byzantine fault tolerance is a different family of protocols.
  • Scale-out for writes. Every write goes through one leader and lands on a majority. Adding servers makes you more fault tolerant and slower, never faster. Scaling writes means multiple Raft groups, one per shard. That is exactly what TiKV, CockroachDB and etcd-at-scale do, and where consistent-hashing comes back in.
  • Progress with fewer than a majority alive. 2 of 5 servers can do nothing at all, by design. This is not a bug to route around.

In one breath: The implementation is about 250 lines, and almost all of it is bookkeeping. Eight lines carry the rules. Each one is a scar from section 10, and each one is marked in the source.

       && log.get(ae.prevLogIndex()).term() == ae.prevLogTerm();

4. Followers delete their own work. Ben crosses out his ghost entries to match the leader. Also in onAppendEntries:

if (log.get(idx).term() != e.term()) {
    while (lastLogIndex() >= idx) log.remove(log.size() - 1);
    log.add(e);
}

This looks dangerous and is not. By Leader Completeness, anything a leader overwrites was never committed.

5. Count replicas, but only for your own term. Ana counts books, but only for orders from her own shift. In advanceCommit:

boolean ownTerm = log.get(n).term() == currentTerm;
if (count >= majority() && ownTerm) commitIndex = n;

Without it: Figure 8 (stage 10.4).

6. A bigger term always wins, everywhere. Cara sees term 5 and adopts it without argument. In stepDown, called from all four handlers:

currentTerm = newTerm; role = Role.FOLLOWER; votedFor = -1;

This one rule replaces every "am I still the leader?" check you would otherwise have to write.

int spread = bugs.fixedTimeout ? 0 : cluster.rng.nextInt(ELECTION_MAX - ELECTION_MIN + 1);

if (now >= heartbeatDeadline) { for (int p : peers) if (p != id) sendAppend(p); }

There is no separate liveness protocol. The thing that replicates data is the same thing that proves the leader is alive.

15. What changes between this toy and etcd?

The chain from here to there

This lab is five objects passing messages through an in-memory network you can delay, drop and partition, on a logical clock.

A real implementation is the same state machine, plus disk, plus snapshots, plus a way to change the membership, plus batching, wrapped in a library (etcd-io/raft in Go, tikv/raft-rs in Rust, hashicorp/raft).

A production system is that library under a key-value store, replicated across availability zones, with clients that retry idempotently and monitoring that watches election rate.

What does an on-call engineer actually watch?

  • Leader elections per minute. The single best Raft health signal. Steady state is ~0. Anything sustained means the network, the disk, or the timeouts.
  • fsync / WAL write latency. A slow disk on the leader looks exactly like a slow network. Heartbeats land late, followers time out, the cluster churns. This is the most common real cause of "etcd is unstable". It is Ana's pen running dry: she is fine, but her "still here!" comes late.
  • Commit index lag per follower. Catches the replica that is quietly falling behind and is about to need a snapshot.
  • Proposal latency and failed proposals. A proposal that fails because the leader changed mid-flight is normal and must be retried idempotently.
  • Database size vs quota. etcd refuses writes when its backend hits the quota. The symptom is a cluster that elects fine and commits nothing.

The trade-offs, stated plainly

Raft buys you one ordered history that survives a minority failing, and it pays for it with:

  • a write round trip to a majority, because you cannot beat the speed of light to your second availability zone;
  • no write scaling, because more servers means more fault tolerance and more latency, never more throughput;
  • an outage on every leader change, measured in one election timeout;
  • an absolute refusal to serve a minority, which is a feature until the day you lose a whole datacentre and want the survivors to keep working.

Choose it for the things that must be exactly one: configuration, membership, locks, shard ownership, leader identity, sequence allocation. Do not choose it for bulk data. Put the decisions in Raft and the data wherever it belongs. That is precisely why etcd is small and Kubernetes is large.

Where this sits. paxos answers "can a group agree on one value?". Raft answers "can a group agree on an unbounded ordered sequence of values, and can a human understand how?". two-phase-commit had a coordinator whose death blocked everyone. Raft's leader dies routinely and costs one election timeout, because leadership is a lease decided by vote, not an appointment. Ana was voted in; she was never appointed.

Commonly confused with. The one sentence that separates each:

  • Raft vs Paxos. Same guarantees. Raft restricts the design (a strong leader, logs that only ever grow forward, elections that only complete-log candidates can win) to trade some flexibility for a protocol you can hold in your head.
  • Raft vs Multi-Paxos. Practically the same algorithm. Multi-Paxos permits holes in the log and out-of-order commits; Raft forbids both, which is most of why it is easier.
  • Raft's majority vs a read/write quorum. A consensus quorum decides an order; R+W>N merely guarantees two sets intersect. They coincide at "2 of 3", which is why they get merged in people's heads. See quorums-and-replication.
  • Raft vs primary-backup replication. Both have one writer. Only Raft can choose a new one safely without an external arbiter. That is the entire difference, and it is why "just use a leader" is not an answer.
  • Raft vs 2PC. 2PC makes different systems commit one transaction atomically. Raft makes identical replicas agree on every transaction's order. They compose: real distributed databases run 2PC across shards, with each shard's participants replicated by Raft.
  • Raft vs gossip. Gossip spreads facts that will eventually agree, cheaply, with no leader. Raft decides facts that must agree immediately and forever, expensively. See gossip-protocols.
  • Raft vs CRDTs. You already have the sentence from the crdts lab: CRDTs guarantee that everyone ends up agreeing; they do not guarantee that what everyone agrees on was ever allowed. Raft is what you buy when the invariant matters: stock >= 0, one owner per shard, a balance that cannot go negative. CRDTs let both sides keep writing during a partition; Raft makes the minority stop. That is the same boundary seen from its two sides. Choosing between them is choosing whether your invariant or your availability is negotiable.
  • Committed vs applied. Committed means "can never change". Applied means "the state machine has run it". Applied always lags, and a read that ignores the difference returns stale data.

Raft — Solutions

Every measured number below was produced by running the lab. Where a claim was checked by running a modified copy, the command is shown.

Rung 1 — EXPLAIN

1.1 Several machines must hold the same list of changes in the same order, so that replaying the list anywhere gives the same answer. "Everyone writes down what they hear" fails because two machines can be told different things at the same moment and both write them into the same slot — and with no shared clock there is no fact of the matter about which came first. Raft manufactures the missing ordering by electing one machine to be the sole writer for a numbered stretch of time.

1.2 The correct version: at most one leader per term, and only the leader of the newest term can commit anything. Two servers genuinely believe they are leader at the same moment all the time — the visualization's partition scenario shows it in three clicks. What that buys you is that Raft never has to prevent split-brain; it only has to make the losing half harmless, which is far cheaper. The old leader cannot reach a majority, so it can never commit, so nothing it does can be observed.

1.3 Because wall clocks disagree, drift, and jump — a machine with a clock 60 seconds fast would win every comparison and could overwrite genuinely newer decisions. (The quorums-and-replication lab measured exactly that: under last-write-wins, one node 60 s fast silently discarded 1000 of 1000 newer writes.) A term is a logical clock: it only ever increases, it increases only when someone attempts leadership, and every server that sees a higher one adopts it immediately. It orders attempts, which is all Raft needs.

1.4 Waiting for all five means one machine in a bad mood stops the shop — and it will happen, because at any moment something is always restarting. Waiting for one means two different machines can each be the "one" for two different writes, and then nobody can say which happened. Three of five is the smallest number with the property that any two such groups must share a member, which is what makes "was this already decided?" answerable by asking any three machines. It is also a tail-latency device: you wait for the third-fastest, never the slowest.

Rung 2 — IDENTIFY

2.1 Two independent signals: the role badge reads leader and the row's dot is teal; and in the message band, the arcs originating from that row are solid (outbound AppendEntries) while every other row only sends dashed replies. A third: the verdict banner at the top names it.

2.2 A dashed, outlined cell is written down but not committed; a solid filled cell is committed. It becomes solid when the leader has learned that a majority of servers hold it — and, if it was created in an earlier term, only once the leader commits an entry of its own term above it.

2.3 Entries written down ≥ entries it knows are safe, always. The follower learns about the commit point on the next message after the one that carried the entry, so it is always one round behind. It can never be the other way round because commitIndex = min(leaderCommit, lastLogIndex) — a server refuses to claim as safe anything it does not actually hold.

2.4 (a) The old leader, still badged leader on the small side, and the new leader on the big side. (b) The term number in each row's metadata. The higher term is the one allowed to commit; the lower one will step down the instant the two sides can talk again.

2.5 The two pairs are (candidate's last index, candidate's last term) and (my last index, my last term). The term comparison decides it first; the index is only consulted when the terms are equal. The event line reads e.g. S1 refuses: cand (idx 0, term 0) < mine (idx 1, term 1).

Rung 3 — PREDICT

3.1 A's RequestVote carries term 6 (it incremented 5 → 6). B and C are already on term 6 and have very likely already voted in it — if so they refuse, A's election fails, and A tries again at term 7, where it will succeed if its log is up to date. The cluster ends on term 7 (or higher). The important part: A's stale term did not make it lose; a stale log would have. This is the "disruptive server" problem, and it is exactly what pre-vote (exercise 7.4) prevents.

3.2 Not committed, and yes it can still be deleted. This is Figure 8. Four of five is a majority, but the entry is from term 5 while the leader is on term 7, so the own-term rule forbids committing it. If the fifth server holds a different entry at index 4 created in term 6, that server's log is more up to date, so it can still win an election and overwrite the index on all four others. Measured with the rule disabled (java src/Experiments.java figure8): 12 distinct safety violations, including index 1 was committed as OLD@t1 but S1 has committed NEW@t2 there.

3.3 (a) yes — measured 30/30 at 50% loss and 26/30 at 70%; (b) mostly no — only 7 of 30 runs committed their writes at 50%, and 0/30 at 70%; (c) no safety property breaks at any loss rate, including 90%. Loss cannot corrupt anything; it can only prevent heartbeats landing inside an election timeout, which turns the cluster into a machine for holding elections.

3.4 It depends entirely on when the current leader was elected, because nextIndex is set optimistically to the leader's own log end at that moment and only ever walks backwards. Measured (java src/Experiments.java log-repair):

  • follower missed 6 entries, leader elected holding all 6 → nextIndex = 7 → 6 rejections, 47 messages.
  • follower holds 5 wrong entries, leader elected when its own log was short → nextIndex = 2 → 0 rejections, and all five wrong entries deleted by a single message.

The counter-intuitive half: being behind is expensive, being wrong can be free.

3.5 Either outcome is legal and neither is a bug. The entry was never committed, so Raft promised nothing about it. If the next leader happens to be a server that stored it, it survives (and gets committed later); if not, it is deleted. What would be a bug is the client treating its timeout as "the write did not happen" — which is why every client of a Raft system must retry idempotently. Same conclusion as transactional-outbox: a failed write means "not confirmed", never "did not happen".

Rung 4 — MODIFY

4.1 Every election test fails; safety tests all still pass. Liveness was lost, safety untouched. Measured over 6000 ticks: term 40 reached, no leader ever elected, 0 violations. This is the cleanest demonstration in the lab that the two properties are independent.

Rule of thumb from the paper: broadcastTime ≪ electionTimeout ≪ MTBF; etcd ships a 10× margin (100 ms heartbeat, 1000 ms election timeout).

4.3 Nothing breaks — duplicates_never_broke_a_safety_property passes, and only-once is applied exactly once on all five servers. Two mechanisms: (1) term filtering — every handler drops a message whose term does not equal currentTerm, so replies from old attempts are ignored; and (2) position-addressed writes — AppendEntries says "put these entries at these indices", not "append these", so re-delivering it writes the same content to the same slots. Raft is idempotent by shape, not by a dedup table.

The overlap argument dies immediately: with N=5, two groups of 2 can be disjoint ({S0,S1} and {S2,S3}). Two candidates can each collect 2 votes in the same term, so two leaders exist in one term; and a "committed" entry can sit on 2 servers that a future leader never has to talk to. Half plus one is not a convention — it is the smallest number that forces intersection.

4.5 It breaks, and it is a liveness bug. The own-term rule makes the set of committable indices non-contiguous: an old-term entry at index 1 may have a majority and still be forbidden, while the leader's own entry at index 2 is allowed. An upward scan that stops at the first index failing the test stops at index 1 forever and never reaches index 2. Reproduced directly:

upward scan   : leader term=2 log=[old@t1 new@t2] commitIndex=0   STALLED
downward scan : leader term=2 log=[old@t1 new@t2] commitIndex=2   committed

The downward scan is not a stylistic choice — it is what lets a new leader commit its own entry and drag its predecessors' entries into the committed region underneath it.

Rung 5 — IMPLEMENT

static boolean candidateLogIsUpToDate(int candLastIndex, int candLastTerm,
                                      int myLastIndex, int myLastTerm) {
    if (candLastTerm != myLastTerm) return candLastTerm > myLastTerm;
    return candLastIndex >= myLastIndex;
}

static boolean shouldGrantVote(int myTerm, int myVotedFor,
                               int candTerm, int candId, boolean candLogUpToDate) {
    if (candTerm < myTerm) return false;
    if (myVotedFor != -1 && myVotedFor != candId) return false;
    return candLogUpToDate;
}

static int highestCommittableIndex(int[] matchIndex, int[] logTerms,
                                   int currentTerm, int commitIndex) {
    int best = commitIndex;
    for (int n = logTerms.length - 1; n > commitIndex; n--) {
        if (logTerms[n] != currentTerm) continue;          // only your OWN term counts
        int count = 0;
        for (int m : matchIndex) if (m >= n) count++;
        if (count >= matchIndex.length / 2 + 1) { best = n; break; }
    }
    return best;
}

5.1 Term is compared first. A short log ending in term 5 is more up to date than a long log ending in term 4, because everything after term 4 superseded it — the long log's extra entries were uncommitted work from a leader that has since been replaced. Comparing length first would let stale-but-verbose logs win, and would delete committed entries.

5.2 Because a RequestVote can be duplicated or retried. If S1 voted for S2 and the reply was lost, S2 retries; refusing the retry would waste an election for no reason. Granting it is safe because it is the same vote, not a second one — the rule is one candidate per term, not one message per term.

5.3 It protects State Machine Safety via Figure 8: an entry from an older term can reach a majority and still be overwritten later by a server holding a higher-term entry at that index. Counting replicas is a valid test only for entries the current leader created itself.

Rung 7 — HANDLE FAILURE (worked discussion, not one right answer)

7.1 — Safe retries. Add (clientId, seqNo) to Entry. The state machine keeps a small map clientId -> (lastSeqNo, lastResult). On applying an entry, if seqNo <= lastSeqNo for that client, do not re-apply — return the stored result. The crucial dependency: this works only because every replica applies the same log in the same order, so every replica's dedup table is identical, so a retry that lands on a different leader after a failover gets the same answer. In other words State Machine Safety is what makes exactly-once possible; the session table is what makes it happen. Note the dedup table has to be part of the replicated state machine (and therefore part of snapshots), not a cache beside it.

7.2 — No-op on election. In checkWon, append new Entry(currentTerm, "<no-op>") before the first heartbeat. Now the leader has an entry of its own term immediately, so as soon as a majority stores it, the own-term rule fires and everything below it commits at once — including all inherited entries. Without it, a new leader that receives no client traffic can sit indefinitely with uncommitted inherited entries, and Tests.noOpLessonTest shows the follower divergence that persists in the meantime. It cannot simply mark them committed directly: that is the Figure 8 bug.

7.3 — Faster repair. Extend AppendReply with conflictTerm and conflictIndex. On rejection the follower reports the term of the entry it has at prevLogIndex and the first index it holds for that term. The leader then either jumps to its own last index for conflictTerm, or straight to conflictIndex. Case A's six rejections become one or two. This is section 5.3 of the paper, where Ongaro notes the optimisation is probably unnecessary in practice because failures are rare — it matters most for a follower rejoining after a long absence, before snapshots kick in.

7.4 — Pre-vote. Reproduce it by partitioning one server off, letting it burn terms, then healing: its inflated term deposes a healthy leader, costing an election for no reason. Pre-vote adds a round asking "would you vote for me if I started an election?" without incrementing the term. Voters answer no if they have heard from a leader recently. Only a majority of yes answers triggers a real election. etcd, TiKV and CockroachDB all ship this on by default.

7.5 — Correct reads. First show the bug: put the leader alone in a minority partition and read from it — it still believes it is leader and happily returns data that the majority has already moved past. Two fixes:

  • ReadIndex: record commitIndex, send one round of heartbeats, wait for a majority to confirm you are still leader, wait until lastApplied >= that index, then read. Costs one round trip; assumes nothing about clocks.
  • Lease read: after a successful heartbeat round, assume leadership for electionTimeout - clockDrift. Costs nothing; assumes bounded clock drift, which is a real assumption that has caused real incidents.

The choice is the whole trade: a round trip, or a belief about clocks.

Rung 8 — DESIGN (discussion, no single right answer)

8.1 — Three datacentres, 50 ms apart.

  • Five servers, 2 + 2 + 1 across London / Frankfurt / Dublin. Not 3-in-one-DC: that survives no datacentre loss at all. Not 1+1+1: it survives one DC, but every single write must cross the ocean, and any one DC loss leaves you one failure from a dead cluster. 2+2+1 tolerates a whole DC and a machine.
  • Write latency ≈ 50 ms, and the reason matters: a commit needs 3 of 5, and the leader plus its local partner are only 2 — so every write waits for one cross-DC round trip, plus the leader's fsync. You cannot engineer this away; it is the speed of light.
  • Dublin cut off: nothing happens. Dublin's single server is a minority; the other four carry on. Dublin's clients must be routed elsewhere.
  • London cut off (holding the leader): Frankfurt + Dublin have 3 of 5 and elect a new leader after one election timeout. London's two servers keep believing, commit nothing, and step down on reconnect. Set the election timeout well above 50 ms — a 150 ms timeout across a 50 ms link will re-elect on every hiccup.
  • Survives unchanged: every safety rule, the message shapes, the failure behaviour. Must change: persistence with real fsync, snapshots, membership changes, batching, correct reads, timeouts tuned to WAN latency, pre-vote, and mutual TLS between DCs.
  • Dashboard: leader elections per minute (page on sustained > 0), fsync p99, commit-index lag per follower, proposal latency p99, quota usage. Page someone at 3am for: sustained elections, or quorum lost.

8.2 — What belongs in Raft.

Verdict
(a) shard ownership Yes. Small, must be exactly one answer, read constantly. This is etcd's core use case.
(b) ledger at 40k writes/sec No. One leader, one majority round trip per write — you would be throughput-bound and latency-bound. Use a partitioned log or database; if you need consensus, use many Raft groups (one per shard), not one.
(c) end-of-day settlement lock Yes, with care — a lock needs a lease with a fencing token, because a leaseholder can be paused by GC and wake up believing it still holds the lock. Raft gives you the token; it does not stop the stale holder acting.
(d) session tokens No. High volume, per-user, and staleness is harmless. Redis or a signed token.
(e) feature flags Yes if flips must be instant and globally consistent; otherwise a config service with eventual propagation is cheaper — see gossip-protocols.
(f) idempotency keys Depends on grain. Inside one shard's state machine, they belong in that shard's Raft group. A global idempotency store at payment volume does not — put them in the same store as the payment, so the key and the effect commit together (transactional-outbox).

8.3 — 40 elections/minute, nothing crashed, network fine.

  1. fsync / WAL write latency on the leader. This is the most likely cause and it is not a network problem at all. Raft must durably store currentTerm, votedFor and new entries before replying; a slow or contended disk delays heartbeats exactly as if the network were slow. A noisy neighbour on the same volume is the classic cause.
  2. Timeout configuration — heartbeat raised without raising the election timeout, or defaults tuned for a LAN running across a WAN.
  3. A disruptive rejoining server with an inflated term, if pre-vote is off.
  4. Database size / quota — a cluster at its backend quota elects fine and commits nothing.
  5. CPU starvation or GC pauses on the leader — same symptom as slow disk.

The lesson from java src/Experiments.java loss is what tells you where not to look: election churn is always a timing problem, never a correctness one. Your data is fine. Something is late.

8.4 — Raft lock service vs SELECT FOR UPDATE.

For: it is one line of SQL against a database you already run, back up, monitor and understand. It is genuinely correct for mutual exclusion while the connection lives. Most teams do not need distributed locks; they need a mutex, and Postgres is a fine mutex. You avoid operating a second stateful system.

Against: your Postgres is a single point of failure and its failover is not consensus — a primary/replica promotion under a partition can produce two writable primaries, and then two holders of your "exclusive" lock. Lock lifetime is tied to a TCP connection, so a hung client holds it until a TCP timeout you do not control. And there is no fencing token, so a paused holder can wake and act.

What I would do: if you already run etcd (and if you run Kubernetes, you do), use it — you get leases and fencing tokens for free, with no new system. If you do not, use Postgres advisory locks with an explicit expiry and a fencing token the downstream resource checks, and write down that your lock's safety is bounded by your Postgres failover story.

8.5 — Payments: where Raft stops.

Crash points between "record the payment" and "call the card network":

  1. After the client sends, before the leader appends — nothing recorded, client times out. Retry must be idempotent.
  2. After the leader appends, before a majority stores it — may or may not survive. Raft does not tell you which.
  3. After commit, before the client is told — recorded, client saw a timeout. The dangerous one: the client will retry, and without an idempotency key it pays twice.
  4. After commit, before the external call — recorded but not executed. Needs a durable outbox and a worker, not Raft.
  5. External call made, response lost — the card network's problem and yours: you must reconcile, using their idempotency key.
  6. External call succeeded, before recording the result — recorded as pending forever until reconciliation.

Raft fixes exactly one thing: 2 and 3 stop being ambiguous internally — once committed, every replica agrees the payment exists, and a failover cannot lose it. It fixes nothing about 1, 4, 5 or 6, because those cross a boundary Raft cannot see. The two-part answer is: Raft for the internal ordering, a transactional-outbox for the hand-off to the outside world, an idempotency key end to end, and a saga-style compensation for what cannot be undone. Consensus makes your replicas agree; it does not make the world agree with you.
