Lab 56 · Running It In Production

Microservices — what actually happens when you cut a system apart

Tell a friend: Asha's bakery, Riverbend, grew from four staff to forty, and everybody now waits for the one weekly moment when the shop's single binder may be changed. So Asha gives each counter its own binder — and keeps one shared storeroom, so a shelf renamed by the bread counter still breaks the gift-box counter at 3am. The only split that works is a split of the data: one counter owns a shelf, and everyone else has to ask. That buys exactly one thing, the right to change your part today without asking anyone, and the bill arrives immediately as arithmetic. The cruellest line on the bill is that a slow neighbour destroys you faster than a dead one.

BEGINNER MODEL

1. Why would anyone cut a working program apart?

In one breath: One program means one turn. Forty people cannot share one turn. Microservices are an attempt to buy more turns.

A company writes one program. The company grows. More and more people need to change that one program, and there is only one of it.

One place to edit. One thing to test. One moment when it goes live.

So everyone has to take turns. And everyone's mistakes land on everyone else.

A person who wants to change the colour of a button has to wait for a release. That release also contains somebody else's half-finished database change. If that database change is wrong, the button change is cancelled too.

Eventually the company spends more time coordinating who may change the program than actually changing it. Microservices are an attempt to buy that coordination back. You cut the one program into many programs. Each one can then be changed and released on its own.

What does it look like when nobody solves this? Releases slow from daily to weekly to monthly. A one-line fix waits three weeks behind an unrelated feature. Every release needs a meeting. One team's bad afternoon takes the whole product offline.

That is the pain. Hold on to it. Everything in this lab is either that pain, or the bill for curing it.

2. What happens when forty people share one turn?

In one breath: Riverbend is one bakery with one binder behind the counter. Forty people now write into it, but it can only be changed once a week, so one person's mistake cancels everybody else's week.

No jargon in this section. A ten-year-old could follow it.

Riverbend starts as one small bakery with one counter. Asha runs it.

Everything the shop knows lives in one binder behind that counter: the recipes, the prices, the list of who has paid, the list of what is in the storeroom. Everyone works from that binder. Four people work there.

It is wonderful. If you want to know anything, you look in the binder. If you want to change anything, you write in the binder.

Ten years later Riverbend has forty staff. It sells bread, cakes, sandwiches, coffee, gift boxes and wedding catering. The binder is now four thousand pages long.

Here is the problem, and it is not the size of the binder.

There is still only one binder. And Riverbend has a rule: you cannot change a page while people are using the binder. So once a week, at 6am on Tuesday, the shop closes. The binder is taken away. Every change everybody wrote that week is copied in at once. Then the binder comes back.

Watch what that rule does to two of Asha's staff.

Sana wants to change the price of a croissant. One number. She has to wait until Tuesday.

Marcus is halfway through rewriting the wedding-catering section. His new pages have a mistake in them.

On Tuesday morning the new binder comes back with Marcus's mistake in it. The shop is chaos. So Asha reverses the whole Tuesday change — including Sana's croissant price, which was fine.

Sana waits another week. For one number.

Now multiply that. Forty people, each writing into the same binder. Each one able to spoil everyone else's Tuesday.

So Asha adds a rule: before Tuesday, every change must be read and approved in a meeting. The meeting takes three hours. Then it takes a day. Then Tuesdays become fortnightly, because a fortnight of changes is too risky to do weekly.

Riverbend has not run out of talent, money, or ideas. It has run out of turns. Forty people are sharing one turn.

🔮 Predict: Asha is about to try the obvious fix. What do you think it is, and what do you think will go wrong with it?

Reveal

The obvious fix is: give each counter its own binder. Split the four thousand pages into a bread binder, a cake binder, a coffee binder, a catering binder — one per counter, each counter free to rewrite its own pages whenever it likes.

If you guessed "hire more staff" or "make the meeting shorter", you are treating this as a people problem. It is not. Forty people are already there, and the meeting exists because of the single binder, not the other way round.

What goes wrong with the split is the part almost every real company gets wrong too, so it deserves its own section. Keep reading — but notice now that we have said nothing at all about the storeroom.

3. Why not just give every counter its own binder?

In one breath: Riverbend splits one binder into six. For about six weeks it is magnificent.

Asha splits.

The four thousand pages become six binders — Bread, Cake, Coffee, Catering, Gift Boxes, Deliveries. One per counter, each owned by a small team. Each counter may rewrite its own binder any morning it likes. No Tuesday. No meeting.

Sometimes one counter needs something from another. The cake counter needs today's coffee prices for the "cake and coffee" deal. So a runner carries a note between counters and brings the answer back.

In software terms, and this is exactly what most companies do first: the one program was split into six programs. Each gets its own repository, its own build, its own deploy button, and network calls between them.

For about six weeks, this is magnificent. Then it starts going wrong. It goes wrong in a way that is genuinely surprising the first time you see it.

4. What did Riverbend forget to split?

In one breath: Riverbend split the binders and kept one storeroom. Every counter still writes on the same stock cards, so every counter is still holding on to every other counter.

Here is what Asha did not split: the storeroom.

All six counters still walk into the same storeroom. They take flour off the same shelf. And here is the part that matters. All six counters still write on the same stock cards that live in that storeroom.

🔮 Predict: Six binders, six teams, one storeroom. Which of Riverbend's two original problems has actually gone away — the waiting, or the breaking-each-other?

Reveal

Neither. That is the surprise. If you said "the waiting is gone, they can each write their own binder now", you have been fooled by the binders. The stock cards are also a thing every counter writes, and nothing about them changed. Riverbend now has six deploy buttons and the same single turn.

Three things happen next, in this order.

4a. How does a change that touches nobody break somebody?

Dev's bread counter decides its stock cards are badly designed. On its own morning it renames the column "Qty" to "OnHand" on the flour cards. Nobody's permission was needed. That is the whole point of having its own binder.

At 3am the gift-box counter's automatic reorder stops working. Nobody at the bread counter knew gift boxes read the flour cards. There was no meeting to miss. No approval to skip. No rule broken. Dev genuinely believed that shelf was his.

This is the single most important sentence in this section:

A shared storeroom is an interface that nobody agreed to, nobody versioned, and nobody can see. Every column any other counter reads is a promise you made without knowing you made it.

Notice what is missing here that would have caught it in the old world. When it was one program, renaming a field was safe because the compiler found every place that used it. Split into six programs sharing one storeroom, you kept every dependency and deleted the only thing that could see them.

4b. Why can nobody release alone any more?

The cake counter needs a new column on the stock cards. Adding it changes the cards. The other five counters read those cards. So the change has to be coordinated with five teams.

They are back in a meeting. It is the same Tuesday meeting, wearing a different hat.

4c. Why does every job still need every counter?

The split was made by kind of thing rather than by kind of work — a "recipes" binder, a "prices" binder, a "stock" binder. So almost every real task now needs three or four counters to change together.

Selling a new seasonal cake touches the recipe binder, the price binder, and the stock binder. Three teams. Three deploys. In a fixed order.

Riverbend now has six programs and still one turn. It has all the coordination cost of the old system, plus runners, plus notes that get lost.

This has a name, and it is the thing this lab exists to help you avoid:

A distributed monolith is a system that has been split into many separately deployed pieces but not separated. The pieces still have to change together. So you pay every cost of being distributed and receive none of the benefit.

It is strictly worse than the monolith it replaced. A monolith is one program, one deployable, one database — Riverbend's original single binder. The monolith at least had a compiler.

So what did the naive fix actually reveal? The thing that binds two pieces of software together is not that they sit in the same file, the same repository or the same process. It is that they share knowledge of the same data. You can move code anywhere you like. If two pieces still read the same table, they are one thing, wherever they live.

5. What did Asha actually want?

In one breath: Not smaller programs. She wanted to change her part today without asking anyone, and without breaking anyone.

Say plainly what Riverbend actually wanted. Not "smaller programs" — it never wanted that. It wanted this:

I can change my part and put it live, today, without asking anyone — and without breaking anyone.

Both halves are load-bearing, and each is a separate obligation.

Without asking anyone means no shared release train. No cross-team approval. No ordering constraint with somebody else's deploy.

Without breaking anyone means no hidden reader of my data. And no change I can make that silently violates a promise somebody is relying on.

There is a sharp test for whether you have this. Keep it for the rest of your career:

Can you rename a column, at 4pm on a Friday, without telling anybody?

If the answer is no, the thing you are looking at is not separate. It does not matter how many repositories, containers or teams it has.

Everything from here on is the pursuit of that one requirement — and then the bill that arrives for having got it.

6. Where does the cut really go?

In one breath: Cut where the data is owned, not where the code is. Then the only thing crossing the line is a promise you chose to make.

Two sentences.

Cut the system where the data ownership falls, not where the code falls: exactly one piece of software may write and read a given piece of data, and everybody else must ask it.

Then the only thing crossing the boundary is a promise you chose to make and can version — so you can change everything behind it whenever you like.

That is the whole idea.

Riverbend's version: each counter gets its own binder and its own storeroom, with a locked door. If the cake counter needs to know how much flour there is, it asks Dev's bread counter and accepts whatever answer comes back. It may never walk in and look.

The locked door converts an accidental, invisible dependency into a deliberate, visible one. And only deliberate dependencies can be managed.

Everything else in this lab is one of two things. Either it is how to decide where the doors go. Or it is what it costs to have runners instead of shelves.

In one breath: Now the professional vocabulary, and only now. Every word below names something you have already watched happen at Riverbend.

Each group of words below is a short story first, then a small table that points back at the moment you met the thing. The full one-line definitions all live in the glossary at section 16.

Riverbend's counters are about to become programs, so meet the people who run them. Asha keeps the order desk. Priya runs the till and the card machine. Arjun keeps the storeroom. Dev owns the recipes and the prices. Ben runs deliveries. Meera sends the receipts and the emails. Omar looks after the front door and everything under the floor.

7a. What is a service, and when is it "micro"?

A service is a program that runs on its own and does one area of the business. Precisely: an independently deployable unit of software that owns its data and exposes its capability only through a network interface.

Why does the word exist at all? To separate a deployment unit from a code unit. A library, a module and a package are code units. They are compiled into somebody else's process. A service has its own process, its own memory and its own lifecycle. It can be restarted without restarting you. The word marks the line where the compiler stops helping.

Priya's till is now a service called payments. It runs as its own process, owns the payments table, and is reached only at POST /payments. That process boundary is why everything you know about communication-protocols suddenly applies to what used to be a method call.

A microservice is a service kept small enough that one team owns all of it. There is no size definition. Anyone who gives you one is guessing, whether it is lines of code, "two pizzas", or "fits in your head". The only definition that survives contact with production is behavioural. A service is microservice-sized when the team that owns it can release it without coordinating with another team. Size is a consequence of that, not a target.

The "micro" exists to mark this style off from the earlier SOA style, where services were large, shared, and coordinated by a central bus. It is not about lines of code. It is about who has to be in the room for a release.

Ben's shipping is 4,000 lines and one team. That is micro enough. Splitting it into shipping-labels and shipping-rates, still both owned by Ben, would add network calls and buy nothing. (known) The arch-patterns lab warned that microservices are "not an upgrade, a tradeoff". This is the sentence that makes that tradeoff checkable.

Independent deployability is the plain sentence "I can ship my thing today without you". Precisely: a new version of one service can be released to production without requiring a simultaneous or ordered release of any other service, and without any other service changing.

This is the word that matters most in the whole lab. It is the only real benefit of the style, and naming it lets you check whether you have it. Every other claimed benefit is either obtainable without splitting, or has to be bought separately. That covers scalability, technology choice and fault isolation. This one is the thing you cannot get from a monolith.

The true test is the release log. Look at the last twenty production releases. If orders and inventory went out together seventeen times, you have one service living in two repositories. That is the Friday-column-rename test from section 5, with the jargon taken out.

7b. Why is coupling not about who calls whom?

(known) From arch-patterns: coupling is how much one thing has to know about another. This lab adds one piece of precision. Coupling is measured by what forces a simultaneous change, not by what calls what.

Read that again with Riverbend in mind. Two counters whose runners visit each other all day, but which have never had to change on the same morning, are less coupled than two counters that never speak and share one stock card.

A distributed monolith is what Riverbend became in section 4. Split into many pieces that still have to move together: all the cost, none of the benefit. Precisely: a system of separately deployed services whose changes still require cross-service coordination, because they share data, share a release train, or are cut along technical layers rather than business capabilities.

Why does that need a name of its own? Because "we have microservices" is a statement about the deployment diagram. This term is the statement about reality. It exists so you can say the diagnosis out loud. Riverbend after section 4 is the example: six deployables, one storeroom, three teams needed for a new cake.

It has a symptom you can measure. Take the last ten features you shipped and count how many services each one had to change. If most features touch three or more, you have this.

(known) Blast radius, also from arch-patterns, is how much of the system a single failure or a single change can damage. This lab's contribution is uncomfortable. The blast radius of a microservice system is not automatically smaller than a monolith's. Sections 10 and 11 show it starting out larger, and show exactly what you must buy to shrink it.

7c. What is the locked door actually made of?

A bounded context is the area within which one particular meaning of a word is true. Precisely, from Domain-Driven Design: a boundary inside which a model and its vocabulary are consistent and unambiguous. Across the boundary the same word may legitimately mean something different, and translation is explicit.

Why does anybody need this term? Because the biggest mistake in boundary-drawing is believing there is one true "Customer" in your business. There is not.

To billing, a Customer is a legal entity with a tax ID and a payment method. To shipping, a Customer is an address and a delivery-window preference. To support, a Customer is a person with a history of complaints. Forcing those into one shared model creates an object nobody can change, because every field belongs to somebody else. The term exists to give you permission to have three Customers.

Asha's orders says "order" and means the customer's intention to buy. Ben's shipping says "order" and means a box with a label on it. They exchange an ID and translate. They do not share a class. This is the answer to "where do I put the door?", which section 10i turns into five concrete rules.

(known by name from arch-patterns.) Database-per-service is what section 4 just bought you. Exactly one service may read or write a given table. Others must go through that service's interface.

Here is the precision that is usually missed. This rule is not about the number of database servers. Two services can share one Postgres instance and still obey it, if they use separate schemas and separate credentials and neither can see the other's tables. It is about who is permitted to know the shape of the data, because the shape is what a rename breaks. Enforce it with database permissions, not with a wiki page. A rule that is only written down is not a rule.

A contract is the promise a service makes about what it will accept and return. Precisely: the observable surface of a service — endpoint paths, request and response shapes, event schemas, error codes, and the behaviour behind them, which means ordering, idempotency and timing. Everything else about the service is private and may change freely.

The word separates what I promised from how I do it. Once that line is drawn, "can I change this?" has a mechanical answer instead of an argument. The contract is the only thing the outside world is entitled to depend on.

Priya's contract is POST /payments {orderId, amountMinor, currency, idempotencyKey} answered by 201 {paymentId, status}. That she stores it in Postgres, retries the card network twice, and writes it in Kotlin is not part of the contract. The idempotency key in that request is part of it, because the caller's retry behaviour depends on it. That makes it a promise rather than a detail. See idempotency.

7d. What is the name for one shop taking down the street?

A cascading failure is one thing breaking, and its breaking breaking the things that were only talking to it — and then their breaking breaking the next ring out.

Precisely: a failure propagates from a dependency to a caller because the caller's finite resources are consumed waiting on that dependency. Those resources are threads, connections, memory or file descriptors. The caller then fails at work that had nothing to do with the dependency.

The term exists to name a difference people keep collapsing. "Payments is down, so you cannot pay" is expected and contained. "Payments is down, so you cannot view your order history" is a cascade: the failure crossed into unrelated functionality. Without the term, people report the second as if it were the first, and the real bug is never found. The worked example is the whole of section 11, with numbers.

You have seen exactly one instance of this already. Failure mode F2 in the distributed-rate-limiting lab, where a slow counter store exhausted request threads while every dashboard stayed green. That was not a rate-limiting quirk. It is the general law of every synchronous call, and this lab derives it.

(known from distributed-rate-limiting) A circuit breaker wraps the calls to one dependency. It counts recent failures. Once they cross a threshold it stops making the calls at all for a while, failing instantly instead of slowly. It has three states. Closed means calls pass. Open means calls fail immediately without being made. Half-open means a trickle is let through to test recovery. Section 10f adds the two things usually got wrong. The first is what the breaker actually protects: the caller's threads, not the dependency. The second is why you need a breaker per dependency rather than one per service.

(known from distributed-rate-limiting) A bulkhead caps how much of a shared resource any one dependency may consume. A dependency going bad can then use up its own quota and no more. It is named after ship compartments: a hull breach floods one compartment, not the vessel. Section 10f deepens this one too.

7e. Which two sums does nobody do correctly in their head?

Availability, serially composed, in plain words: if you need five things to work, and each works 99.9% of the time, you are not at 99.9%.

Here is that sum as a thing at Riverbend. A customer's order needs the price desk, the storeroom, the till, and the front door. If any one of them is shut, there is no sale. So the chance of a sale is the chance all four are open at once, which is each chance multiplied together.

In words: multiply every required service's availability together to get the chain's. In symbols, writing one service's availability as A_i and the whole chain's as A_total: A_total = ∏ A_i. Unavailability adds up, roughly, until the numbers get large.

The term exists because the intuition is wrong in one specific direction. Everybody's gut says "they're all 99.9%, so we're about 99.9%". The arithmetic says 99.5%, which is five times more downtime. The term exists to force the multiplication, and section 10a does it in hours per year.

Tail latency amplification, in plain words: if you have to wait for many things, you wait for the slowest one — and rare slowness stops being rare.

The Riverbend version: Asha sends six runners at once and cannot answer the customer until the last one is back. Each counter is occasionally slow. The chance that Asha waits is the chance that at least one of the six was slow, which is much larger than the chance that any particular one was.

In words: one minus the chance that every single service was fast. In symbols, writing the number of services as N and the chance one of them is slow as p: 1 − (1−p)^N.

Why is that worth a name? Because a service's own p99 stops being the number that matters once it has callers who fan out. Dean and Barroso named this in The Tail at Scale (CACM, 2013). A server that is 1 ms on average but 1 s at p99, touched by a request that fans out to 100 such servers, makes 63% of requests take over a second. A p99 that looks fine locally is a catastrophe globally.

7f. What replaces the phone book and the stack trace?

Service discovery is how one service finds where another one is right now. Precisely: the mechanism that maps a stable logical name such as payments to the current set of healthy network addresses, kept current as instances start, stop, move and fail health checks.

In a monolith the answer was "it is a method, it is at this memory address". Once instances are created and destroyed constantly by an orchestrator, addresses are not something a human can put in a config file. The term names the machine that replaces the config file.

Concretely, payments.riverbend.svc.cluster.local resolves to six pod IPs. A pod is killed, and discovery removes it within about ten seconds. That ten-second window is why callers sometimes get connection refused, which is why retries are legitimate, which brings in idempotency. Section 10e has the precise rule.

Distributed tracing is a shared ID stapled to a request so you can see its whole journey across every service it touched. Precisely: a trace identifier is created at the system edge and propagated on every outbound call. The W3C standard header is traceparent. Each service records timed spans tagged with that trace ID, and a collector reassembles them into one waterfall.

In a monolith, the answer to "why was this slow?" was a stack trace and a profiler. Both stop at the process boundary. Tracing is the replacement, and it is not optional — it is the thing you traded the stack trace for. One Riverbend checkout is 14 spans across 6 services, and section 10h shows that waterfall.

Consumer-driven contract testing is each caller writing down what it actually depends on, so the callee runs those checks before every release. Precisely: consumers publish executable expectations about the provider's contract, called a "pact". The provider's build verifies every published expectation and fails if one is broken. No shared test environment, no end-to-end suite.

This one exists to replace what Dev destroyed in section 4a. In a monolith the compiler told you who you broke. In a distributed system nothing does, unless you build this. The term names the compiler's successor.

8. What two pictures should you carry?

In one breath: Riverbend is now a row of separate shops on one street. That picture gets the layout right and the behaviour badly wrong, so you need a second picture beside it.

8a. What does the market street look like?

Carry this picture around.

Riverbend is no longer one shop. It is a row of separate shops on one street. Each shop has its own staff, its own locked storeroom, its own opening hours, and its own sign on the door saying what it will sell you.

When Dev's cake shop needs to know the price of coffee, it sends a runner next door. The runner might get lost. The coffee shop might be closed. The runner might have to queue.

And here is the sentence to remember. Dev's shop has only three runners. While all three are waiting at the coffee shop's counter, nobody is left to serve cake.

That last sentence is the entire failure section of this lab, in advance.

8b. What does the same street look like to an engineer?

Precisely, and with the story still on stage.

Each service is a process with private memory and a private data store. That is a shop with a locked storeroom.

The only thing crossing a boundary is a message over a network. It may be delayed, lost, duplicated, or answered by a machine that has since died. That is the runner.

Every service has a finite pool of workers — threads, or connections, or goroutines, some countable thing. A request occupies one worker for its whole duration, including the time it spends waiting for a downstream reply. Those are Dev's three runners.

Now the one piece of arithmetic that generates the rest of the lab. Meet it first as a thing on the street.

Dev sends a runner next door every so often. Each trip takes a while. How many runners are standing at the coffee counter at any given moment? However often he sends one, multiplied by how long a trip lasts. Send one every second, let each trip last three seconds, and three runners are permanently away.

In words: the number of workers busy at any moment equals how many requests arrive each second, multiplied by how long each one takes.

In symbols, and engineers call this Little's Law: workers in use = arrival rate × time each call takes.

That one equation generates every failure in section 11. It is the reason timeouts, retries, bulkheads and breakers exist at all, and you will watch it produce each of them in turn.

The last piece is the sign on the door. A service's contract is a versioned promise. Everything behind it is free to change. Nothing in front of it may change without a migration.

8d. Where does the story lie to you?

Every analogy is a loan. Here is what you owe. Read this part, do not skip it.

1. Real shops do not take each other down; services do. On a real street, the coffee shop being slow does not close the cake shop. In software it does. The runners are not people who can be reassigned. They are a hard-capped pool, and a blocked call holds one until it returns or times out. The cascade has no equivalent on the street, and it is the single most important behaviour in this lab. If your intuition is coming from the street picture, you will be surprised by section 11. That is the point.

2. A shop being "slow" is mildly annoying; a service being slow is worse than it being dead. A closed shop sends the runner straight back, and one second is wasted. A shop with a huge queue keeps the runner for five minutes. Dead is cheap. Sick is expensive. This inverts most people's intuition, and you will see the arithmetic in 10c.

3. Runners do not multiply. Retries do. If a shop is slow, a real street does not respond by sending three times as many runners. Software does exactly that, by default, because retrying is the standard reaction to a failed call. So the response to overload is more load, aimed precisely at the thing that is already dying. That is 10d.

4. There is no such thing as a partly-completed purchase on the street. If the cake shop takes your money and the box shop has no boxes, a human sorts it out. Across services there is no shared transaction to roll back. You learned that in two-phase-commit and its answer in saga. The street picture quietly implies an atomicity that does not exist.

5. The street is not a picture of correctness, only of shape. Everything about ordering, duplication, staleness and partial failure has to come from the engineering model. Use the street to remember the layout. Use Little's Law and the availability product to reason about behaviour.

10a. What is the first bill for splitting?

Version one is what everybody builds. POST /checkout arrives at Omar's gateway, which calls Asha's orders. Asha then calls Dev's catalog for prices, Arjun's inventory to reserve stock, Priya's payments to charge the card, Ben's shipping to book a courier, and Meera's notifications to send the receipt.

Each of those is a blocking HTTP call. Each one is required to succeed before checkout returns 201 Created.

It works. It is easy to reason about. It is also the most fragile thing you will ever ship, and here is the first reason.

For checkout to succeed, every service in the chain must be up. So availability multiplies. The table below is the whole argument of this subsection. Look at the right-hand column, and compare the first row with the last.

Services required Availability Downtime per year
1 (the old monolith) 99.900% 8.8 hours
2 (gateway, orders) 99.800% 17.5 hours
3 99.700% 26.3 hours
4 99.601% 35.0 hours
5 (gateway, orders, catalog, inventory, payments) 99.501% 43.7 hours

Where does 43.7 come from? Three steps, in words first and symbols second.

Step In words The sum
1 five services, each up 99.9% of the time, all needed at once 0.999^5 = 0.995010

3 multiply that share by the hours in a year 0.004990 × 8760 hours = 43.7 hours

Read that as a sentence. You took a system that was down 8.8 hours a year, split it into five equally reliable pieces, changed nothing else, and are now down 43.7 hours a year. A five-fold increase in outage, bought with a large amount of work.

🔮 Predict: Someone proposes fixing this by running three replicas of every service. Does that get you back to 8.8 hours a year?

Reveal

No, and the reason matters more than the answer.

If you said yes, you are treating that 99.9% as if it were made of one thing. It is not. Split it into causes:

  • Machine and network failure — a node dies, a disk fills, an availability zone blips. Replication genuinely fixes this: three replicas across three zones make simultaneous loss very unlikely.
  • Bad deploys — a release with a bug. Replicas do not help; every replica runs the same bad code. Only canary or blue/green deploys help, and they reduce it, not remove it.
  • Dependency failure — the service is up but its database or its downstream is not. Replicas do not help at all.
  • Overload — replicas help only if the bottleneck is CPU, and not at all if it is a shared database or a shared downstream.

In most real services, deploys and dependencies dominate. So redundancy lowers the number, and cannot take it to zero. The honest conclusion of this whole subsection is uncomfortable and worth memorising:

Splitting a system multiplies the number of independent things that must be working. You can pay some of that back with redundancy and canaries — you cannot pay all of it back. If you want availability, you must remove services from the required set, not just make each one better.

"Remove services from the required set" is 10g, and it is the real answer.

10b. Which of these calls must really succeed?

The first refinement arrives before any code changes. Look again at Asha's chain. Ask, for each call, one question. If this fails, must the customer's checkout fail?

  • Dev's catalog gives the price. Yes, required. You cannot charge an unknown amount.
  • Arjun's inventory reserves the stock. Yes, required, if you promise not to oversell.
  • Priya's payments charges the card. Arguably required, though see 10g.
  • Ben's shipping books a courier. No. You can book the courier in the next five minutes. The customer does not need it to have happened before the page says "thank you".
  • Meera's notifications sends the receipt email. Obviously no.

The set of services that genuinely must succeed before you answer the customer is called the critical path. Everything not on it is a candidate to be moved off — done later, asynchronously, by a message.

This is the first and biggest availability lever, and we apply it properly in 10g. Note now what it costs. Once shipping happens after the response, there is a window in which the order exists and no courier is booked. That window is a consistency window, and you already know its shape.

10c. Why is a slow counter worse than a closed one?

This is the most important subsection in the lab.

Priya's payments is not down. It is sick. Its card-network provider is degraded, and calls that took 20 ms now take 5 seconds and eventually succeed.

Every dashboard is green. payments is returning 200 OK. Its error rate is zero.

🔮 Predict: Asha's orders has 200 threads and takes 100 requests per second. Priya's payments has gone from 20 ms to 5 s. What happens to orders? Pick one before revealing.

(a) Checkout gets slower, about 5 seconds instead of instant, but works. (b) Checkout fails, but the rest of orders (order history, order lookup) keeps working. (c) The whole of orders stops responding, including endpoints that never touch payments.

Reveal

(c). And it takes two seconds to happen.

If you said (a), you are imagining runners who can be borrowed back. If you said (b), you are imagining a separate pool per endpoint. There is one pool, and it is shared by every endpoint on the service.

Use Little's Law. In words: workers busy at any moment equals how many requests arrive each second, multiplied by how long each one takes.

Healthy, a hundred arrivals a second each holding a thread for two hundredths of a second needs two threads. Asha is nearly idle. Sick, a hundred arrivals a second each holding a thread for five seconds needs five hundred threads. Asha has two hundred.

Threads fill at 100 per second, so the pool is full two seconds after payments gets sick. From that moment, every new request waits for a free thread and gets none. That includes GET /orders/123, the health check, and the order history page.

Requests queue. The queue grows without bound, because arrivals exceed completions. Latency goes to infinity. The load balancer's health check times out and takes orders out of rotation, which sends its traffic to the other orders replicas — which then die the same way, faster.

The blast radius is one degraded external card network taking out Asha's entire order service, including read-only pages that touch nothing but their own database.

That is a cascading failure, and the mechanism is not mysterious. The thread pool is a resource shared by all endpoints, and one dependency was allowed to consume all of it.

Priya's till In words The sum Threads needed Asha has
Healthy, 20 ms 100 arrivals a second, each holding a thread 0.02 s 100/s × 0.02 s = 2 threads 2 200
Sick, 5 s 100 arrivals a second, each holding a thread 5 s 100/s × 5 s = 500 threads 500 200

10d. Does a timeout fix it, and does a retry help?

Refinement one is a timeout. Cap the call at 1 second. Little's Law again, in words: a hundred arrivals a second, each holding a thread for at most one second, needs a hundred threads. That is under the 200 Asha has.

Setting In words The sum Threads needed Asha has
1-second timeout, no retries 100 arrivals a second, each capped at 1 s 100/s × 1 s = 100 threads 100 200
1-second timeout, 3 attempts each checkout may now hold a thread for 3 × 1 s = 3 s 100/s × 3 s = 300 threads 300 200

Read the first row. orders survives. Order history keeps working. Checkout fails, which is correct, because payments really is broken.

A timeout is not an error-handling nicety. A timeout is the thing that converts an unbounded resource consumption into a bounded one. That is why a call without a timeout is a bug, always, even in code that looks fine.

Rule worth keeping: every synchronous call needs a timeout, and the timeout must be chosen from the caller's thread budget, not from the callee's normal latency. The question is not "how long does payments usually take?" but "how many threads am I willing to have stuck here?" — which Little's Law turns into a number.

Now refinement two, the retry, and this is the trap. Checkout is failing, so the obvious next step is to retry: three attempts before giving up.

🔮 Predict: the timeout is 1 s and retries are 3 attempts. Priya's payments is still sick at 5 s. Is orders still safe?

Reveal

No. You have re-created the original failure, and you have made payments worse at the same time.

Read the second row of the table above. Each checkout now occupies a thread for up to three seconds. A hundred arrivals a second, each holding a thread three seconds, needs three hundred threads. Asha has two hundred. The pool saturates again, in about four seconds instead of two.

If you guessed "safe, because the timeout is still one second", you were budgeting per attempt. The thread does not care how many attempts there were. It cares about total time held.

And look at what happened on the other side. Priya was receiving 100 requests per second. Now she receives 300. You have tripled the load on a service that is failing because it is overloaded. This is a retry storm, and it is how a recoverable five-minute degradation becomes a two-hour outage. The moment payments starts to recover, the backlog of retrying callers immediately knocks it over again.

The fixes, all of which you need:

  • Budget the retries in the caller's time, not in attempts. The timeout that matters is the total one: 1 second for the whole operation including retries, not 1 second per attempt.
  • Exponential backoff with jitter. Without jitter, all failed callers retry at the same instant, and you have built a synchroniser. It is the same thundering herd you met in distributed-rate-limiting, where a dishonest Retry-After header made your own response the clock everyone retried on.
  • Retry only what is safe to retry — see 10e.
  • A retry budget: cap retries at a small fraction, say 10%, of total traffic, so retries can never multiply load. This is what Google's SRE book recommends and what service meshes implement.

Run both scenarios. Tab 3, S3 (timeout) then S4 (timeout + retries). S4 shows both gauges: the caller's pool refilling and the load arriving at payments tripling.

10e. When is a retry actually safe?

The rule is precise, and it comes straight from idempotency:

You may retry freely when you know the request was never received. You must have an idempotency key when you do not know.

What you observed Was it received? Safe to retry?
Connection refused / DNS failure Certainly not Yes, always
TCP reset before request sent Certainly not Yes
503 Service Unavailable from the server Received, not processed Yes (the server is telling you so)
429 Too Many Requests Received, rejected Yes, after Retry-After
Timeout Unknown Only with an idempotency key
500 Internal Server Error Unknown — it may have charged the card and then failed Only with an idempotency key

The timeout row is the dangerous one, and it is the whole reason the idempotency lab exists. A timed-out POST /payments may have charged the customer. Retrying it without a key charges them twice.

The key is minted by the caller before the first attempt and reused verbatim on every retry. That is what makes the ambiguity harmless: Priya's service claims the key atomically with the effect, and replays the stored response.

Service discovery makes the top row common rather than exotic. When an instance is killed, discovery takes several seconds to notice, and during that window callers get connection refused. Those retries are free and correct. Do not let the danger of the timeout row scare you out of the safety of the first row.

10f. What do a bulkhead and a breaker each protect?

Refinement three is a pair of tools. The timeout bounded the damage. It did not contain it. With a 1-second timeout and no retries, 100 of Asha's 200 threads are still tied up in payments. That is half her capacity spent on a call that is going to fail.

A bulkhead caps how many threads any one dependency may hold. Give Priya's payments a quota of 40. Now three things are true. At most 40 threads can ever be inside a payments call. The other 160 threads remain for everything else, and they keep working perfectly: order history, order lookup, health checks. And calls beyond the quota are rejected immediately rather than queued.

The bulkhead's job is isolation. It makes "payments is broken" mean "payments is broken" instead of "orders is down".

A circuit breaker goes further. After enough consecutive failures it stops making the calls at all.

  • Closed — normal. Calls pass through; failures are counted.
  • Open — after, say, 20 consecutive failures or a 50% failure rate over a rolling window, the breaker trips. Every call now returns an error immediately, in microseconds, without touching the network.
  • Half-open — after a cooldown, say 30 s, let a small number of probe calls through. If they succeed, close. If not, open again.

Little's Law once more, with the breaker open. In words: a hundred arrivals a second, each held for twenty microseconds, needs no threads worth counting.

Breaker state In words The sum Threads held
Open 100 arrivals a second, each returning in about 0.00002 s 100/s × 0.00002 s ≈ 0 threads none worth counting

The dependency is now free.

Two things about breakers are commonly got wrong, and they are the reason this term needed re-teaching rather than a refresher.

First, the breaker protects the caller, not the callee. People describe it as "protecting payments from load". That is a side effect. Its purpose is to stop Asha spending her own finite capacity on calls that are going to fail. It is a resource-management device wearing an error-handling costume.

Second, there must be one breaker per dependency, not one per service. If Asha has a single breaker covering all outbound calls, Priya failing opens the breaker on Dev's catalog too. You have then manufactured a cascade with the tool you bought to prevent one. The state is per-dependency — and in the real world, per dependency and endpoint, because POST /payments failing does not mean GET /payments/{id} is failing.

Why do you need all four tools? Because each does one job, and none substitutes for another.

Tool Bounds Without it
Timeout how long one call may hold a thread unbounded occupancy → pool exhausted
Retry (budgeted, jittered) recovery from transient loss avoidable failures; unbudgeted → retry storm
Bulkhead how much of the pool one dependency may hold one sick dependency starves all endpoints
Circuit breaker how long you keep paying for a known-broken dependency you keep paying the timeout on every request, forever

You met exactly this quartet in the rate-limiting lab, as the four siblings its design "needed to survive". Now you have the derivation of why it is exactly these four, and Little's Law is the derivation.

Watch the gauge split. Tab 3, scenarios S5 (bulkhead) and S6 (breaker). In S5 the pool gauge splits into a payments compartment and everything else. In S6 the latency of a failing checkout drops from 1000 ms to about 0.02 ms when the breaker opens. The load arriving at payments then collapses to the probe trickle, which is what lets Priya recover.

10g. How do you take work off the critical path?

Refinement four is the last one. Everything so far has been damage control. This is the actual cure, and it uses three patterns you already own.

Rewrite checkout so that Asha does the minimum that must be true before answering the customer, and nothing else.

  1. In one local database transaction, orders writes the order row and an outbox row saying OrderPlaced. That is the transactional-outbox pattern. It exists precisely because writing the row and telling the world are two physical acts that can be interrupted between.
  2. orders returns 201 Created to the customer with status PENDING.
  3. A relay drains the outbox to the broker, at-least-once.
  4. Priya, Arjun, Ben and Meera consume the event and do their work. Each is an idempotent consumer — mandatory, because at-least-once means duplicates are certain, not possible.
  5. If payment fails, a compensating action cancels the order and releases the reservation. That is a saga: a sequence of local transactions, each with a business-level undo, replacing a distributed commit you cannot have.

What does that buy, in the same arithmetic as before? The critical path is now Omar's gateway plus Asha's orders, and nothing else.

Design Required services Availability Downtime/year
Monolith 1 99.900% 8.8 h
v1 — everything synchronous 5 99.501% 43.7 h
v4 — async off the critical path 2 99.800% 17.5 h

And here is the honest part. 17.5 hours is still twice the monolith's 8.8. You cannot get back to the monolith's number by cleverness. You have genuinely added a required component, Omar's gateway, and a network.

What you can do is three things. Shrink the required set to its true minimum, which is what you just did. Make each required member more reliable than 99.9% with replication and canaries, remembering the ceiling that 10a's prediction box explained. And accept that the remaining gap is part of the price you agreed to pay for independent deployability.

If nobody ever tells you that last sentence, you will spend years believing you have implemented microservices wrong.

There is also a part that is not free. Moving work off the critical path moves it. It does not delete it.

  • The customer sees PENDING, so the interface must be honest about it. "Order placed, payment confirming" is a product decision, not an engineering detail.
  • Payment can now fail after the customer was told the order was placed. The compensation is visible to a human: an email from Meera saying the order was cancelled. You traded a synchronous rejection for an asynchronous apology.
  • Stock can be oversold in the window between order and reservation, unless you reserve synchronously. That is why Arjun's inventory is often the one call that legitimately stays on the critical path even when everything else moves off.

That last bullet is the general rule. Whatever must be exactly true at the moment you answer the customer stays synchronous. Everything else goes async.

10i. Where do the boundaries actually go?

Everything above assumed the cut was already made in a sensible place. Making it is the hardest and least mechanical part of this topic. Here are the five rules that survive contact with production, each with the failure it prevents.

Rule 1 — Cut around things that change together. If two services are almost always released together, they are one service that you have made harder to work on. This is measurable: read your last twenty production releases and count co-deployments.

Rule 2 — Cut around data ownership: exactly one writer. Every table has exactly one service that may write it. By default only that service may read it. This is the rule from section 6, and it is the one that prevents the distributed monolith. Enforce it with database grants; a rule that lives only in a wiki is not a rule.

Rule 3 — Never cut through an invariant that must hold exactly. Suppose Riverbend requires "stock never goes negative" to be true at every instant. Then reserving stock cannot be split across two services, because there is no distributed transaction to make it atomic. Two-phase-commit is the option you rejected, and a saga's compensations are undo rather than rollback, so intermediate states are visible. So: anything that must be atomically consistent must live inside one service. That is the same reasoning as a DDD aggregate boundary, one level up. This rule is also the honest escape hatch. If two candidate services would share a strict invariant, do not split them. The invariant wins.

Rule 4 — Cut around teams (Conway's law) (known). A service with two owning teams has none. If your organisation chart cannot be laid over your service diagram, one of them will bend, and it will not be the organisation chart.

Rule 5 — Never cut along technical layers. A "validation service", a "database service", a "business logic service" — these guarantee that every single feature touches every single service, which is rule 1 violated by construction. The cut is by business capability (ordering, paying, shipping), never by technical role.

How do you measure whether your cut is good?

Take your last ten shipped features and build this matrix. Count how many services each one had to change.

Feature Services touched (good cut) Services touched (layer cut)
Add gift-wrap option orders orders-api, orders-logic, orders-data, ui
Support Apple Pay payments payments-api, payments-logic, payments-data
Show delivery estimate shipping, orders 5 services
Change refund window payments 4 services

If the median feature touches more than one service, your boundaries are wrong, and no amount of tooling will rescue it. This number is the single most useful architectural metric you can collect, and almost nobody collects it.

What is the chatty-boundary trap?

A correct boundary can still be badly shaped.

Asha's order-history page shows 50 orders. Each one carries a product name owned by Dev's catalog.

🔮 Predict: the obvious implementation asks Dev once per order. Fifty calls at 5 ms each. What is the real cost — the 250 ms, or something else?

Reveal

Both, and the something else is worse. Fifty round trips at 5 ms each is 250 ms of pure waiting. But it is also fifty chances to fail, and by Little's Law it is fifty times the thread-occupancy per page view.

If you said "250 ms, and that is acceptable", you priced the latency and ignored the availability. A page that needs fifty successful calls is far more fragile than a page that needs one.

This is the N+1 problem you know from communication-protocols, now with a network in each iteration.

There are three real fixes, in increasing order of commitment.

  1. Batch the call: GET /products?ids=1,2,3,… in one round trip. Cheapest, and usually enough.
  2. Cache locally with a TTL, accepting a consistency window.
  3. Event-carried state transfer: Dev publishes ProductRenamed events, and Asha keeps her own small read-model copy of the product names she needs. Now the order-history page makes zero cross-service calls, and Dev being down does not affect it at all. This is cqrs's projection idea applied across a service boundary, and it is how you take a service off the critical path for reads rather than writes.

Option 3 is powerful and it is not free. Asha now holds a copy of Dev's data, which will be stale, and she owns a projector that can fall behind or break.

The rule is the one from your eventual-consistency lab. A copy like this is for display, never for a decision. Pricing an order from a cached catalog copy is how you sell things at last month's price.

ENGINEERING MODEL

11. What are the nine ways this system fails?

In one breath: Nine things go wrong at Riverbend. Two are about time, two are about promises, three are about copies and blindness, and the last one is about people. Only the first two have anything to do with speed.

Each failure below is a short story: what Asha does to cause it, what happens and why, what the system does about it, and what a real on-call engineer sees. The table at the end lines all nine up side by side.

F1 — Why does a slow dependency take out the whole caller?

Asha turns Priya's latency from 20 ms up to 5 s and leaves traffic at 100 requests a second. She watches the orders thread gauge on tab 3, scenario S2.

A hundred arrivals a second, each holding a thread for five seconds, needs five hundred threads. Two hundred exist. The pool is full in two seconds. Every endpoint on orders dies, including ones that never call payments, because the thread pool is shared across all endpoints.

What does the system do about it? Nothing, by default. That is exactly why the mitigations in 10f exist.

Data correctness survives — nothing is written wrongly. What is lost is the availability of Asha's entire order service, not just checkout.

In production the alert fires on orders, not on payments, so the wrong team is paged. The tell is orders p99 climbing while its own database sits idle, and the trace showing all the time inside one downstream span. Prevented by timeout plus bulkhead; recovered by the breaker.

F2 — How does a retry turn a blip into an outage?

Asha enables 3 retries with no backoff while Priya is degraded. She watches two gauges on tab 3, scenario S4: her own pool, and the arrival rate at payments.

Caller occupancy triples, because 100 × 3 s = 300 > 200. And Priya now receives 300 requests a second instead of 100. The service that was struggling is now being overloaded by its own callers.

By default nothing responds. With a retry budget, the amplification is capped.

Nothing structural survives here. What is lost is the recovery itself: every time payments comes back, the retry backlog knocks it over again, turning a five-minute degradation into hours.

In production the signature is unmistakable once you know it. The load graph on the failing service rises during its own outage, and the recovery oscillates. Prevented by total-operation timeouts, exponential backoff with jitter, and a retry budget capping retries at about 10% of traffic. Service meshes implement all three, which is a large part of why they exist.

F3 — What happens when you rename a field somebody depended on?

Asha renames the event field status to state and adds a new value AWAITING_FULFILMENT. She watches tab 1, "rename a field", with private stores selected — the consumers that break are now visible, which is the whole point.

Consumers deserialize into a fixed shape. A removed field becomes a null. An unknown enum value is an exception, or a silent default. Neither is caught at compile time, in either service.

So the consumer crashes. Or, worse, silently mis-handles.

The producer survives, which is fine, and Asha has no idea anything happened. What is lost is the consumer's correctness, possibly quietly.

In production this is handled by expand/contract migration, also called parallel change. It is always three deploys and never one:

  1. Expand — add state, keep writing status too. Deploy. Nothing breaks.
  2. Migrate — every consumer moves to state, one at a time, on its own schedule. This step is only possible if you can enumerate your consumers, which is exactly what a private database buys you and a shared one destroys.
  3. Contract — once no consumer reads status, stop writing it. Deploy.

Three other things help. Consumer-driven contract tests run in the provider's CI, and they are the compiler's replacement. A schema registry (known) enforces compatibility rules on every event schema. And the receiver practises tolerant reading: ignore unknown fields, never fail on an added one. The rule underneath all of it: additive changes are free, removals and renames are never free.

F4 — Why is a shared database an invisible dependency?

Two services read the same table, and one of them alters it. Asha watches tab 1 in "shared store" mode, where the breakage list fills up with services she did not know were readers.

A shared table is an interface with no version, no owner and no consumer list. There is no artefact anywhere that records who reads it.

The breakage happens at runtime. Usually in a batch job at 3am. Usually in the service least likely to be looked at.

Nothing you can rely on survives. What is lost is independent deployability itself — this failure is the distributed monolith of section 4.

In production the only real fix is structural: separate schemas, separate credentials, and a hard rule enforced by grants. The migration path off a shared database is the strangler fig (known). Put the new owning service in front of the table, move readers to its API one at a time, then revoke their database access. The revoke is the step that actually completes the migration. Without it, you have added a service and kept the coupling.

F5 — What happens when the money leaves and the courier does not?

Asha kills Ben's shipping after Priya's payments has succeeded. She watches the saga trace: the compensation running backwards.

🔮 Predict: the customer has been charged and no courier is booked. What does the system roll back?

Reveal

Nothing, because there is no "back". If you expected a rollback, you are still carrying the monolith's transaction with you. The payment is a fact in another company's system. It cannot be un-happened.

What runs instead is a compensation, which is a new forward action that undoes the business effect: refund issued, reservation released, order cancelled, customer emailed by Meera. That is a saga.

Eventual consistency of the business outcome survives — the system ends in a valid state. What is lost is atomicity and isolation. The intermediate state, paid but not shipped, was real and was visible to anyone looking.

In production, compensations must themselves be idempotent and retried forever, which means you need a durable saga log. A compensation that fails is an operational incident with a human at the end of it, not a silent retry. The refund is also visibly slower than the charge, so this is a customer-facing design decision rather than an implementation detail.

F6 — How does a stale read reach a decision?

Asha prices an item from her locally cached copy of Dev's catalog. To watch this one you re-read the consistency-window discussion in cqrs and eventual-consistency.

Every cross-service read is a read of a copy, and every copy is behind. Usually by milliseconds. During a projector outage, by hours.

Nothing responds, because nothing detects it. A stale value is a perfectly valid value.

Availability survives — that is what the copy bought Asha. What is lost is the correctness of anything computed from it.

In production the rule is the one you already know from the eventual-consistency lab, stated exactly: a replicated or projected read is for display, never for a decision. Prices, balances, entitlements and stock levels used in a write path must be read from their owner, or guarded by a conditional write. And staleness must be monitored by age, not by lag count — a projector that has died produces no events at all, so a queue-depth alarm stays silent forever.

F7 — What does an incident look like with no trace ID?

One service forgets to forward traceparent on an outbound call. To watch it, look at the waterfall in 10h, then imagine it without the IDs.

Each service's logs are individually correct and mutually unrelatable. "Checkout was slow at 14:32" cannot be turned into a single request's story.

There is no response, because this is a missing capability rather than a fault.

The system survives perfectly. What is lost is your ability to diagnose it, which during an incident is the same thing.

In production, propagation is done by shared instrumentation or by the service mesh's sidecar, never by hand — precisely because one service forgetting breaks everything below it. The operational rule: trace-ID propagation is a platform responsibility, and its coverage should itself be monitored.

F8 — Why is the shared low-level counter the most dangerous one?

Dev's catalog is used by Asha's orders, by search, by the website and by the mobile app. Asha makes catalog slow rather than payments on tab 3, with bulkheads off.

A dependency shared by many callers converts one failure into many simultaneous ones. Worse, all of those callers retry at once, so Dev receives a synchronised surge exactly when he is weakest.

The response is each caller's own breaker and bulkhead, acting independently.

Callers that isolated the dependency survive. Everything that did not, is lost.

In production the shared low-level service is the highest-risk node in your architecture. It needs the highest availability target of anything you own. It should be more replicated, more cached, and more aggressively load-shed than its callers. Its callers should each hold a local cache that can serve stale data when it is unreachable — the control-plane rule from distributed-rate-limiting and http-caching. And it should be the first place you look when several unrelated services alert at once.

F9 — What breaks when a service has no owner?

A team reorganises, and a service it owned is not reassigned. You watch your incident channel, in about six months.

Dependencies keep being added to it. Its libraries age. Its alerts go to a rota nobody reads. Eventually it fails in a way nobody present understands.

There is no response. This failure mode is not technical and has no technical mitigation.

The code survives, and runs fine. What is lost is the whole premise. Independent deployability was bought to give teams autonomy. An unowned service has no team, so it has no autonomy to give, and it is now strictly worse than a module in the monolith — which at least had the codebase's owners.

In production you need a service registry with a named owning team, checked in CI. And you need a genuine willingness to re-merge services whose owners have gone. This is the least-discussed and most common failure of microservice architectures. It is why the arch-patterns lab's line, microservices are not an upgrade, was aimed at organisations rather than at code.

12. What do you get, what do you not, and what did you give up?

In one breath: Everything below has been earned by a section above. Nothing is listed for completeness. One benefit is unobtainable any other way; almost everything else on the list is a cost.

What does this architecture guarantee, if you do the work?

Independent deployability. Each service can be released alone. This requires rules 1 to 5 in 10i, a private database, and versioned contracts. It is the only benefit you cannot obtain any other way, and it is the reason to do this.

Independent scalability. The service under load can be scaled without scaling the rest. Real, and genuinely useful when one component's resource profile is wildly different from the others'. That is the fact that made Dev's catalog, which is read-heavy and cacheable, worth separating from Priya's payments, which is write-heavy, latency-bound and compliance-bound.

Fault isolation. Purchasable, not automatic. Section 11 is entirely about the fact that the default is the opposite. You get it once you have timeouts, bulkheads, breakers, and an async critical path. Not before.

Technology heterogeneity. Each service picks its own language and store. Real, and a mixed blessing: every added runtime multiplies your operational surface. Treat it as an escape hatch for genuine mismatches, not a policy.

Team autonomy. The organisational payoff, and the actual goal. Only real when every service has exactly one owning team, which is F9.

What does it explicitly not guarantee?

Atomicity across services. Gone, permanently. There is no distributed transaction. There is a saga with business-level compensations, and intermediate states are visible to users. This is a product decision.

Isolation across services. Gone. Another request can observe your saga half-done. If you need to prevent that, you need a semantic lock: a status field that means "do not touch". Building one is application-level work.

Read consistency. Every cross-service read is stale. The window is usually milliseconds and occasionally hours.

Higher availability. Lower, by default, by exactly the product in 10a. You buy some back by shrinking the critical path and adding redundancy. You do not get all of it back.

Lower latency. Strictly higher. You replaced method calls with network round trips and added serialisation at every hop. The 812 ms trace in 10h contains real network time that a monolith would not have spent.

Lower cost. Higher, on every axis: more machines, more observability, more platform engineers, more time per feature that spans services.

A correct decomposition. Nothing in the architecture prevents a bad cut. A bad cut gives you a distributed monolith, which is worse than what you had.

Simplicity. Complexity is moved, not removed. It leaves the codebase, where a compiler and a debugger could see it, and enters the network and the operations layer, where they cannot.

What did you permanently give up?

This is the part people discover too late, and it is worth stating starkly.

You had You now have Replacement you must build
A compiler that found every caller of a renamed field Runtime nulls and enum failures Consumer-driven contract tests, schema registry, expand/contract migrations
A stack trace across the whole request A stack trace that stops at your process Distributed tracing with propagated trace context
Database transactions across your data Local transactions only Sagas, outbox, idempotent consumers
Reading the code to understand behaviour Behaviour that only exists at runtime, in the interactions Tracing, service catalogue, architecture decision records
"It works on my machine" for the whole system You cannot run the whole system Contract tests, ephemeral environments, testing in production with canaries
A function call that cannot fail halfway A network call with three outcomes: success, failure, and unknown Timeouts, retries with idempotency keys, breakers, bulkheads

The bottom row is the deepest one. A method call has two outcomes; a network call has three. The third one is I do not know whether that happened, and it is where all of distributed systems lives.

That third outcome is why idempotency exists, why two-phase-commit blocks, why flp-impossibility holds, and why saga's compensations must be idempotent too. Splitting a monolith is the act of introducing that third outcome into places where it did not exist.

Which promises are safety, and which are liveness?

Use the frame from flp-impossibility and eventual-consistency.

Safety means "nothing wrong ever happens". At Riverbend that is: no service ever reads or writes another's data; no order is charged twice, thanks to idempotency keys; no saga ends in an invalid business state, thanks to compensations. These hold at every instant. A single observation can falsify them, so they can be tested.

Liveness means "something good eventually happens". At Riverbend that is: every outbox row is eventually published; every saga eventually reaches a terminal state; every projection eventually catches up. None of these can be falsified by any finite observation, because the answer is always "wait longer". That is precisely why they must be monitored by age, never by a count. The three ages to watch are the oldest unpublished outbox row, the oldest non-terminal saga, and the projector's time-since-last-progress.

That distinction is not decoration. A queue-depth alarm on an outbox is a liveness alarm implemented as a safety check, and it goes silent exactly when the relay dies. Alarm on the age of the oldest row.

What is an SLO, and what is an error budget?

An SLO, a service level objective, is a target for a measurable property of the service, over a window. Riverbend's is: "99.9% of checkout requests succeed in under 500 ms, measured over 28 days".

An error budget is the complement of that. 0.1% of 28 days is about 40 minutes of allowed failure.

The budget is what makes availability a decision rather than an aspiration. While budget remains, ship faster. When it is exhausted, stop shipping features and fix reliability.

In a microservice system every service has its own SLO. The arithmetic of 10a is exactly how you derive what each one must be, from what the customer-facing SLO promises.

What does the on-call engineer actually see at 3am?

At 03:00 Omar's phone says orders p99 > 2s. Here is the sequence a competent responder follows. Every step of it is something this lab explained.

  1. Open the trace, not the logs. Find a slow checkout and look at the waterfall from 10h. The time is in one span, and that names the culprit service in seconds rather than hours.
  2. Check whether the culprit is slow or failing. Green error rate plus climbing latency is F1 — a sick dependency, not a dead one. Green dashboards are the signature, not evidence of health.
  3. Check the breaker states. An open breaker means the system already contained it, and the alert is about degraded functionality rather than an outage. A closed breaker on a failing dependency means the thresholds are wrong.
  4. Check the caller's saturation, not just the callee's. Thread pool utilisation, connection pool utilisation, queue depth. This is the number that predicts a cascade, and it is the one most teams do not graph.
  5. Check whether load is rising during the outage. If it is, you are in F2, a retry storm. The correct action is to shed load, not to add capacity. Shedding load means rejecting a fraction of incoming requests immediately, so that the rest can succeed.
  6. Check liveness by age, not by count: oldest unpublished outbox row, oldest non-terminal saga, projector lag age.

Four graphs are worth having on the wall for every service, and none of them is CPU: request rate, error rate, latency distribution (p50/p99), and resource saturation (thread/connection pool utilisation). Saturation is the leading indicator. The other three tell you it has already happened.

What did this lab deliberately leave out?

Stated plainly, so you know the shape of what you do not yet know.

  • Security between services. Who is allowed to call whom, service identity, mTLS, propagating the user's authorisation across six hops without letting any service forge it. This is a large topic and this lab has none of it.
  • Data migration at scale. Extracting a table from a monolith while it is serving traffic — dual writes, backfills, verification, cutover. The strangler fig in F4 is one paragraph where reality is one to two quarters.
  • Testing strategy. The test pyramid inverts: end-to-end tests across a system you cannot run locally are the wrong tool. Contract tests, component tests with stubbed dependencies, and testing in production with canaries replace them.
  • Local development. "Run the system on my laptop" stops being possible at around a dozen services. What replaces it is a real cost nobody budgets for: remote dev environments, stub services, telepresence-style proxying.
  • Multi-region. Everything in this lab assumes one region. Cross-region brings back cap-theorem and pacelc at every boundary.
  • Cost. Both infrastructure and the platform team required to operate all of the above. A rough rule from practice: you need dedicated platform capacity before you need microservices, which is why the honest advice for a ten-person company is a modular monolith.

What did the industry actually do, including the retreats?

Real, dated, and worth knowing — because the retreats are the more useful half.

  • Amazon, ~2002. The internal mandate that all teams expose data and functionality only through service interfaces, with no back doors. Widely repeated, most famously in Steve Yegge's 2011 public post, and anecdotal — it is not a published Amazon document. But it is a precise statement of this lab's section 6, twenty years early.
  • Netflix, 2010s. The public reference implementation of the style, and the origin of Hystrix, whose entire job was the breaker, bulkhead and timeout quartet of 10f. Hystrix was later put into maintenance mode in favour of Resilience4j and mesh-level implementations. The pattern moved from library to platform.
  • Segment, 2018. Publicly documented the re-merge of a system of over a hundred services back into a monolith, in "Goodbye Microservices", because the per-service operational overhead exceeded the benefit at their team size.
  • Uber, 2020. Published Domain-Oriented Microservice Architecture, a deliberate move from roughly 2,200 fine-grained services toward coarser domains with defined gateways. A retreat from over-splitting, by the company usually cited as the extreme of splitting.
  • Amazon Prime Video, 2023. Published that moving one component from distributed serverless components to a single process cut costs by about 90%. The component was the audio/video quality monitoring service. Widely mis-reported as "Amazon abandons microservices". It was one team and one component, and the actual lesson is exactly rule 1 in 10i.

The pattern across all five: the split is right when the coordination pain exceeds the distribution bill, and wrong the rest of the time — and it is reversible. Re-merging two services that always deploy together is a normal, healthy engineering act, not an admission of defeat.

Which pairs are commonly confused, and how do you tell them apart?

One sentence each.

  • Microservices vs SOA — the same "services" idea; SOA put the coordination logic in a central smart bus, microservices keep the pipes dumb and the endpoints smart. (Also in the arch-patterns lab.)
  • Microservices vs modular monolith — identical boundaries, different deployment: a modular monolith enforces the same module lines inside one process, so you get local reasoning without the network. It is where you should start, and it is where you rehearse the boundaries before paying for them.
  • Microservices vs a distributed monolith — the deployment diagram is the same; the difference is whether a feature can be shipped by one team alone. Only the release log can tell them apart.
  • Microservices vs serverless — orthogonal. Serverless is a way to run a unit of code; microservices is a way to draw boundaries. A microservice can be a container or a function; a monolith can run in a function too.
  • Circuit breaker vs bulkhead vs rate limiter — the breaker stops calling a broken dependency. The bulkhead caps how much of your capacity one dependency may hold. The rate limiter caps how much of your capacity one caller may consume. Inbound, outbound, and outbound-quota respectively.
  • API gateway vs service mesh — the gateway handles north–south traffic (outside → in: TLS, auth, routing); the mesh handles east–west traffic (service → service: mTLS, retries, breakers, tracing). Different directions, often the same underlying proxy.
  • Orchestration vs choreography (known from saga) — a central coordinator that tells each service what to do, versus services reacting to each other's events with no coordinator. Orchestration is easier to debug; choreography couples less.

What is this lab not?

That paragraph is correct, and this lab does not repeat it.

What that paragraph does not tell you is the thing that actually decides whether your split succeeds or ruins your year:

  • what physically breaks the moment two pieces of code stop sharing a process,
  • the arithmetic that turns five reliable services into one unreliable checkout,
  • why a slow dependency destroys a system harder than a dead one,
  • and why the fix that every engineer reaches for first, a retry, makes it worse.

That is this lab. It is the mechanics, with numbers.

You also arrive holding most of the parts already. You know saga, transactional-outbox, cqrs, event-sourcing, two-phase-commit, idempotency, eventual-consistency and communication-protocols. Every one of those is a scar: a fix invented because splitting a system broke something specific. Until now you learned the fixes one at a time, each in its own lab, without seeing the single wound they all belong to.

This lab is the wound.

Where do the numbers come from?

Nothing in this lab is run. Everything quantitative is one of three things: arithmetic you can check by hand, a scenario you can play in visualization.html, or a measured result cited from one of your earlier labs.

The four running figures were chosen to be realistic rather than observed: 99.9% per service, 100 requests per second, 200 threads, and 20 ms per healthy call. The 200 is the Tomcat default. The published studies are named where they are used: Dean and Barroso's The Tail at Scale (CACM, 2013) for the 63% figure, and the five industry write-ups in section 13 for the dated events.

Can you say the whole lab in three sentences?

The three-sentence version, for when somebody asks you in a corridor:

Microservices buy exactly one thing — independent deployability, the ability for a team to release its own part without asking or breaking anyone — and the only way to get it is to make the boundary a data boundary, where one service alone may touch a given table and everything else must ask. The bill arrives immediately and is arithmetic, not opinion: availability multiplies downward (five 99.9% services give 99.5%, five times the downtime), a slow dependency destroys a caller faster than a dead one because workers = rate × duration fills a finite pool in seconds, and every fix you already know — timeout, bulkhead, breaker, outbox, saga, idempotency key — is the scar of one of those failures. What you permanently give up is the compiler, the stack trace and the transaction; what you must build to replace them is contract testing, distributed tracing and sagas — which is why the honest default is a modular monolith, and why re-merging two services that always deploy together is a healthy engineering act rather than a defeat.

Microservices — Solutions

Worked answers. Rungs 7 and 8 are design discussions, not single correct outputs — what matters is naming the right mechanism, not matching my wording.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 (A model answer, 92 words.)

Right now the whole product is one thing, so everyone who wants to change any part of it has to take a turn, and everyone's mistakes land on everyone else. A one-line fix waits behind someone else's unfinished work, and if their work is wrong, ours gets cancelled too. Splitting the product into separate pieces gives each team its own turn. The cost is that the pieces now talk to each other over a network, which is slow and sometimes fails, so the product will break in new ways and we will need new tools to see why.

1.2 Ask: "When did you last release one of them on its own, without coordinating with another team?" — or equivalently, look at the last twenty production releases and count how many were co-deployments. That question and not another because twelve repositories is a fact about the source control layout, while independent deployability is the only benefit of the style; everything else (scaling, tech choice, isolation) is either obtainable without splitting or must be bought separately. If they always release together, they have one system in twelve repositories, and they are paying the distribution bill without collecting anything.

1.3 A dead dependency refuses the connection in about a millisecond, so the caller's thread is released immediately and only the feature that needed it fails. A slow one holds the thread for the whole call: at 100 requests per second and a 5-second call, 100 × 5 = 500 threads are needed and only 200 exist, so the pool is empty two seconds in and every endpoint on the service stops working. And it is invisible — the slow dependency is returning 200 OK, so its error rate is zero and every dashboard is green.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 Healthy steady state: about 2 of 200 threads in use (Little's Law: 100/s × 0.02 s = 2), essentially all of them inside payments calls, with a 100% checkout success rate. The point of reading this before touching anything is that the healthy state is boring — the service is 1% utilised, which is why nobody thinks the thread pool is a risk.

2.2 POST /checkout fails first, which is expected and contained. GET /orders/{id} fails a moment later, and that is the surprise. The page says why in a full sentence: it is not failing because it needs payments — it never calls payments — it is failing because there is no free thread to run it on. That distinction is the definition of a cascading failure and it is the whole reason the tab exists.

2.3 Five services at 99.9% → 43.7 hours/year. To get under 20 hours you need two services (17.5 h); three is already 26.3 h. Which is the blunt statement of 10g: the only large lever is removing services from the required set, not improving them.

2.4 In "cut by layer", the worst is a tie at 5 services — "Show a delivery estimate" and "Add gift receipts", each of which needs a change in the UI, the API layer, the business-logic layer, the data-access layer and the batch jobs. Only the "cut by capability" arrangement brings the median feature to exactly one service — and note that even there, some features legitimately touch two. The test is the median, not the maximum; a good cut still has cross-cutting features.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 It survives, with no margin. 100/s × 0.8 s = 80 threads of 200, 40% utilisation. But note two things. First, from the work-pressure-and-deadlines lab's ρ/(1−ρ): at 40% utilisation queueing already adds ~0.67× the service time, so customer-visible latency is worse than 800 ms. Second, this configuration dies if traffic rises 2.5× or if payments degrades a little further — and both of those happen on the same afternoon, because a slow checkout makes customers reload the page. Surviving is not the same as being safe, and the number that tells you which one you are is saturation, not error rate.

3.2 The pool does not exhaust (100 × 5 = 500 < 2000) — and "more threads" is still not a fix, for four reasons:

  1. The bottleneck moves, it does not vanish. Your database connection pool is probably 20–50. Your outbound HTTP connection pool is probably 100. One of those exhausts instead, and the failure is identical with a different graph.
  2. Memory. Platform threads cost roughly 1 MB of stack each; 2,000 of them is real memory and real scheduler pressure.
  3. It does not help the customer. Every checkout still takes 5 seconds.
  4. It is not a bound. If payments degrades to 30 s you need 3,000. A number you have to keep raising is not a limit; a timeout is a limit, because it caps duration rather than counting occupants.

Worth connecting to your java-virtual-threads lab: virtual threads make the thread count nearly free, which removes reason 1's thread-stack limit — and makes bulkheads more important, not less. With cheap threads, nothing stops you from having 50,000 concurrent calls in flight to a dying dependency; the constraint simply relocates to the downstream service and to your connection pools, where it is less visible. Cheap concurrency removes the accidental limit and leaves you needing a deliberate one.

3.3 100/s × 1 s = 100 threads of 200. orders survives comfortably. Checkout success rate: 0% — every call times out, because payments genuinely takes 5 s and the timeout is 1 s. GET /orders/{id}: 100% success, normal latency, because 100 threads are free.

That is exactly the right outcome, and it is worth saying out loud why: the timeout did not fix checkout and was never going to — payments is broken. What it did was convert a total service outage into a single broken feature. That is what all of section 10f buys: not success, but containment.

3.4 Fan-out 6, p = 0.01: 1 − 0.99^6 = 1 − 0.9415 = 5.85%. Fan-out 40: 1 − 0.99^40 = 1 − 0.6690 = 33.1%. So a dependency's p99 becomes roughly the caller's p94 at width 6, and its p67 at width 40. This is Dean and Barroso's point in The Tail at Scale: at fan-out 100, 1 − 0.99^100 = 63% — the majority of requests hit the rare case. A p99 is a property of a call, not of a system, and it does not survive being fanned out.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1

  • Bulkhead on, breaker off — GET /orders/{id} stays alive. The bulkhead caps payments at (say) 40 threads, so 160 remain for everything else. Checkout still fails slowly: each attempt burns its full timeout. Property protected: isolation — one dependency cannot consume the whole pool.
  • Breaker on, bulkhead off — checkout fails quickly, in microseconds rather than a second, because calls are not made at all. Property protected: efficiency of failure — you stop spending capacity on calls known to fail, which also lets payments recover by removing its load.
  • Neither replaces the other. Before the breaker trips (the first 20 failures, or the first window) you are unprotected, and that is exactly when the bulkhead is holding the line. After it trips, the breaker is doing the work and the bulkhead is idle. You need both because they cover different intervals of the same incident.

4.2 Thread occupancy goes from 100 × 1 s = 100 to 100 × 3 s = 300, which exceeds 200, so the pool exhausts again — you have undone your own fix. Simultaneously the rate arriving at payments goes from 100/s to 300/s, tripling load on a service that is failing.

Liveness is lost, safety is not. Nothing incorrect is written; the system simply stops making progress, and — the nastier part — stays stopped, because every time payments recovers the retry backlog knocks it down again. This is the distinction from flp-impossibility and eventual-consistency applied to an operational incident: the failure has no wrong state to point at, which is precisely why it is hard to diagnose.

(Caveat: safety is only preserved here because we assumed the retry is safe. If POST /payments has no idempotency key, the retry is a safety violation too — see 6.3.)

4.3 Three things that became product decisions, and who decides:

  1. What the customer is told at checkout. "Order placed" is now a lie until payment settles; it must say something like "Order received — confirming payment". Decided by: product and legal (in some jurisdictions what you may claim about an order is regulated).
  2. What happens when payment fails after the customer left. An email? A retry with a saved card? A grace period? Decided by: product, with finance, because it changes the conversion and refund numbers.
  3. What happens to the stock in the meantime. If you reserved it, someone else cannot buy it while a probably-failing payment settles; if you did not, you may oversell. Decided by: product and operations, because "we oversold, here's an apology and a discount" is a business policy, not a bug.

The general lesson: moving work off the critical path converts an engineering problem into a product problem. That is a real conversion, not an evasion — and shipping it without having that conversation is how teams end up sending "your order is confirmed" emails for orders that will be cancelled.

4.4 The rename is identical; the consequence differs in who can see the dependency.

  • Shared store: the readers are other services querying the table directly. There is no artefact anywhere — no import, no call, no config — that records they are readers. The rename compiles, deploys, passes every test, and breaks a batch job at 3am in a service you have never opened.
  • Private store: the only readers are callers of your contract. They are enumerable — from your access logs, your consumer groups, your service catalogue — so the rename is a known breaking change and goes through expand/contract.

Nothing in either codebase differs. The difference is entirely in whether the dependency is discoverable, and that is why database grants, not code review, are the enforcement mechanism.

Rung 5 — Trace

Throughout: threads = arrival rate × duration, cap 200. The 20 req/s of GET /orders/{id} take ~5 ms each and occupy ~0.1 threads, so they are negligible until there are none left.

5.1 — t = 1.0 s. ~2 threads in use. Nothing has changed yet; the requests in flight are the healthy 20 ms ones. Customers: fine.

5.2 — t = 2.0 s. 100 threads in use. In the last second, 100 checkouts arrived and none has completed — the first sick one will not finish until t = 6.0 s. Checkout is neither succeeding nor failing; it is waiting, which is why no error-rate alarm has fired. Customers: staring at a spinner.

5.3 — t = 3.0 s. 200 threads — the pool is full. A GET /orders/{id} arriving now finds no free thread. It does not fail immediately; it queues, and nothing will free a thread until t = 6.0 s. So it will sit for ~3 seconds and then almost certainly hit the client's own timeout.

5.4 — t = 6.0 s. The first sick checkouts complete, freeing threads at 100/s. But arrivals are 120/s. The queue grows by 20 requests per second, forever. Latency therefore rises without bound. The load balancer's health check is itself an HTTP request needing a thread, so it times out; the LB marks orders unhealthy and removes it. Its share of traffic is redistributed to the sibling orders replicas, which now receive more than 100/s each and saturate faster than this one did. The whole orders fleet is gone within seconds of the first instance going.

5.5 — First GET /orders/{id} failure: just after t = 3.0 s (it queues from 3.0 and dies at its client timeout). That time is the important number because it is the moment the incident changes category. Before it, the story is "payments is broken, so customers cannot check out" — contained, expected, and correctly attributable. After it, the story is "orders is down", and the page goes to the wrong team about the wrong service. Every mitigation in 10f exists to push that moment to never.

5.6 — With a 1-second timeout. Steady state is reached at t = 2.0 s (one timeout period after degradation) and holds:

  • 100 threads in use (100/s × 1 s), 100 free.
  • Checkout: 0% success, each attempt failing after 1 s.
  • GET /orders/{id}: 100% success at normal latency, forever.
  • The load balancer's health check succeeds, so the fleet stays up.

One number changed and the blast radius went from "the order service and every replica of it" to "the checkout feature".

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 — The shared library. The flaw is that riverbend-common contains domain types and entity mappings, so it is the shared database schema wearing a Maven coordinate.

Concrete run: the platform team releases riverbend-common 2.0, which adds a required fulfilmentState field to Order and changes an enum. orders upgrades and starts emitting the new shape. shipping is still on 1.9 and fails to deserialize. To fix it, all seven services must upgrade — in an order, in a window, with a meeting.

Violated: independent deployability. Also rule 2 if the entity mappings imply shared tables.

The distinction that makes this usable: shared libraries are fine for technical concerns with no domain knowledge — logging, metrics, tracing propagation, HTTP client defaults — and poisonous for domain types. Across a service boundary, duplication is correct: shipping should have its own small Order class containing only the four fields it needs, and if orders adds a field, shipping ignores it (tolerant reading). Two teams maintaining two slightly different Order classes is not technical debt; it is the whole point of a bounded context.

6.2 — The one breaker. The flaw is that breaker state is global to the service rather than per-dependency.

Concrete run: the card network degrades, payments fails 20 times, the breaker opens. Now the same open breaker rejects calls to catalog and inventory, which are perfectly healthy — so the product page and the order lookup fail too. The mitigation manufactured the cascade it was bought to prevent.

Violated: fault isolation, and specifically the containment property in 10f.

Second, subtler flaw: "20 consecutive failures" is the wrong trigger under mixed traffic. If a healthy catalog call succeeds between every two failing payments calls, the consecutive counter resets forever and the breaker never opens. The correct trigger is a failure rate over a rolling window (e.g.

50% of at least 20 calls in the last 10 seconds), evaluated per (dependency, endpoint) — because POST /payments failing tells you nothing about GET /payments/{id}.

6.3 — The helpful retry. Every part of the retry policy is right — the timeout, the backoff, the jitter, the budget. What is missing is an idempotency key.

Concrete run: orders sends POST /payments. payments receives it, charges the card, and begins writing its response — which is lost, or arrives at 2.1 seconds. orders timed out at 2.0 s and retries. payments sees a request it cannot distinguish from a new one and charges the card again.

Violated: safety — the at-most-once effect. And note the shape from your idempotency lab: a timeout is the unknown outcome, and retrying an unknown without a key is the definition of the double-charge bug.

Fix, and all three parts are needed: the caller mints the key before the first attempt and reuses it verbatim on every retry (a fresh key per retry defeats everything and the server cannot tell); payments claims the key atomically with the effect — an INSERT under a UNIQUE constraint in the same transaction as the charge record, not a check-then-act; and it stores and replays the response, because answering "duplicate!" leaves the caller as ignorant as before.

6.4 — The reporting shortcut. "Read-only" addresses the wrong risk. The coupling created by a read is on the shape of the data, and shape is exactly what a schema change breaks.

Concrete run (schema): orders renames status to state. The nightly analytics job fails at 3am, in a service the orders team does not know exists and cannot see in any dependency graph. This is F4 verbatim.

Concrete run (operational): an analyst runs an unindexed aggregate over orders at 14:00. It holds a long transaction, consumes IO, inflates replication lag and pushes checkout p99 past its SLO. A read-only credential grants no writes and unlimited ability to hurt.

Violated: independent deployability (rule 2), and operational isolation.

The fix ladder, weakest to strongest: (1) point them at a read replica — removes the IO coupling, keeps the schema coupling entirely; (2) CDC into an analytics store — removes the operational coupling, and still couples to your table shape unless the producer owns the transformation; (3) publish events designed as a contract, versioned, with the same expand/contract discipline as any other interface. Only (3) actually makes analytics a consumer you can see and a shape you can change.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

(Design discussions. What matters is naming the mechanism.)

7.1 — The late decline.

  • Order state machine, explicitly: PENDING → PAID → CONFIRMED on the happy path; PENDING → PAYMENT_FAILED → CANCELLED on this one. The states are customer-visible, so they get customer-visible names.
  • What the customer sees. They were told "Order received — confirming payment", never "confirmed". On decline: an email within a minute saying payment could not be taken, with a link to retry using another card, and the order held in PAYMENT_FAILED for a grace window (say 24 h) before cancelling. If a confirmation email was already sent, you now owe a correction email — which is why the confirmation must be sent after the pivot step, not before. That naming rule is the actual fix; everything else is cleanup.
  • The reservation. Two independent mechanisms, because either can fail. (a) The saga's compensation explicitly releases it, idempotently. (b) The reservation carries a TTL — 30 minutes — after which inventory releases it unilaterally. (b) exists because (a) can be lost forever, and a reservation that leaks is stock you can never sell again.
  • The pivot step. In saga terms, the charge is the pivot: before it, compensations are cheap (release a reservation); after it, they cost real money (a refund with fees). Design so that everything reversible happens before the pivot and everything irreversible after it.
  • What support needs. The saga log must be durable and queryable, not a transient queue: the agent needs to see every step attempted, its outcome, and its timestamp; to retry the charge against a new card; to force-cancel and release; and to see which compensations failed. A saga you cannot query is a saga you cannot support.
  • Idempotency everywhere. The decline event will be delivered more than once. So will the compensation. Both must be idempotent, or the customer gets two cancellation emails and, worse, two refunds.

7.2 — Surviving a catalog outage.

  • Order-history page: stop calling catalog at all. Store the product name and the price on the order line, at the time of the order. This is not a cache and it does not go stale — an order is a historical record of what was bought at the price paid, and showing today's name and price on last year's order is not a fresher answer, it is a wrong one. Recognising that a cross-service read is really a missing snapshot is the highest-value move in this exercise, and it removes the dependency permanently rather than softening it.
  • Product-detail page: cache with serve-stale-on-error. A 60-second TTL for freshness, plus the ability to keep serving the last known value when catalog is unreachable — the stale-if-error behaviour from your http-caching lab, and the same control-plane rule you met in distributed-rate-limiting: a cached copy that keeps serving when its source is gone. Cap it: beyond a chosen max-stale (say 24 h) show an error rather than a year-old page.
  • Never from the stale copy: the price used to charge. F6's rule — display, never decide. Either read the price from catalog synchronously on the checkout path, or (better) snapshot the price into the order at the moment the customer added the item and charge from the snapshot, so there is no cross-service read on the critical path at all.
  • catalog itself, as the highest-risk shared node (F8): the highest availability target you own, more replicas than its callers, HTTP caching at the gateway, and load shedding so it degrades rather than dies.
  • Every caller gets its own bulkhead and breaker on catalog, so its slowness cannot become their outage.

7.3 — Renaming status to state.

  • Find the consumers. In rough order of reliability: the schema registry / event catalogue if you have one; consumer groups on the topic (for events) and access logs on the endpoint (for HTTP), which give you actual observed callers rather than declared ones; the service catalogue's declared dependencies; and finally announcement-and-wait, which is the weakest and the most common.
  • Say the honest part out loud: for events, you often cannot enumerate consumers, because subscribing does not require your permission. That is precisely the gap a schema registry with a consumer registry exists to close, and if you do not have one, this exercise is the business case for it.
  • The sequence, three deploys:
    1. Expand. Emit both status and state, with identical values. Deploy. Verify by consuming your own topic that both fields are present on every message. Nothing can break — this is an additive change and tolerant readers ignore the new field.
    2. Migrate. Notify every known consumer with a deadline. Each moves on its own schedule. Measure before proceeding: every known consumer has positively confirmed (not "we announced it and heard nothing"), consumer lag is normal, error rates unchanged. Wait weeks, not days.
    3. Contract. Stop emitting status. Deploy. This is the only step that can break anyone, which is why it is last and separate.
  • If one consumer never migrates: you do not remove the field. You either keep dual-writing indefinitely — recording it as debt with a named owner and a date — or you escalate. And if the consumer turns out to have no owner, you have found F9, and the fix is organisational: assign it, or delete it. Do not let an unowned service hold your schema hostage forever by default.

7.4 — Monitoring the outbox relay.

Why queue depth is the wrong instrument — three reasons, and the third is the one that matters:

  1. It is traffic-dependent. A depth of 500 is a crisis at 3am and normal at noon, so any static threshold is either noisy or blind.
  2. It reads zero when nothing is being written. "Depth is 0" cannot distinguish "healthy and idle" from "the relay is dead and so is the writer".
  3. A head-of-line block hides completely. If the relay is running but one poison row fails repeatedly, the relay keeps draining nothing while depth stays low and flat — and the oldest row ages forever. Depth is at its most reassuring exactly when this is happening.

The three alarms to set — all of them phrased as age, per the liveness rule from eventual-consistency:

  1. Age of the oldest unpublished outbox row > 60 s → page. This is the SLO restated as an alarm, it is traffic-independent, and it catches the poison row, the dead relay, and the slow broker with one signal.
  2. Time since the relay last published anything > 120 s, while writes are occurring → page. A machinery heartbeat: it catches the case where the relay dies during a genuinely quiet period, when alarm 1 has nothing to measure.
  3. Publish failure rate > 0 sustained for 60 s, or any row's retry count above a threshold → page (and route the row to a DLQ). This names the poison row explicitly so the responder is not hunting for it.

Plus a fourth that is worth more than all three: an end-to-end synthetic probe. Write a canary order every 30 seconds and measure how long until its event is observed by a downstream consumer. That is t-visibility from the eventual-consistency lab, it measures the property the customer actually cares about, and it is the only one of these that survives you being wrong about which component broke.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 — The decomposition brief.

(a) What I would actually do in the first quarter: not split. The stated pain is a 4-hour weekly release with a go/no-go meeting and cross-team breakage — and none of that requires splitting to fix. In order:

  1. Measure the real seams. Take the last 100 merged pull requests and build a co-change matrix: which modules changed together. That is empirical evidence of where the boundaries actually are, and it usually contradicts the architecture diagram. Splitting without this is guessing.
  2. Fix the release process. Trunk-based development, feature flags, an automated pipeline, canary deploys with automatic rollback. This takes weekly 4-hour releases to daily low-risk ones and directly addresses "one team's change broke another team's feature" — faster, cheaper, and more reversibly than any extraction.
  3. Enforce module boundaries inside the monolith. A modular monolith with build-tool-enforced dependency rules and one owning team per module. This is where you rehearse the boundaries for free: if a boundary is wrong, you find out in a refactor rather than in a migration.
  4. Build the prerequisites you will need anyway: distributed tracing (yes, in the monolith — it works and you will need the instrumentation), a service catalogue with owners, and contract-testing capability.

(b) First extraction: product-search. Criteria, all four of which it meets:

  • A genuinely different resource profile — CPU-bound, 70% of traffic, while everything else is comfortable. That makes independent scaling a quantified benefit rather than a slogan, and it is the one benefit a modular monolith cannot give you.
  • A read path. No transactional invariant crosses the boundary, so rule 3 is satisfied and no saga is needed.
  • Its data can be a projection — a search index fed by events, so there is no shared-table extraction to do.
  • One team can own it.

payments is the plausible second: a different compliance boundary, a different change cadence, and a clean invariant boundary. Extract one, live with it for a quarter, and measure what it cost before extracting another.

(c) The monthly metric: the median number of deployable units changed per shipped feature — which should stay at 1. Alongside it: deploy frequency per team, and change failure rate. Those three say whether the benefit is arriving. A count of services says nothing at all, which is why it is the number everyone reports.

(d) Three refusals, each in one sentence to leadership:

  • "Microservices by Q3" as the goal → "The date can't be the target because the thing we actually want is teams shipping without blocking each other — I'll report that number every month, and we'll add a service each time that number says a service is what's blocking it."
  • Splitting by technical layer → "A separate validation or data-access service means every feature has to change every service, which is exactly the problem we have today with a network added to it."
  • Splitting all 340 tables in one migration → "That's a two-quarter feature freeze, and the first thing we'd learn is that some of the boundaries were wrong; we can move one table at a time and keep shipping the whole way."

8.2 — Availability budget.

(a) Four equal services to reach 99.95%: each needs 0.9995^(1/4) ≈ 0.999875, i.e. 99.9875%, about 1.1 hours of downtime per year each — including deploys, dependency failures and bad code. That is a demanding target and it is worth stating in hours rather than nines, because "99.99%" sounds achievable and "one hour per year, deploys included" does not.

(b) The card network publishes a 99.9% SLA and is not under your control. payments cannot be more available than its hard dependency, so payments ≤ 99.9%, so a chain containing it synchronously is at best 99.9% — already worse than the 99.95% you promised, before counting your own three services. The promise is unachievable as designed, and no amount of work on your own services changes that. An external dependency's SLA is a hard ceiling on every path that requires it.

(c) The only two structural options:

  1. Take payments off the critical path. Accept the order, charge asynchronously (10g). The synchronous chain becomes gateway + orders + inventory, needing 0.9995^(1/3) ≈ 99.983% each (~1.5 h/year each) — hard, but possible, and entirely within your control.
  2. Make the dependency redundant. Integrate a second card processor with automatic failover. If the two really are independent, the combined unavailability is the product and the ceiling disappears — but "independent" is an assumption that fails on the days that matter (a shared network path, a shared card scheme outage), and a failover path that is not continuously exercised does not work when you need it.

Pick option 1 as primary. It is under your control, it needs no vendor negotiation, and — decisively — it protects you against the dependency being slow as well as down, which option 2 does not. Add option 2 later if the business genuinely requires synchronous authorisation before confirming an order.

There is an honest third option nobody lists: renegotiate the promise. 99.95% may simply be the wrong number, and discovering that from the arithmetic before you commit to it is worth more than any amount of engineering afterwards.

(d) The three SLOs, and the one to page on:

  1. Checkout success rate ≥ 99.95% over 28 days — the customer-facing promise. This is the one you page on at 3am, and as a burn-rate alert (e.g. burning the 28-day budget at 14× over a 1-hour window), not a raw threshold — a threshold either fires on every blip or misses a slow bleed.
  2. Checkout latency p99 < 500 ms — ticket-level unless burning fast. Note from 3.1 that latency degrades before availability does, so this is your leading indicator.
  3. Payment settlement freshness: 99.9% of accepted orders reach a terminal payment state within 5 minutes. This is the SLO the asynchronous design created, it is the one that catches a dead outbox relay or a stalled saga, and it is the one teams forget to write — which is why 7.4's alarms exist.

8.3 — Three regions, 150 ms apart.

Survives unchanged: the service boundaries and contracts; the outbox + saga shape; timeouts, bulkheads and breakers — which become more important, because a 150 ms floor means a cross-region call's timeout budget is mostly network and a retry is expensive.

Must change — orders data placement. Partition by region with home-region pinning: a customer's orders live in their home region and are written only there, with async replicas elsewhere for reads. The alternative — multi-region writable replication — buys you write conflicts on a transactionally meaningful entity, and the resolution ("last write wins" on an order) is never acceptable. Cross-region reads are stale, and the customer's own reads must be routed to their home region for read-your-writes (causal-consistency).

Must change — inventory, and this is the genuinely hard one. Two structures with a real trade:

  • One global stock pool. Reservations must be linearizable and therefore cross-region: +150 ms on every checkout, and unavailable in the minority region during a partition. This is the CP choice, and the cap-theorem lab decides it.
  • Stock partitioned per region (e.g. 40/30/30 of the pool). Reservations become local, fast and partition-tolerant — the AP choice — at the cost of stranded stock: one region sells out while another has surplus. That is precisely the "stranded quota" and "regional share reallocation" shape you already met in distributed-rate-limiting, and the same fix applies: a slow background reallocation process, plus the ability to borrow across regions on a longer timescale than a request.

Note which lab decides which half. cap-theorem decides the partition case; pacelc decides the normal case, and the normal case is what you live in every day — 150 ms added to every checkout is a conversion-rate problem that will hurt you far more often than a partition will. Most businesses choose EL/PA here and accept stranded stock.

Outbox and saga during a cross-region partition — the new failure mode. The outbox is local, so writes keep succeeding (good). The relay to a cross-region broker stalls, so events queue and your age alarm fires (7.4, and it fires correctly). The dangerous part is the saga: a saga whose next step lives in an unreachable region stalls mid-flight, holding a semantic lock and an inventory reservation. If your reservation TTL or saga timeout is shorter than the partition, the system will compensate orders that would have succeeded — cancelling paid orders because of a network event, then having to un-cancel them when the partition heals. So: reservation TTLs and saga step timeouts must exceed your expected partition duration, and a saga that times out during a known partition should escalate to a human rather than compensate automatically. False compensation is the new failure mode multi-region introduces, and it is invisible until the first real partition.
