Lab 57 · Running It In Production

Queueing theory — why waiting is not proportional to load

Tell a friend: Dana runs the only desk at a passport office. Each application takes her 80 seconds, and 36 people an hour walk in, so she has a fifth of every hour spare. Yet the average person still waits more than five minutes, because people arrive in clumps and a quiet minute cannot be saved up for the next clump. Push Dana from 80% busy to 90% and the wait doubles again. Push her past 100% and the line grows forever and never comes back on its own. Every server you own is Dana's desk, and this lab gives you the arithmetic to say how long its line will be before the traffic arrives.

1. Why does the pager go off when nothing is broken?

In one breath: Traffic went up by a quarter. The box never reached 100%. The slow requests still got nearly three times slower, and not one component failed.

Your service runs on one box. It is healthy. CPU sits around 72%, memory is flat, no errors, no restarts, no bad deploys.

Meera is on call. She looks at that 72% and says the sentence every capacity spreadsheet encodes:

"We're at 80% CPU, so we have 20% headroom."

Then marketing runs a campaign and traffic goes up by 25%. Nothing breaks. CPU climbs to 90%. Still not 100%, still "fine". But the 99th-percentile latency that customers actually feel goes from 1.3 seconds to 3.7 seconds, and support starts getting calls.

Here is what Meera saw, before and after. Read each row across.

Traffic rose 25%. Busy-ness rose 18 percentage points. The slow tail got 2.8× worse. There is no proportionality anywhere in that table. No component failed in order to produce it.

Now look at Meera's sentence again. You do not have 20% headroom at 80% busy. At 80% busy the average request already spends 5× its own work time inside your service. Taking it to 90%, a change your dashboard draws as a small step, doubles that again. Headroom is not counted in leftover capacity. It is counted in how far you are from the cliff, and the cliff is not where the graph looks like it is.

What goes wrong if you do not know this? You spend the incident hunting a bug that does not exist. You add logging, you profile, you suspect the database, you roll back a deploy that was innocent. Eventually someone adds a box and it goes away. The postmortem says "increased load", which explains nothing and teaches nothing, so it happens again next quarter at a different service.

The alternative is to be able to say, before the campaign: "at 90% we will see about 3.7 s at the 99th percentile, and here is how busy we must never let it get." This lab builds that sentence from the ground up.

2. What happens at Dana's desk on one ordinary morning?

In one breath: One clerk, one desk, people walking in at their own pace. Dana has a fifth of the morning spare. Guess how long people wait, then find out.

Forget servers for a while. There is a passport office with one desk and one clerk, Dana. She is fast, she is consistent, and she never takes a break. Each application takes her exactly the same time, every time.

A first draft of this story gave Dana 4 minutes per application. That is 15 per hour, and the draft sent 45 people through the door in the hour the office is open. Three times what she can do teaches nothing, so let us make the numbers honest instead.

Dana takes 80 seconds per application, so she can serve 45 per hour exactly. And 36 people arrive in the hour. Capacity 45 an hour, demand 36 an hour. Dana is busy 80% of the morning and idle 20%. There is a fifth of an hour of slack sitting there, unused.

Arjun manages the office. He wrote those two numbers on the whiteboard and thinks the morning looks comfortable. Here is the question the rest of this lab answers.

How long does the average person wait before Dana calls them up?

The reasonable answer, the one almost everyone gives, is zero, or nearly zero. There is spare capacity. Nobody should have to wait.

🔮 Predict: Dana is only 80% busy. Guess the average wait before you read on: seconds? about as long as one application takes? several times that?

Reveal

About 320 seconds, four whole applications' worth of waiting, when people turn up at random times. The simulator's figure, converted into Dana's units, is 326 seconds of waiting for 80 seconds of service. Each person spends 5× their own service time in the building.

If you said "seconds", you read an average as if it were a timetable. If you said "one application's worth", you pictured one person ahead of you in the line. There are four. That gap between intuition and reality is the whole subject, and section 4 shows where it comes from.

3. Why not just plan with averages?

In one breath: Arjun's capacity plan is four lines of arithmetic. Three of them are true. The fourth is where it dies.

The instinct is right and the arithmetic is right, so let us write it down properly. It is exactly what a capacity plan looks like. Here is Arjun's whiteboard:

  1. One application takes 80 seconds. So Dana's capacity is 45 an hour.
  2. 36 people arrive per hour.
  3. 36 ÷ 45 = 0.8. In words: arrivals per hour, divided by how many Dana can serve per hour. Dana is busy 80% of the time.
  4. 0.8 is less than 1, so demand fits inside capacity. Nobody needs to wait.

Steps 1 to 3 are all true. Step 4 is where it dies.

Hold on to the number in step 3. It is the most important number in the subject, and section 7 gives it a name.

4. Why can't Dana save idle time for later?

In one breath: A quiet minute cannot be put in a drawer and spent on the next busy one. Every clump of arrivals leaves a backlog, and the backlog is paid off only out of future quiet minutes.

Stop reasoning about the whole hour and watch one actual morning, minute by minute. People do not arrive on a metronome. They arrive when they arrive. Here are the first eight, and the times they walked in:

0:00, 0:30, 0:55, 4:10, 9:00, 9:20, 9:35, 9:50

Priya is the eighth. She walks in at 9:50.

Reveal

By the time Priya arrives, Dana is serving person 6, person 7 is standing in line, and the three and a half idle minutes are already gone. If you said "one application's worth", you counted only the person at the desk and forgot the two behind. Trace it below.

Between person 4 leaving at 5:30 and person 5 walking in at 9:00, Dana sits doing nothing for 3 minutes 30 seconds. That idle time is real, it is paid for, and it is gone. When four people show up in the 50 seconds after 9:00, Dana cannot spend those saved minutes on them. She can only work at 80 seconds per person, starting now.

That is the whole mechanism, and it is worth its own line:

Idle capacity is perishable. Demand is not. A quiet minute cannot be banked. A busy minute's overflow is banked, in the line, and it is paid off out of future quiet minutes that may not come soon.

So the line is not caused by too much work. Over the full hour there is genuinely enough capacity. The line is caused by work not arriving evenly, together with an inability to save the slack.

And now the second, nastier half. Look at Priya. She waited 190 seconds because of a clump that started 50 seconds before she arrived. A line is a memory. A burst that happened in the past is still being charged to people who arrive in the future.

The busier Dana is, the longer that memory lasts, because there is less idle time available to erase it. That is why the wait explodes near full capacity instead of rising smoothly. At 50% busy a burst is erased quickly. At 95% busy there is almost no idle time to erase it with, so the memory of one bad minute persists for many minutes and lands on hundreds of people.

5. What do we actually need to be able to say?

In one breath: "Does the work fit?" is the wrong question. We need to predict the wait, know where the cliff is, connect the three things we can count, and know what happens past the edge.

Arjun's plan answered "does the work fit?". That question is necessary and useless on its own. The real requirement is to be able to do four things.

  1. Predict waiting, not just how much work gets through. The inputs are how fast work arrives, how long it takes, and how irregular both of those are.
  2. Know where the cliff is. That is the point past which small increases in load produce large increases in delay. Then we can choose an operating point instead of discovering one.
  3. Connect the three things we can actually count: how fast work arrives, how long each item spends inside, and how many are inside at once. Knowing two should give us the third for free.
  4. Know what happens when demand exceeds capacity, and what to do about it, given that the line will not fix itself.

Everything from here is the pursuit of those four.

6. Which two sentences generate the whole subject?

In one breath: One sentence counts what is inside the building. The other says how long the wait is. Every formula in this lab is one of those two, made precise.

Sentence one, the accounting identity.

The average number of people inside the office equals how fast they arrive, multiplied by how long each one stays.

Engineers call that Little's Law. It is not a model and not an approximation. It is a statement about areas, true for any line whatsoever: any arrival pattern, any service order, any number of desks. It is the reason you never need to guess two of those three quantities.

Sentence two, the shape of waiting.

Waiting time = (a busy-ness factor that blows up near full capacity) × (an irregularity factor) × (the time one item takes).

That is Kingman's formula. The first factor is the cliff. It grows like 1/(1 − busy). As Dana's idle fifth shrinks towards nothing, the wait grows towards infinity, so the last few percent of a machine's capacity cost more delay than the first eighty percent put together.

The second factor says that two offices at identical busy-ness can differ by 30× purely in how irregular their arrivals and their work are. The third says a line of slow things is worse than a line of fast things at the same busy-ness. That is why making each request faster helps you twice over.

The two sentences side by side. No symbols yet; section 7 earns each one.

How fast do people walk in? The arrival rate

Thirty-six people walked into Dana's office in an hour. That number is how fast work shows up: the long-run average number of arrivals per unit time. Engineers write it with the Greek letter λ, said "lambda", and call it the arrival rate. For Dana, λ (the arrival rate) is 36 per hour, which is 0.01 per second.

Notice what λ (arrivals per unit time) does not say. It says nothing about when they arrive. Thirty-six an hour is thirty-six an hour whether they trickle in one by one or come in a single clump of 36.

The word exists to separate how much work arrives from how it is spread out. Those two are independent. They have completely different effects on waiting, and mixing them up is the single most common error in capacity planning. The rate limit in distributed-rate-limiting is a hard cap placed on λ, the arrival rate. Rate limiting exists because λ is the one input you do not control.

How long does one application take, and how many can Dana finish?

Dana takes 80 seconds per application. That is the service time: the stretch from a server picking an item up to putting it down, excluding all waiting. Engineers write it S.

Turn the service time upside down and you get how many she can finish per unit time when she never idles. One application per 80 s is 45 an hour. That is the service rate, written μ, said "mu". So μ (the service rate) is one divided by S (the service time).

Why keep two words for one desk? Because "how long a request took" in your logs is usually not S. It is S plus waiting. Keeping them apart is what lets you tell "my code got slower" from "my line got longer", and those are two problems with opposite fixes. Arjun's stopwatch measures S, the service time. The latency dashboard shows S plus waiting, and cannot tell you which of the two moved.

How busy is Dana? Utilisation

In section 3 Arjun divided 36 by 45 and got 0.8. That fraction is how busy Dana is: arrivals per hour, divided by how many the desk can serve per hour. Equivalently, it is the long-run fraction of time the desk is not idle. Engineers call it utilisation and write it ρ, said "rho".

Dana's ρ (utilisation) is 0.8. Meera's box at 72% CPU has a ρ (utilisation) of about 0.72.

With more than one desk, divide by the number of desks as well. Engineers write the number of desks as c. So utilisation is arrivals per unit time, divided by desks times finishes per desk per unit time. The formula sits in the table below, with its words next to it.

Why does the word exist? Because it is the single number that positions you on the cliff. Two systems with wildly different arrival rates and service times but the same ρ (utilisation) have the same shape of behaviour. This is the "80% allocated" of work-pressure-and-deadlines. It is also, near enough, the CPU utilisation on your dashboard, which is exactly why that dashboard is more dangerous than it looks.

Does the line ever settle down? Stability

What if 47 people an hour came to Dana's 45-an-hour desk? The line would grow all morning and never shrink.

Engineers say a line is stable when it settles down, meaning its length has a long-run average. It does that when utilisation is below 1. When utilisation reaches 1 or more, the line grows without bound, linearly in time, and no amount of waiting fixes it.

The word exists because ρ = 0.99 and ρ = 1.01 are not "nearly the same". They are categorically different systems. One has an answer; the other has no answer. Every formula in this lab silently assumes ρ < 1 and returns nonsense otherwise.

Dana at 0.8 is stable. At ρ = 1.05 the line reached 27 people after 1 minute, 385 after 5, and 1,619 after 30, with no sign of turning. That is failure F2 in section 11, the single most expensive failure mode in the lab.

How many are inside, and for how long? The four counts

Look back at the trace in section 4. There are two things you can count and two you can time. People standing in the line are one count. People in the building, the line plus the one at the desk, are another. Time spent waiting before Dana calls your name is one duration. Time from walking in to walking out is the other.

Engineers keep four names for those, and the difference between them matters.

  • Lq is the average number waiting in line.
  • L is the average number in the system: waiting plus being served.
  • Wq is the average time waiting.
  • W is the average time in the system, which is waiting plus its own service. So W (time in the system) = Wq (time waiting) + S (service time).

Why four words? Your queue-depth metric measures Lq, the number waiting. Your latency metric measures W, the time in the system. Little's Law relates L to W, and Lq to Wq. Pair them the wrong way round and the arithmetic quietly breaks. L is what a "queue depth" alert fires on. W is what customers feel.

How does Little's Law read in symbols?

Sentence one of section 6 was "people inside = rate in × time inside". In symbols that is L = λW: the number in the system equals the arrival rate times the time in the system.

For any system in steady state where nothing is created or destroyed, the time-average number inside equals the arrival rate times the average time each one spends inside. It assumes nothing about arrival pattern, service distribution, service order, or number of servers.

Why does it exist? It converts a quantity you can count cheaply into one you cannot. You can always count what is inside. You often cannot instrument how long each item stayed. Thread-pool sizing is Little's Law read backwards, and section 13 does exactly that.

Check it on Dana. At 80% busy her wait comes out at 326 s and her total time at 406 s. Little's Law says people inside = 0.01 arrivals per second × 406 s = 4.06. The simulator counted 4.07. ✅

How irregular is it? The coefficient of variation

In section 4 people did not arrive on a metronome, and that irregularity needs a number of its own.

Engineers use a scale where 0 is a metronome and 1 is "completely unpredictable". They call it the coefficient of variation and write it c². In words: the variance divided by the square of the mean. It is deliberately unitless, because it describes the shape of a distribution rather than its size. A stream of 5 ms requests and a stream of 5 s requests can have exactly the same c² (irregularity).

Three landmarks on that scale:

  • c² = 0 is perfectly regular. Clockwork.
  • c² = 1 is exponential, also called "memoryless". This is the classic no-pattern-at-all stream. How long you have waited tells you nothing about how much longer you will wait.
  • c² = 4 or 16 is clumpy, spiky, bursty.

Every line has two of them, one for arrivals and one for service. Engineers write the arrivals one c²ₐ and the service one c²ₛ. People arriving at Dana's desk at random gives c²ₐ (arrival irregularity) = 1. If 3% of applications need Omar the supervisor and take 16× as long, then c²ₛ (service irregularity) is about 16.

Why does the word exist? Because "the average request takes 80 ms" is two systems' worth of behaviour hiding under one number. The difference between them is 30× in latency, and the table in section 10 shows it. Every "the 99th percentile is bad but the median is fine" conversation is a c²ₛ conversation.

What is a queue called? Kendall notation

Papers name a queue with three slots: arrival pattern / service pattern / number of servers. Engineers call it Kendall notation and write it A/B/c.

The letters are a shorthand for irregularity. M means memoryless, which is c² = 1, the random case. D means deterministic, which is c² = 0, clockwork. G means general, meaning any shape at all.

So M/M/1 is random arrivals, random service, one server. M/D/1 is random arrivals with a fixed service time. G/G/c is the real world. Dana's desk, with random arrivals and a fixed 80 s procedure, is M/D/1. Your web service is G/G/c and always was.

Why does the notation exist? Every formula is valid for exactly one of these shapes, and papers quote their results by this name. It is the vocabulary you need in order to read anything. Section 12 shows that the M's are precisely where the assumptions live.

What comes out of the door? Offered load, throughput, goodput

Imagine 47 people an hour trying to get into Dana's 45-an-hour office. Forty-seven is what people asked for. Forty-five is what the desk delivered.

Engineers call the first offered load, which is λ (the arrival rate), and the second throughput, the completion rate. Below saturation the two are equal. Above it, throughput sticks at capacity while offered load keeps climbing, and the difference becomes queue growth, rejections, or timeouts.

Why the two words? Because a saturated system's throughput graph looks flat and healthy. Flat throughput under rising load is the signature of a system that is failing. On a dashboard it is indistinguishable from a system that is fine.

Now the nastier one. Some of those 45 people got their stamped passport after they had already given up and gone home. The work Dana did for them counted as throughput and was worth nothing.

Engineers call the work you did that somebody still wanted goodput: completions that arrive before the client's deadline. Throughput minus wasted work.

The simulator makes this brutal. At ρ (utilisation) = 1.2 for ten minutes, 9,122 requests were offered and 7,711 completed, which is exactly capacity. 0 were rejected. Of those completions, 7,647 were delivered after the client had already given up. Throughput: excellent. Customers served: 64. Across every queue size in that run, throughput was identically 7,711 while goodput ranged from 64 to 7,033. That is failure F6, and the whole argument for bounded queues.

What can you do at the door? Backpressure and load shedding

Two more words, both about the door.

Backpressure is any mechanism that propagates "I am full" upstream, so the source stops producing. Picture Dana's reception telling the street to slow down.

Load shedding is rejecting work quickly, a fast 503, instead of queueing it. Picture turning people away at the door with an apology. Shedding is what you do when you have no way to apply backpressure.

Why do the words exist? Because the alternative, an unbounded line, is not a neutral default. It is an active decision to convert a throughput problem into a latency problem, and then into a goodput problem. io-multiplexing named backpressure; distributed-rate-limiting is shedding with a policy. This lab supplies the reason both exist.

8. What two pictures should you carry, and where do they lie?

In one breath: Keep Dana's desk for thinking fast. Keep a single counter that goes up and down for thinking precisely. Then learn the four places the desk lies to you.

There are two mental models here. Carry both, and know where each one lies.

What is the simple picture? Dana's desk

A line of people, one clerk, work that takes as long as it takes. When you are reasoning quickly, this is the picture. A burst arrives, the line grows, the line drains during quiet spells, and how fast it drains depends on how much quiet there is.

What is the engineer's picture? A counter that goes up and down

Precisely, a queue is a single integer: the number of people in Dana's office. Call it n. Add two rules for changing it. Someone walks in, and n goes up by one. Dana finishes someone, and n goes down by one, but only if there was somebody to finish.

Here is that counter drawn as a chain. Read each arrow as "how often this step happens". The up-arrows happen at the arrival rate, λ. The down-arrows happen at the service rate, μ, but only when there is someone at the desk.

        λ          λ          λ          λ
   n=0 ───▶  n=1 ───▶  n=2 ───▶  n=3 ───▶  ...
       ◀───      ◀───      ◀───      ◀───
        μ          μ          μ          μ
    (only when n ≥ 1: an idle server cannot finish anything)

Arrivals push n up at rate λ (arrivals per unit time) whatever its value. Departures pull it down at rate μ (finishes per unit time), but only when n ≥ 1. That asymmetry, the down-arrow vanishing at zero while the up-arrow never does, is the entire reason queues behave the way they do. It is the state-machine spelling of "idle time cannot be banked".

From it, everything follows. Call the long-run chance of finding exactly n people in the office P(n), said "the probability of n". In the long run the flow up across any boundary between two neighbouring states must equal the flow down across it. Solve that and you get the whole distribution, and from the distribution the average.

Here is the derivation, one line at a time, words first and symbol last.

Where does the story lie to you?

Four places, and every one of them matters.

1. Real people leave. Dana's don't. In this lab's basic model an item waits forever, however long forever is. Real clients time out at 1 second and give up. Crucially, your server usually does not find out. It keeps working on a request nobody is listening to. That single mismatch is failure F6, and it is the reason goodput needed a separate word.

2. Real people who leave come back. Priya gives up, walks out, and rejoins the line an hour later. Every give-up adds to λ, the arrival rate. λ going up pushes ρ (utilisation) up, which pushes waiting up, which causes more give-ups, which pushes λ up again. Dana's model has no such loop. Your system does, and it can hold itself in a failed state long after the original cause is gone. That is F3, the most dramatic result in this lab.

3. The line has a wall. Dana's corridor is infinitely long. Your queue is a fixed-size array, a socket backlog with a specific integer limit, or a connection pool with a maxPoolSize. That is good news. Section 11's F6 shows the bounded queue outperforming the unbounded one by a factor of 100 on useful work.

4. Some "people" are worth more. Dana serves in arrival order because it is fair. A production system serving in arrival order under overload delivers almost nothing on time. The same system serving newest-first delivers 73% on time, which is F7 and a 104× difference. Fairness is a policy choice with a price you can count, not a law of nature.

Everything the story gets right lives in sections 1 to 10. Everything it gets wrong is section 11.

INTERMEDIATE MODEL — section 10

10. What number does the wait actually come out at?

In one breath: We build the model of Dana's line three times. The first two are what a careful engineer would honestly write down, and each of them is then broken by a run of the simulator. The third one survives.

Dana (the one server) is still at her desk. What changes now is that we stop describing her morning and start predicting it.

Model v1 — what if the world were clockwork?

The simplest honest model: arrivals are perfectly evenly spaced, and every job takes exactly the same time.

Concretely, one job every 100 ms, each taking exactly 80 ms. In the words of section 7, that is an arrival rate of 10 per second, a service time of 80 ms, and a utilisation of 0.8. In symbols, λ = 10/s, S = 80 ms, ρ = 0.8. It is the same 80% busy as Dana's morning.

Nothing here is random, so we can simply write it out:

job 1 arrives   0 ms   starts   0 ms   done   80 ms   waited 0
job 2 arrives 100 ms   starts 100 ms   done  180 ms   waited 0
job 3 arrives 200 ms   starts 200 ms   done  280 ms   waited 0
job 4 arrives 300 ms   starts 300 ms   done  380 ms   waited 0
...forever...

Every job finds the server free, because the server always finishes 20 ms before the next one arrives. The wait is 0. The line is empty. Utilisation is exactly 80%.

Run it for an hour with clockwork arrivals and clockwork work, which section 7 called D/D/1, and the simulator agrees: Wq = 0.00 ms, W = 80 ms, L = 0.8. The model is not approximately right. It is exactly right.

So at 80% utilisation a line can genuinely have zero waiting. Arjun's plan from section 3 is correct in this world.

🔮 Predict: Now change exactly one thing. Arrivals still average one per 100 ms, but they come at random times instead of evenly spaced. Every job still takes exactly 80 ms. What happens to the average wait?

Reveal

It goes from 0 ms to 161 ms. Two full service times of waiting appear out of nowhere. Nothing about the workload got bigger. The server is still busy exactly 80% of the time. The only thing that changed is when jobs arrive.

If you said "a little, maybe 10 or 20 ms", you were treating irregularity as noise sitting on top of the model. It is an input to the model. This is the moment Arjun's plan dies, and it dies for a reason with a name.

What breaks model v1? Randomness alone creates a line

Take random arrivals, a fixed 80 ms of work, and ρ = 0.8, which section 7 named M/D/1, and run it for an hour. The wait comes out at Wq = 161 ms, against the exact theoretical value ρS/(2(1−ρ)) of 160 ms.

Where did that come from? From the trace in section 4. Randomly-timed arrivals produce clumps, a clump creates a line, and the line drains during the gaps.

But the drain is slow. The server can only work off the backlog at 20 ms of progress per 100 ms of wall clock, because the other 80 ms go to the jobs that are still arriving. So the memory of a clump lasts a long time, and everyone who arrives during that memory pays for it.

The fix to v1 is not a fix. It is an admission: irregularity is an input to the model, not noise on top of it.

Model v2 — what if everything is random?

Second pass: make service times random too. A real handler takes 40 ms when the cache hits and 300 ms when it does not. It is not a constant, any more than Omar the supervisor takes the same time as Dana.

Use the "no pattern at all" distribution for both, the one section 7 called exponential with c² = 1. That is M/M/1, the canonical queue, and the one every closed-form result is written for.

Now the arithmetic works out, and it is short. Words first:

Quantity What it means in words The formula
how busy arrivals divided by finishes per unit time ρ = λ / μ
number in the system busy divided by idle L = ρ / (1 − ρ)
time in the system one divided by the gap between capacity and load W = 1 / (μ − λ)
time waiting the time in the system, scaled by how busy you are Wq = ρ / (μ − λ) = ρ·W
number waiting in line busy squared, divided by idle Lq = ρ² / (1 − ρ)

And the same five, written the way a paper writes them:

  ρ  = λ / μ                       how busy
  L  = ρ / (1 − ρ)                 average number in the system
  W  = 1 / (μ − λ)                 average time in the system
  Wq = ρ / (μ − λ)  =  ρ·W         average time waiting
  Lq = ρ² / (1 − ρ)

The one worth memorising is the second. The one worth understanding is W = 1/(μ − λ), the time in the system.

Look at what sits in that denominator. It is not capacity, and it is not load. It is the gap between them. Your latency is set by your spare capacity, and it is set by the reciprocal of it. Halve the spare capacity and you double the latency, wherever you started from.

That reciprocal is the vertical asymptote in the waiting-time formula. As load climbs towards capacity, the gap between them shrinks towards zero, and one divided by something shrinking to zero grows without limit. The curve does not bend upward gently. It goes up a wall.

Worked with Dana's numbers, scaled to milliseconds. Dana finishes 12.5 a second, 10 a second walk in, so the gap is 2.5 a second. One divided by 2.5 is 0.4 seconds, which is 400 ms. Of that, 80 ms is real work and 320 ms is pure waiting. Over 6 simulated hours across 8 seeds the run gave W = 403 ms, Wq = 323 ms, L = 4.03.

Check Little's Law on the spot. It says the number in the system should be the arrival rate times the time in the system: 10 × 0.403 = 4.03. The simulator's independently-counted time-average number in the system is 4.03. That is not an approximation. It is the same quantity counted two different ways.

Three practical consequences fall straight out of the three factors.

Reducing the service time helps twice. It appears as a multiplier, and it also lowers utilisation. Making a handler 2× faster at fixed traffic takes ρ from 0.8 to 0.4, and the wait from 326 ms to about 27 ms. That is a 12× latency improvement from a 2× code improvement.

Smoothing arrivals is free capacity. Anything that turns a burst into a trickle moves c²ₐ down: a small client-side buffer, jittered cron times, a shaper. It buys you exactly as much as buying hardware would.

Cutting the tail of service times beats cutting the mean. The service irregularity c²ₛ is driven by the slow requests. Fixing the 3% that take 16× removes more waiting than making the other 97% faster. That is where "one bad query" comes from.

Why is the mean a lie? What percentiles do

Every table above quotes an average and a 99th percentile, and the 99th is always several times the average. That is not noise. It is structural.

🔮 Predict: At ρ = 0.8 the average total time is 406 ms. You now push to ρ = 0.9, where the average total time is 824 ms, roughly double. What happens to the ratio between the 99th percentile and the average: does it get worse, stay put, or improve?

Reveal

It stays put. At ρ = 0.8 the ratio is 1,886 ÷ 406 = 4.64. At ρ = 0.9 it is 3,735 ÷ 824 = 4.53. The multiplier is a constant of the distribution, not a function of load.

If you said "worse", you had the right instinct about tails and the wrong mechanism. The tail does not stretch relative to the mean. The mean itself is being dragged up the cliff, and the tail rides along at four and a half times the distance.

In M/M/1 the waiting time is exponentially distributed. In words: the chance that a request is still inside after some length of time falls off by a constant factor for each further chunk of time you wait. In symbols:

  P(total time > t)  =  e^(−t/W)          (exactly exponential, mean W)
  P(waiting  > t)    =  ρ · e^(−t/W)      (an atom of size 1−ρ at zero: no wait)

The tail decays with a time constant of W, the whole time in the system. So the percentile multipliers are fixed numbers:

  p99   = W · ln(100)  = 4.61 × W        at ρ=0.8 the run gave:  1,886 / 406 = 4.64  ✅
  p99.9 = W · ln(1000) = 6.91 × W        at ρ=0.8 the run gave:  2,663 / 406 = 6.55  ✅

The percentile multiplier is a constant. It does not depend on load at all, which means the 99th percentile tracks the average, and the average is 1/(1−ρ). Everything the cliff does to your mean, it does to your tail, four and a half times over.

Two consequences follow, and both change what goes into a design document.

A 99th-percentile target sets your utilisation ceiling, not your average-latency target. If you need the 99th percentile under 1 s with S = 80 ms, you need the average total time under about 220 ms, which means ρ < 0.64. Not 0.9.

If one request fans out to 10 backends and waits for all of them, your 99th percentile is roughly each backend's 99.9th. For all 10 to come in under some threshold you need F(t)¹⁰ = 0.99, which means F(t) = 0.999. At ρ = 0.8 that turns an 1,886 ms 99th percentile into a 2,663 ms one.

And the fan-out's median is each backend's 93rd percentile, because 0.93¹⁰ ≈ 0.5. So your typical request now experiences what used to be a bad one. Fan-out does not average the tail away. It walks you up it. This is Dean and Barroso's "tail at scale", and it is why hedged requests exist.

F1 — Why did latency move when nobody changed the code?

In one breath: Traffic rose a little, spare capacity fell a lot, and latency is set by the spare capacity.

Nothing at all happened to Dana. She still takes 80 seconds. Two more people an hour started walking in, and the line got much longer than two people's worth.

In your own service it shows up as "latency regression with no code change", and it is the reason a latency dashboard needs a utilisation panel next to it. The honest alert is not on latency. It is on 1/(1−ρ) crossing a threshold, or equivalently on spare capacity falling below a floor.

In one breath: Priya gives up and comes back, which is one extra arrival. Multiply that by a whole client fleet and the system holds itself under.

The same transient. One system absorbs it inside four minutes. The other never recovers. The cause was a burst that lasted one instant and ended twenty minutes ago.

Why? Retries create a positive feedback loop. The effective arrival rate is the real one multiplied by one plus the retry rate. And the retry rate is itself an increasing function of latency, which in turn climbs with the effective arrival rate.

Below a threshold the loop has gain under 1 and dies out. Above it the gain is over 1, and the system has a second stable state that is entirely failed. Engineers call this metastable failure, and its defining property is that removing the trigger does not restore service.

Nothing survives this one. Everything is lost, including the usual remedy of waiting.

The production signature is a system that stays down after the cause is fixed, and comes back only when you stop the traffic entirely. "Turn it off and on again" works here, and this is why. The defences, in order of how much they help:

  1. A retry budget: cap retries at a small fraction of total requests, around 10%, across the whole client fleet rather than per request.
  2. Exponential backoff with jitter, which lowers arrival irregularity as well as the arrival rate.
  3. A circuit breaker, which sets the retry rate to zero exactly when it matters most.
  4. Never retry a timeout that was caused by queueing. A fast 503 is safe to retry. A slow timeout is not, because a slow timeout means the queue is full and you are about to make it fuller.

Ben runs the load test. His harness sends a person in, waits for them to come out, then sends the next one. When Dana freezes for a full second, Ben's harness politely stops sending anyone at all.

You cannot create this one in the simulator, and that is the point. The failure lives in the measurement, not in the system.

Ben's generator has 50 threads. Each sends a request, waits for the response, records the latency, and sends the next. It reports a 99th percentile of 120 ms, and he ships.

Trace what the harness actually recorded. Suppose the server stalls for 1 second at the 10-second mark.

In an open-loop world, which is the world real users live in, users keep arriving during the stall. At 10 arrivals a second that is 10 more requests, and the tenth one waited 1,000 ms. Your own 99th percentile must include all ten.

In Ben's closed-loop generator, all 50 threads are blocked on their in-flight request. Zero new requests are sent during the stall. When it clears, each thread records one bad sample, so 50 bad samples instead of 500, and then resumes. The stall is under-counted by a factor of 10. Worse, the queue that real users would have built was never created, so its knock-on latency was never measured either.

Why does this matter so much? A closed-loop generator has a built-in negative feedback loop: the system getting slower reduces its offered load. Real users do not do this. The generator's arrival rate is not a property of the test. It is a property of the system under test.

The numbers are arithmetically correct. What is lost is their meaning.

Two fixes, and a second trap. Use an open-loop or constant-arrival-rate generator: wrk2, Gatling with a constant-rate injection profile, JMeter with a throughput shaper, or k6 with constant-arrival-rate. Alternatively, correct after the fact by back-filling the requests that should have been sent during each stall.

The second trap is where you measure. Server-side timers usually start when the handler picks the request up, which excludes the socket accept backlog and the load balancer's queue. Those are the two places the waiting actually happened. Measure at the client, or at the earliest possible point in the server.

F5 — Why does one slow tenant become everybody's problem?

In one breath: Omar's 22-minute case is in front of you in the same line, and your own two-minute case cannot go round it.

Three applications in every hundred need Omar the supervisor, and Omar takes 16× as long as Dana does. You are behind one of them. Your own paperwork is fast, and that fact is now completely irrelevant to how long you stand there.

To create it, set service variability to spiky (c²=16) at ρ = 0.8. Then 97% of jobs take about 41 ms and 3% take about 1,320 ms. Watch the queue box during one of the long jobs.

From the Kingman table in section 10: the wait goes from 326 ms to 2,730 ms. That is an 8× latency regression for every customer, caused by 3% of requests, at unchanged utilisation and unchanged average service time.

Why? A first-come-first-served line behind one server means the 1,320 ms job blocks the 41 ms jobs behind it. Those jobs' own service time has nothing to do with their latency. This is the c²ₛ term of Kingman's formula made physical.

Fairness survives, in the strictest sense: everyone is served in the order they arrived, and every answer is correct. What is lost is any per-tenant latency guarantee. The fast tenants pay for the slow one.

In your own service this arrives as the "noisy neighbour" ticket. The real defences are worth listing, because one of them contradicts section 10 on purpose:

  • Separate pools per class of work. This is the one case where the pooling result of section 10 should be overridden, because you are deliberately trading average latency for isolation.
  • A concurrency limit per tenant, which engineers call a bulkhead.
  • Timeouts sized to the fast path, not to the slow one.
  • Shortest-Job-First, or a size-based queue split, where the work allows it. It provably reduces the mean wait and it starves the big jobs.

Note that HTTP/2 solved exactly this at the protocol layer, with multiplexed streams over one TCP connection, and then re-created it at the transport layer. That is why HTTP/3 moved to QUIC.

F6 — Why does a bigger queue serve fewer customers?

In one breath: Past a certain length, every extra place in the line is a place where somebody waits, gives up, and is then served anyway for nothing.

Dana's corridor holds as many people as want to stand in it. Nobody at the back will be seen before their bus leaves, and Dana does not know that, so she stamps their passports anyway after they have gone home.

Create it with scenario 7: overload at ρ = 1.2, clients giving up after 1 s, ten minutes, 9,000 requests offered. Vary only the maximum queue length. Watch the "answered in time" column and nothing else.

🔮 Predict: 9,000 requests hit a service that can only finish 7,700, and every client gives up after 1 second. You may cap the queue at any length you like. Which cap gets an answer to the most customers in time: unbounded, 100, 20, 5, or 0?

Reveal

The rule of thumb falls straight out of that sentence. The last job in a full queue must still finish in time, so maxQueue ≈ (deadline / S) − c. Here that is 1000/80 − 1 = 11.5. The row at 11 delivers 6,352, within 10% of the best row in the table, derived from two numbers you already knew. ✅

Every queue you own has a bound, whether you chose it or not. The ServerSocket backlog is one. So is ThreadPoolExecutor's LinkedBlockingQueue, which defaults to unbounded and is the JDK's most expensive default. So are your connection pool's wait queue and the load balancer's surge queue. Bound them explicitly, reject fast when full, and drop jobs whose deadline has already passed before starting them. That last one is a two-line check, and it recovers most of the gap between the first row and the fourth.

F7 — Why is serving people in order the wrong thing under overload?

In one breath: The person who has waited longest is the person most likely to have already left.

Dana serves in arrival order because it is fair. Under overload, the person at the front of her line has been standing there longest, which means they are the most likely to have given up and gone. She spends the whole morning stamping passports for people who are already on a bus.

Run scenario 7, then flip the ordering control to newest-first. Same overload, maximum queue 200, 1-second deadline, ten minutes.

order answered in time answered too late fast 503s
oldest first (FIFO) 64 7,647 1,220
newest first (LIFO) 6,660 1,051 1,220

And the same two runs, seen as latency and waste:

order median 99th pct server time wasted
oldest first (FIFO) 15,010 ms 18,153 ms 99%
newest first (LIFO) 179 ms 36,366 ms 15%

Why? Under overload the oldest job in the queue is the one most likely to be already doomed. Serving oldest-first spends every cycle on the job least likely to still be wanted, which is how 99% of server time becomes waste. Serving newest-first takes the freshest, most-likely-still-wanted job: 73% of clients get an answer in time instead of 0.7%, a 104× improvement in goodput from changing poll() to pollLast().

Throughput is identical again. What is lost is fairness, visibly. The 99th percentile doubled to 36 s, because some unlucky jobs at the bottom of the stack are never reached at all. That is the trade, and it is a real one.

Newest-first is not a default. It is an overload mode. Serve oldest-first normally, and switch when queue depth crosses a threshold. Facebook's production load shedder does this, and so does the adaptive LIFO in Google's SRE book. Do it together with deadline-dropping from F6 and the 99th-percentile problem largely disappears, because the starved jobs get rejected quickly instead of sitting there.

F8 — Why does the same work hurt more when it arrives all at once?

In one breath: Fifty people through the door at the same instant means the fiftieth waits for forty-nine applications, and no amount of capacity below fifty desks changes that.

A coach pulls up outside Dana's office four times an hour and unloads fifty people. The same 200 people a morning. The same 80 seconds each. A completely different morning.

Create it with scenario 5, or by setting arrivals to a batch of 50 every 5 s. Same average rate, same utilisation.

arrival pattern ρ Wq 99th pct of W worst queue
smooth (random) 0.80 326 ms 1,977 ms 47
batch of 50 every 5 s 0.80 1,971 ms 4,728 ms 70

Six times the wait, from re-arranging when identical work arrives. This is the arrival-irregularity term c²ₐ, and it is the term you can most often actually control.

Why? The 50th job in a batch waits for 49 × 80 ms = 3.9 s before it starts, by construction. No amount of capacity below "50 servers" changes that.

Everything except latency survives. What is lost is the latency target, four times a minute, on a schedule.

In your own fleet, every 0 * * * * cron fires at the same instant. So does every client that retries after exactly 1,000 ms. So does every cache entry that expires at the same time-to-live, which is the "thundering herd" of caching, and which is this failure. The fixes all have the same shape: add jitter. Randomise cron minutes, jitter retry backoff, jitter time-to-live values. Jitter is not politeness. It is a direct reduction in arrival irregularity, and by Kingman it is worth exactly as much as buying servers.

12. What is guaranteed, and what is only approximate?

In one breath: Exactly one thing in this lab is a theorem. Everything else is an approximation with a stated range, and the approximations are all optimistic.

Dana's morning obeyed one law exactly and several formulas approximately. Here is which is which, so you know what to bet on.

Which single formula is a theorem?

Little's Law is an identity, not a model. In words: the number inside equals the rate in, times the time inside. In symbols, L = λW.

It follows from a counting argument. Draw the number in Dana's office over time. The area under that curve can be summed two ways. Sum it by time intervals and you get the average number inside times the length of the window, which is L × T. Sum it by individual people and you get the number of arrivals times the average time each one stayed, which is arrivals × W. Equate the two and divide by the window.

It therefore holds:

  • for any arrival process, however bursty or correlated;
  • for any service-time distribution, however heavy-tailed;
  • for any service order — first-come, last-come, priority, random;
  • for any number of servers;
  • for any subsystem you can draw a boundary around, including one that contains other queues inside it.

It requires only two things. The system must be in steady state, with nothing accumulating without bound over the measurement window. And you must use consistent boundaries: count what is inside the same boundary you timed people across.

At utilisations of 0.5, 0.8, 0.9 and 0.95, the arrival rate times the average time and the independently-counted time-average number inside agreed to within 0.01 jobs at every point. That is why it is the one formula worth trusting in production. It hands you the third quantity for free, always.

What is only an approximation, and how wrong is it?

Everything else. In descending order of trustworthiness:

  • The M/M/1 formulas are exact, for a system that is genuinely M/M/1. Yours is not. They are still the right first estimate, because the shape 1/(1−ρ) is universal even when the constant is not.
  • Kingman's formula is an approximation. It is exact in the heavy-traffic limit as ρ approaches 1, and conservative at low ρ. It landed within 5% across eight irregularity combinations at ρ = 0.8. Expect it to be optimistic for very heavy-tailed service times, where the mean of the service distribution stops being informative at all.
  • Erlang-C, the multi-server result, is exact under the same M assumptions. It produced the pooling numbers above and matched them to the digit.

Which four statements never break?

Invariant What it says in words The check it gives you
work conservation a server is never idle while work waits all such disciplines have identical throughput
flow balance in steady state, departures equal arrivals if they differ, no average means anything
stability a steady state exists only below full capacity past it, the useful quantity is the growth rate
the two sums total time is waiting plus service catches instrumentation errors immediately

Each of those deserves a sentence of its own.

Work conservation. A work-conserving server is never idle while work waits. Every discipline in this lab, both oldest-first and newest-first, is work-conserving, so they have identical throughput and identical mean number in the system. F7's table shows both at exactly 7,711. Discipline redistributes waiting. It cannot create or destroy it. Priority, preemption and dropping are not work-conserving, and that is precisely where their power comes from.

Flow balance. In steady state, departures equal arrivals. If they differ, you are not in steady state, and no average you compute means anything.

Stability. A utilisation below 1 is necessary and sufficient for a steady state to exist with a single queue. At ρ ≥ 1 all the averages above are ∞, and the useful quantity becomes the growth rate, (λ − μ).

The two sums. W = Wq + S and L = Lq + ρc, always, by definition. Both are useful for catching instrumentation errors.

What did the model quietly assume?

Four things, and every one of them makes reality worse than the tables say.

We assumed independence. Real arrivals are correlated. A page load fires 30 requests; a failure causes a retry which causes another failure. Correlation makes everything worse than these formulas say, and it is why F3 is not predicted by any equation here.

We assumed stationarity. A real arrival rate has a daily cycle, and "the average over the day" is meaningless when the peak hour is 3× the mean. Apply every formula in this lab at the peak, never at the average.

We assumed the service time is a property of the job. In reality service slows down as load rises, through context switching, cache pressure, garbage collection and lock contention. So the real capacity falls as the arrival rate rises, which makes the real cliff sharper and earlier than the tables above. Every number in this lab is therefore an optimistic bound.

We ignored abandonment in the analytic model and only simulated it. The models that include abandonment, called Erlang-A, are gentler than these, because giving up is a natural safety valve. It is one your system may or may not have.

What is the complexity, for completeness?

Queue operations themselves are O(1). The complexity that matters here is not computational. It is that latency is O(1/(1−ρ)) in utilisation and O(c²) in irregularity. Those two are the only asymptotics in the subject, and you now know both.

How big should the pool be? Little's Law read backwards

Little's Law says the number inside equals the rate in times the time inside. Read it the other way round: the number of workers you need is the arrival rate times the time each request occupies one of them.

Work it through. The offered load is 100 requests a second, and the service time is 200 ms, mostly spent waiting on a database. The concurrency needed is the arrival rate times the service time, written λ × S, so 100 × 0.2 = 20.

🔮 Predict: Little's Law says you need 20 workers. You provision exactly 20. What is the average total time per request: about 200 ms, about 400 ms, or something much worse?

Reveal

3.2 seconds, and climbing. Twenty workers puts you at exactly ρ = 1.00, which is the F2 cliff. If you said "200 ms", you read Little's Law as a sizing answer rather than as a floor. It tells you the smallest pool that could possibly keep up, and "just keeps up" is the one operating point with no steady state at all.

Twenty simulated minutes across three seeds:

pool size ρ throughput W 99th pct of W
10 1.00 50.1/s ⚠️ 298 s (growing) 593 s
15 1.00 75.1/s ⚠️ 149 s (growing) 298 s

Little's Law gives you the floor, not the setting. At exactly 20 you sit at ρ = 1.00, with latency growing without bound. At 25, which is 25% headroom, you get 209 ms, being 200 ms of work plus 9 ms of waiting. Going to 40 buys 9 more milliseconds and costs 15 threads.

So the rule is: size to the arrival rate times the service time, then divide by your target utilisation. For a target of 0.8 that is λS/0.8 = 25. Choose the target utilisation from your 99th-percentile requirement, using the cliff table in section 10.

Look at rows 10 and 15 as well. Throughput is pinned at the number of desks divided by the service time, c/S, giving 50 a second and 75 a second. A too-small pool does not make things slow. It makes your service a smaller service.

What belongs on the dashboard?

Most latency dashboards cannot tell "my code got slower" from "my queue got longer", and those are two problems with completely different fixes. Fix that first, then the other five.

  1. Split the total time into waiting and service. Timestamp the request when it is accepted, not when the handler starts. Emit both time_in_queue and time_in_handler. If you only ever add one metric from this lab, add time_in_queue.
  2. Plot utilisation next to latency, and plot 1/(1−ρ) if you want an honest vertical axis. On that axis the cliff is visible.
  3. Alert on queue depth and its derivative. A rising queue depth is the earliest signal of ρ ≥ 1, and it leads latency by minutes.
  4. Alert on spare capacity, not utilisation. A threshold of "ρ > 0.8" is arbitrary. The quantity μ − λ falling below some requests a second is what actually sets latency in F1 and recovery time in F2.
  5. Track goodput, not just throughput. Count responses delivered before the client's deadline. In the F6 runs, throughput was identical across every queue setting while goodput varied from 64 to 7,033.
  6. Report percentiles, never means, and never average percentiles across hosts or time buckets. The 99th percentile of a set of 99th percentiles is not a 99th percentile. Keep histograms.

Why is a concurrency limit a better knob than a rate limit?

distributed-rate-limiting sets a limit on requests per second. Queueing theory says the more robust limit is on concurrent requests in flight.

Here is why. By Little's Law, a concurrency limit of N automatically enforces an arrival rate no greater than N divided by the service time, and it self-adjusts when the service time changes. If your database gets slower, a rate limit lets the same arrival rate through into a system that can no longer take it. A concurrency limit tightens on its own.

This is why Netflix's concurrency-limits library and Envoy's adaptive concurrency exist, and why a bulkhead, which is just a small semaphore, is usually better protection than a token bucket.

What are the two words for what you do at the door?

The failures in section 11 all end in the same place. A decision, made when a request shows up or when a worker picks it up, about whether this piece of work should exist at all. Production has two words for that decision.

Admission control is the policy that decides what you accept. It is the sum of four settings you now know how to choose. The concurrency limit says how many may be in flight. The queue bound says how many may wait. The ordering says who goes first. The deadline rule says when to stop bothering. Most services have all four and chose none of them. They inherited a library's defaults, and those defaults were written by somebody with no knowledge of your deadline.

Deadline propagation is what makes the fourth of those possible. The client sends an absolute instant, "this expires at 14:03:07.900", rather than a duration. Every service then passes the remaining budget to everything it calls.

Now, at the moment a worker picks a job off the queue, it can ask a question it otherwise cannot answer: is anybody still waiting for this? If the remaining budget is smaller than the expected service time, the job is dropped without being started.

That single check is the difference between the top row and the bottom row of the F6 table, 64 useful answers against 7,033. It needs no coordination, no extra round trip, and about two lines of code. gRPC has it built in, as context.WithDeadline. Most HTTP stacks make you carry it yourself in a header. It is the highest value-per-line change in this entire lab.

Which five sentences should you carry out of here?

  1. Latency is set by spare capacity, not capacity — W = 1/(μ−λ).
  2. Utilisation, irregularity and service time are three independent multipliers, and irregularity is usually the cheapest one to fix.
  3. Little's Law is free and always true — use it to size pools, and to cross-check any two metrics you have.
  4. Every queue must be bounded, and the bound is small — roughly deadline/S − c.
  5. Above ρ = 1 nothing recovers on its own. Shed load, hard and early.

Four things get confused with this lab. Here is the one sentence that separates each of them.

Queueing theory against throughput tuning. Tuning raises capacity, which helps. Queueing theory says that at ρ = 0.8 you can get the same latency win by lowering service irregularity, by jittering a cron or splitting a slow tenant out, for none of the engineering cost.

Queueing theory against circuit breakers. A breaker is a reaction to a queue that has already failed, which is F3. Queueing theory tells you the bound that stops it filling in the first place, which is F6.

ρ/(1−ρ) against 1/(1−ρ). The first is the queue multiplier Wq/S, and is shaped like the number waiting in line. The second is the total-time multiplier W/S. They differ by exactly one service time. Quote the wrong one and you are off by 25% at ρ = 0.8, and by 5% at ρ = 0.95.

Queue depth against latency. They are related by Little's Law and are not interchangeable as alerts. Queue depth leads latency, and at ρ ≥ 1 latency has no steady value to alert on at all.

Rung 1 — EXPLAIN

1.1 Two things are wrong with it.

First, busy-ness is not the same as the delay people feel. At 80% busy on a single server, the average request already spends five times its own work time inside the service — 406 ms measured, for 80 ms of actual work. There is no headroom left in the sense the sentence means; the damage has already happened.

Second, the remaining 20% is not usable in the way it sounds. Work does not arrive evenly. When two requests turn up in the same instant, one has to wait — and the quiet second that came before cannot be spent on it, because idle time cannot be saved. Every burst leaves a backlog, and the backlog is only paid off out of future idle time. At 80% busy there is little of that left, so the backlogs last longer and land on more people. Take it to 90% and the average total time doubles again; take it to 95% and it doubles again after that.

The useful reframing: what sets latency is spare capacity, not capacity. Going 72% → 90% did not remove 18% of anything. It removed 64% of the slack (3.5 spare req/s down to 1.25), and the latency multiplied by almost exactly the inverse of that.

1.2 It says the number of things inside a system equals how fast they arrive times how long each one stays. Its value is that you can usually measure two of those three cheaply and the third not at all.

Concrete example: how many worker threads you need. You can measure requests per second at the load balancer (100/s) and average handler time in a profiler (200 ms). You cannot easily measure "how many requests are in flight at once" — but Little's Law gives it to you: 100 × 0.2 = 20. (See 1.3's sibling warning in 6.2: that is the floor, not the setting.)

The other everyday use is as a consistency check: if your queue-depth metric and your latency metric do not satisfy depth = rate × latency, one of them is instrumented wrongly, and finding out which is usually a good afternoon's work.

1.3

  • Utilisation vs throughput. Throughput is how much you finish per second; utilisation is what fraction of the time you were busy doing it. Two systems can have the same throughput at wildly different utilisation, and one of them is in trouble.
  • Throughput vs goodput. Throughput counts answers produced. Goodput counts answers somebody was still waiting for. Under overload the first stays flat and healthy-looking while the second goes to zero — measured, F6: identical 7,711 throughput at every queue size, goodput ranging from 64 to 7,033.
  • Waiting vs service time. Service time is your code. Waiting is the queue in front of your code. Your latency dashboard shows their sum and cannot tell you which one moved, which is why "latency went up" is not yet a diagnosis.
  • Backpressure vs load shedding. Backpressure tells the sender to slow down and keeps the work. Shedding throws the work away. You shed when you have no way to make the sender listen — which, for an HTTP service facing the public internet, is always.

1.4 Because they are not two points on one curve; they are two different kinds of system.

Below capacity, the queue length is a random walk with a downward drift. It wanders up during a burst and comes home during the quiet. There is a long-run average, formulas produce finite numbers, and waiting it out works.

At or above capacity the drift is upward and there is no home to return to. The queue grows linearly and forever, at exactly (arrivals − capacity) jobs per second. There is no average to compute; every formula in the lab returns infinity. Waiting does not help, because the queue has no memory of being small.

The practical consequence is that the only useful actions change sides: below capacity you tune, above it you must reduce arrivals or raise capacity, and nothing else will ever work. Measured at just 5% over: 27 waiting after one minute, 385 after five, 1,619 after thirty, with the oldest job having waited two minutes and still climbing.

Rung 2 — IDENTIFY

2.1 With default settings and a 20-minute measurement you should see approximately: utilisation 80%, average wait ≈ 320 ms, average total ≈ 400 ms, 99th percentile ≈ 1,900 ms. (Shorter runs come out lower, because the simulation starts with an empty queue and that transient is a real part of a short window — a 60-second run typically reports 70% and 234 ms.)

2.2 Average wait is Wq; average total is W. Their difference is exactly the service time, W = Wq + S, by definition — 400 − 320 = 80 ms. If your two readings do not differ by about 80 ms, you misread one of them.

2.3 Judgement question — the point is that the inspector answers all three from the screen: it prints how long the job has waited, its position in the line, and (when a give-up time is set) how many milliseconds remain before its client abandons it. A job at position 12 with one server needs 12 × 80 = 960 ms before it even starts.

2.4 Around 7–8 req/s (ρ ≈ 0.6) it stops being visually straight, and past 10 (ρ = 0.8) it climbs steeply. Note how much of the x-axis looks safe: the curve is nearly flat across the entire left two-thirds, which is exactly why the cliff is invisible on a dashboard that plots utilisation linearly.

2.5 About 3% of jobs are slow, and they take roughly 16.5× the average — with S = 80 ms that is 97% at ~42 ms and 3% at ~1,320 ms. The average service time is unchanged at 80 ms. That is the whole trap: the average is identical, utilisation is identical, and the average wait goes from 326 ms to 2,730 ms.

Rung 3 — PREDICT

3.1 — 6×, not 2×. Halving service time does two things at once: it halves the multiplier S and it halves utilisation (0.8 → 0.4), which collapses the 1/(1−ρ) factor from 5 to 1.67.

  before:  W = 1/(12.5 − 10) = 400 ms      measured 401 ms
  after:   W = 1/(25   − 10) =  67 ms      measured  67 ms

The waiting component improves even more — 321 ms → 27 ms, a factor of 12. This is the single best argument for performance work: at high utilisation the payoff is quadratic-ish, not linear.

3.2 — About 8×. Kingman's variability factor is (c²ₐ + c²ₛ)/2, so going from (1, 0) to (1, 16) takes it from 0.5 to 8.5. Measured: 156 ms → 2,692 ms, a factor of 17 against the clockwork-service case — and 326 ms → 2,730 ms, a factor of 8.4, against the fully-random baseline, which is what the question's "both averaging 80 ms" most naturally means. Either way, an order of magnitude, at unchanged load and unchanged average work.

3.3 — About one fifth. Measured at 80% busy: 4 separate lines give 320 ms of waiting, one shared line gives 61 ms — 5.2× better. Rung 4 — MODIFY

4.1 The wait goes from 0 ms to about 156 ms — from nothing to two full service times, at unchanged load and unchanged utilisation. You moved c²ₐ, the arrival-variability term, from 0 to 1.

What broke is the naive capacity plan itself: "demand fits inside capacity, so nobody waits" is true in a clockwork world and false everywhere else. The queue you just created is made entirely of the clumps that randomness produces, paid for out of idle time that has already been thrown away.

4.2 The metric that got ~110× better is answered in time (64 → 7,033). The metric that did not change is throughput (7,711 either way).

Throughput did not change because both configurations are work-conserving — the server is never idle while work waits, so it completes exactly as many jobs per second either way. Capacity is capacity. What the queue bound changed was which jobs got that capacity: with an unbounded queue every completion went to someone who had already left. You did not make the machine faster; you stopped it wasting itself.

4.3 You lost latency, by roughly 5× — a liveness degradation, not a safety one. Nothing incorrect happens; every job still gets exactly one correct answer in a legal order. It simply takes longer, because a server can now sit idle while somebody waits in another line and there is no path between them.

(The reason anyone would still do this is in 6.4: separate lines buy isolation, and isolation is a safety property for tenants even though it costs liveness for everyone.)

4.4 Judgement question, but the shape of the answer is fixed: with retries on at 85% base load, it typically takes only one or two 40-job bursts before the queue stops returning to its old level, because each burst pushes latency past the 600 ms deadline, which manufactures retries, which raise the arrival rate above capacity and keep it there.

With retries off you cannot reproduce it at all, no matter how many bursts you inject. Each burst is absorbed and the system returns to its old state. The difference is not the size of the disturbance — it is the presence of a feedback loop with gain above 1. This is the definition of a metastable failure, and it is why "we were only slightly over" is not a defence.

4.5 Answered in time gets dramatically better (64 → 6,660, and server time wasted drops from 99% to 15%). The 99th percentile gets dramatically worse (18.2 s → 36.4 s).

The customers who pay are the ones who arrived earliest and are now at the bottom of the stack — they may never be served at all. That is a real cost, and it is why newest-first is an overload mode rather than a default: run oldest-first normally, switch above a depth threshold, and pair it with dropping jobs whose deadline has already passed, which converts most of that starvation into a fast rejection the client can act on.

5.5 The loop, five links:

  1. A burst arrives, which causes the queue to grow past what one service time can absorb.
  2. The queue grows, which causes total latency to exceed the client's deadline.
  3. Latency exceeds the deadline, which causes clients to give up and retry.
  4. Retries arrive, which causes the offered arrival rate to rise above capacity.
  5. Arrivals exceed capacity, which causes the queue to grow — back to link 1, now with no external input required.

Once the loop closes, removing the original burst changes nothing, because the loop's input is its own output.

Rung 6 — SPOT THE FLAW

6.1 — This design's failure is that it never rejects anyone, which is exactly how it ends up serving nobody.

Concrete run, using the measured numbers: 15 requests/second arrive at a service that can complete 12.5, with a client deadline. Over ten minutes, 9,122 requests turn up.

  • With the unbounded queue: 7,711 completed, 64 of them before the deadline, 7,647 delivered to clients that had closed the connection, and 1,410 still queued at the end. Median latency 51 seconds. Every worker thread is 99% occupied producing answers nobody will read.
  • With a queue bounded at 5: identical 7,711 completions, of which 7,033 arrive in time, and 2,060 people get an immediate rejection they can act on.

The work it is doing has a name: it is not throughput, it is waste — the lab's term is that its goodput is 64. The design confused "nobody is rejected" with "everybody is served", and those become opposites under overload.

The second, slower failure: LinkedBlockingQueue with no capacity argument is unbounded in memory too, so a long enough overload ends in OutOfMemoryError rather than a graceful degradation.

6.2 — Three mistakes.

  1. 200 threads is the floor, not the setting. λ × S = 4,000 × 0.05 = 200 is the concurrency at which utilisation is exactly 1.00 — the saturation point, where latency has no steady value at all. Measured directly (λ=100/s, S=200 ms, so the floor is 20): at 20 servers, W = 3.2 s and rising; at 25, W = 209 ms. The rule is λS ÷ ρ_target; for ρ_target = 0.8 they need 250.

  2. A queue of 10,000 guarantees the back of it is answered far too late. With 200 threads at 50 ms each, the queue drains at 4,000/s, so job number 10,000 waits 2.5 seconds before it starts. Any client deadline under that makes the whole tail of the queue pure waste. The bound should come from the deadline: queue ≈ servers × (deadline/S − 1).

  3. Nothing in the plan mentions variability. "Peak is 4,000/s and each takes 50 ms" fixes ρ and S and says nothing about c²ₐ or c²ₛ — the middle term of Kingman's formula, which is worth up to 8× on its own (measured: 326 ms versus 2,730 ms at identical ρ and identical mean service time). If their traffic is bursty or their service time has a fat tail, a correctly-sized pool still misses its latency target.

A fourth, if you want it: "peak is 4,000/s" is almost certainly a one-minute average, and the within-minute peak is higher.

6.3 — The sequence, and the point of no return.

  1. The service is at 70% utilisation; total latency averages ~167 ms, p99 around 770 ms. Some requests already exceed 1 s.
  2. A minor disturbance — a garbage-collection pause, a slow database query, a deploy — pushes latency up briefly. Now a few percent of requests cross the 1-second timeout.
  3. Each of those becomes up to four requests (the original plus three retries), sent immediately, with no backoff, so they land in the same congested moment that caused the timeout.
  4. Offered load rises. Utilisation rises. Latency rises. More requests cross 1 s.
  5. The point of no return is the moment the retry-inflated arrival rate exceeds capacity — the moment utilisation reaches 1.00. From then on the queue grows without bound, every request times out, every timeout produces three more, and the system's own output is its input.

After that moment, removing the original trigger does nothing: the GC pause is long over and the system stays down. The only exit is to stop the traffic entirely — which is precisely why "turn it off and on again" is the fix that works when nothing else does.

The three flaws in the policy, in order of severity: retrying immediately with no backoff (concentrates retries into the worst moment, and raises c²ₐ); three retries (a 4× load multiplier, when a healthy system has ~30% headroom); and no fleet-wide budget, so nothing caps the total.

6.4 — Because they turned one shared line into N separate ones, and idle capacity in a separate line is unreachable.

Measured at 80% busy with four servers: one shared line waits 61 ms, four separate lines wait 320 ms — 5.2× worse, with the same threads and the same traffic. The mechanism is that tenant B's thread can now sit idle while tenant A's request waits, and there is no path between them. The effect is worse at low utilisation (11× at 50% busy), because that is when idleness is most common.

When it is nevertheless right: when one tenant can hurt the others. If 3% of one customer's requests take 16× the average, a shared pool spreads that damage across everybody — measured, 326 ms → 2,730 ms for all tenants. Separate pools convert a shared latency regression into a private one. That is a deliberate trade of average latency for isolation, and it is the right call exactly when you have a noisy neighbour and not before. Splitting "for isolation" with no noisy neighbour is a pure loss.

6.5 — Coordinated omission. The harness is closed-loop: each of its 100 threads sends a request, waits, and only then sends another. That means the harness's arrival rate is a function of the system's speed.

Trace it. Suppose the service stalls for one second.

  • Real users keep arriving throughout the stall. At 100 req/s that is 100 more requests entering the queue, the last of which waits nearly a full second plus whatever backlog the first hundred created. All of those slow samples belong in the p99.
  • The harness: all 100 threads are blocked on their in-flight requests. It sends zero new requests during the stall. It records exactly 100 slow samples instead of ~200, and — the bigger error — the queue those extra requests would have built was never created, so its knock-on latency never existed to be measured.

The harness is arithmetically correct about the requests it sent. It is wrong about which requests a real system would have received.

What it must do differently: drive open-loop — send at a fixed arrival rate regardless of whether earlier responses have come back (wrk2, Gatling's constant-rate injection, k6's constant-arrival-rate, JMeter's throughput shaper), letting requests pile up when the system is slow, exactly as users do. Failing that, correct after the fact by back-filling the requests that should have been sent during each stall. And measure at the client, since a server-side timer that starts when the handler picks the request up excludes the accept backlog and the load balancer queue — the two places the waiting actually happened.

6.6 — The throughput alert never fires, and that is the problem.

At 110% of capacity, throughput sits pinned at exactly 100% of capacity — higher than it was at 90% load, and completely flat. The graph looks like the healthiest thing on the dashboard. It will not drop below 5,000 because saturation does not reduce throughput; it caps it.

The p99 alert does fire, and it fires late and then stays useless: past ρ = 1 latency has no steady value to compare against a threshold — it rises linearly with elapsed time, so it will cross 2 s, then 20 s, then 200 s. By the time the 5-minute condition is satisfied the backlog is already thousands deep, and the recovery time is backlog ÷ spare capacity, which is now the metric that matters.

What the runbook should alert on, in order of how early it fires:

  1. Queue depth and its first derivative. A queue that is rising steadily for 60 seconds is proof of ρ ≥ 1 and it leads latency by minutes.
  2. Spare capacity (capacity − arrivals) falling below a floor. This is the quantity that sets both latency and recovery time, and unlike utilisation the threshold is not arbitrary.
  3. Goodput ratio — answers delivered inside the client's deadline, divided by requests received. It is the only metric that distinguishes "busy" from "failing", and in the F6 measurement it ranged from 0.7% to 77% while throughput never moved.

Rung 7 — HANDLE FAILURE

These are design discussions, not single answers. What follows is one good version of each.

7.1 — Three mechanisms.

  1. Bound the queue at roughly servers × (deadline/S − 1), and reject instantly when it is full with a fast 503 and an honest Retry-After. Cost: you now turn people away, and someone must decide that is acceptable. Cannot save you from: a client fleet that retries the 503 immediately — which converts your shed into a storm. The Retry-After must be honest and jittered, or your own header becomes the synchroniser.

  2. Drop work whose deadline has already passed, before starting it. A two-line check at the moment a worker picks a job up: if now − arrivedAt > deadline, discard and count it. Cost: almost nothing, and it is the highest value-per-line change in this whole lab. Cannot save you from: work that expires during service, which needs cancellation propagation.

  3. Switch to newest-first above a depth threshold. Measured: 64 → 6,660 answered in time. Cost: the p99 doubles and the oldest jobs may starve — mitigated by mechanism 2, which rejects them instead of stranding them. Cannot save you from: sustained overload; it improves who gets served, not how many.

None of the three raises capacity. If the overload is permanent, all three buy you a graceful failure and nothing else — you still need more servers or less traffic.

7.2 — A retry policy that cannot storm.

Rules:

  1. Retry only idempotent operations, and only on errors that are evidence the work did not happen (connection refused, fast 503, 429). See the idempotency lab for what makes retrying safe at all.
  2. Never retry a slow timeout. A timeout caused by queueing is direct evidence that the queue is full; adding to it is the storm. A fast failure is safe to retry; a slow one is not. This distinction is the single most important rule here.
  3. Exponential backoff with full jitter: sleep = random(0, base × 2^attempt). The backoff caps the rate; the jitter lowers c²ₐ and prevents the fleet from re-synchronising into a batch. Both halves matter.
  4. A fleet-wide retry budget: each client tracks a rolling ratio of retries to total requests and stops retrying above ~10%. This is the rule that actually prevents the storm, because rules 1–3 bound each request's behaviour while the budget bounds the aggregate, and the aggregate is what the feedback loop is made of. Without it, 5,000 clients each behaving reasonably still sum to a 4× load multiplier.
  5. A circuit breaker as the backstop: on sustained failure, stop sending entirely for a cool-down, then probe with a single request. This sets the retry rate to zero at the exact moment it is most dangerous.
  6. At most one retry for interactive requests. The second retry arrives after the user has usually already given up, so it is pure added load.

7.3 — Two fixes for the slow tenant.

Isolate. Give the noisy tenant its own pool with its own concurrency limit — a bulkhead. The other tenants' c²ₛ collapses back toward 1 and their p99 returns to roughly where it was. Worse off: the noisy tenant, whose own latency gets worse (they now queue behind themselves in a smaller pool), and everyone, slightly, because you have given up pooling — the measured cost of separate lines is ~5× on average wait, which the isolation gain has to exceed.

Reorder. Split the queue by expected size and serve short jobs first, or preempt long ones. Mean waiting time provably falls, and it falls a lot when the distribution is this skewed. Worse off: the long jobs, unboundedly — under sustained load they can starve completely. Fixable with ageing (a job's priority rises with its wait), which gives back some of the improvement.

The judgement call: isolation is the right default when the slow work belongs to an identifiable tenant you can bill or throttle; reordering is right when the slow work is a kind of request (a report, an export) rather than a customer, because then you can move it to an asynchronous path and stop pretending it is interactive at all.

7.4 — The autoscaler, minute by minute.

Capacity C. At 09:00 arrivals jump to 1.3 C.

  • 09:00–09:01. Utilisation hits 1.0 and the queue starts growing at 0.3 C jobs per second. Latency begins climbing linearly, not to a new plateau. CPU reads 100% and stops carrying information — it cannot distinguish 101% from 200% offered load. This is the first reason CPU is the wrong signal.
  • 09:01–09:03. The autoscaler's own averaging window (typically 1–3 minutes) has to fill before it decides. The queue grows the whole time. This lag is added to the provisioning lag, and teams routinely forget it.
  • 09:03. Decision made; instances requested.
  • 09:03–09:06. Provisioning: boot, image pull, health checks, JIT warm-up, connection pools filling. Three more minutes at 0.3 C accumulation. By now the backlog is roughly six minutes × 0.3 C.
  • 09:06. New capacity arrives, say 1.5 C total. The system is stable again — but latency does not return to normal, because the backlog must drain first, at 1.5 C − 1.3 C = 0.2 C per second. Draining six minutes of accumulation at 0.2 C takes about nine minutes.
  • 09:15. Latency finally normal. Total incident: fifteen minutes, of which only six were "waiting for servers".

Scale on queue depth, or on spare capacity, not CPU. Queue depth rises before saturation and keeps carrying information after CPU has pinned at 100%; it is also the quantity that determines how long recovery will take. The second-order lesson: because recovery costs roughly 1.5× the accumulation period, the scaling step must be big enough to leave real drain headroom — scaling to exactly 1.3 C would leave a backlog that never clears.

7.5 — Deadline propagation.

The client sends a deadline, not a timeout: an absolute instant, now + 900 ms, carried in a header and passed to every downstream call.

Each service, on receiving a request:

  1. On accept: if the deadline has already passed, reject immediately without doing any work.
  2. On dequeue — the highest-value rule: if remaining < expected service time, do not start. Reject with "deadline exceeded". This is what stops the 99%-wasted-server-time failure.
  3. On calling downstream: pass remaining − a small reserve as the child's deadline, so the child inherits a budget it cannot overrun, and so the parent keeps enough time to write an error response.
  4. On the way out: if the deadline passed while it was working, still return the answer if it is cheap to do so, but count it as a miss.

What each service can now do that it could not before: decide. Without a deadline a server has no information about whether the work in front of it is still wanted, so it must assume it is — which is precisely the assumption that turns an overload into 99% waste. With a deadline, dropping doomed work becomes a local decision requiring no coordination, and the queue stops being a place where requests go to die quietly.

Rung 8 — DESIGN

Worked design discussions. Yours may differ; what matters is that every number traces back to a measurement or a formula.

8.1 — The payments gateway

First, the thing that no amount of capacity fixes. Some authorisations take 3 seconds at the card network. The merchant's deadline is 2 seconds. Those requests cannot be answered in time by any design — the work itself is longer than the budget. Say this out loud in the design doc, because otherwise the capacity plan gets blamed for it. The job is to (a) fail them fast and cleanly rather than late, and (b) stop them destroying everyone else's latency.

Concurrency. Little's Law gives the floor: 6,000/s × 0.22 s = 1,320 in-flight authorisations. That is the number at which utilisation is exactly 1.0, so it is not the answer. Divide by a target utilisation.

Utilisation target. With this many parallel slots, pooled, the queueing penalty at a given ρ is far gentler than the single-server tables suggest — measured: 32 pooled servers at 80% busy wait 2 ms against 316 ms for one server at 80%. So the target is not set by queueing at all. It is set by two other things: the burstiness (c²ₐ ≈ 3 means merchants arrive in batches, and a batch must fit in the slack) and the autoscaling lag. Target ρ = 0.7, giving 1,320 / 0.7 ≈ 1,900 concurrent slots at peak. Provision for the peak, never the 2,200/s daily mean — that mean is a number about billing, not capacity.

Queue bound. Late is worse than rejected, by the problem statement, so bound tight. The queue drains at c/S = 1,900/0.22 ≈ 8,600 jobs per second, so a queue of Q adds Q/8,600 seconds of wait. Allowing ~300 ms of queue delay gives Q ≈ 2,500. Anything beyond that is a customer you will answer too late, which by the stated cost model is worse than the rejection you could have sent.

Ordering. FIFO normally, because fairness matters when money does. Adaptive newest-first above a queue-depth threshold, paired with deadline dropping — measured, that pairing is the difference between 0.7% and 73% answered in time under overload.

Admission control. In priority order:

  1. Deadline-drop on dequeue — never start an authorisation whose remaining budget is under ~250 ms. Highest value per line of code in the whole design.
  2. Bulkhead the slow tail. If the 3-second responses correlate with a particular card network, issuer, or merchant, give that route its own concurrency limit. This is the one place where you deliberately give up pooling (costing ~5× on average wait for that route) to buy isolation for everyone else — justified here because c²ₛ ≈ 6 is measured to cost roughly 8× on the p99 of all traffic.
  3. Shed at the edge with a fast 503 and a jittered Retry-After.
  4. Concurrency limit, not a rate limit — see 8.3. The card network's latency is the one thing you do not control, and a concurrency limit adapts to it automatically.

Idempotency is mandatory, not optional. A late answer causes a merchant retry, and a retry without an idempotency key causes a double charge. Every authorisation carries a key; the gateway deduplicates on it. (See the idempotency lab.) Without this, the whole design is unsafe under exactly the conditions it exists to handle.

Monitoring. In-flight vs limit; queue depth and its derivative; spare capacity (c/S − λ); the card network's own p50/p99, because a rise in S silently consumes your concurrency budget; shed rate; and wasted server time.

The one number that says the plan is failing: the fraction of authorisations answered inside the merchant's 2-second deadline. Not throughput, which stays flat and healthy right through a total outage. Not latency, which has no steady value once you are saturated. Goodput ratio is the only metric that moves in the right direction for the right reason, and it is the one to put on the wall.

8.2 — The incident paragraph

At 14:00 a campaign raised arrivals about 30%. The service had been running near its limit, so the increase took offered load past capacity. Past that point a queue does not settle at a new higher level — it grows at (arrivals − capacity) jobs per second, and latency grows linearly with elapsed time rather than plateauing. The p99 of 6 s at 14:10 was therefore not a measure of how slow the service was; it was a measure of how long it had been saturated. At 14:12 we scaled from 10 to 15 instances. Latency did not improve for nine minutes and then recovered within ninety seconds. That shape — flat, then a cliff — tells us the new capacity was not actually serving traffic until roughly 14:21: had the extra capacity been live at 14:12 and merely working off a backlog, latency would have declined steadily over those nine minutes rather than holding flat. The nine minutes is instance start-up plus health-check and warm-up time; the final ninety seconds is the backlog draining at the new spare capacity, which is fast precisely because we added 50% capacity rather than the 30% that would merely have restored balance.

What would have caught it at 13:00: an alert on spare capacity (capacity − arrivals) falling below a floor, or equivalently on the latency multiplier 1/(1 − utilisation) crossing a threshold. At 13:00 the service was already close enough to the edge that a 30% increase would saturate it, and a spare-capacity alert says so before any latency moves. A latency alert cannot: latency is nearly flat across the whole safe region and then vertical, so by the time it moves, the decision window has closed. The secondary alert is queue depth rising steadily for 60 seconds, which is direct evidence of ρ ≥ 1 and leads latency by minutes.

8.3 — Rate limit or concurrency limit

Take the concurrency limit, and the argument is Little's Law.

A concurrency limit of N automatically enforces an arrival rate of at most N/S, because N = λS. It is therefore a rate limit that re-derives itself whenever S changes — which is what you want, because S is the thing you do not control.

The scenario where the rate limit fails badly: the downstream dependency slows from 50 ms to 200 ms. The rate limiter happily keeps admitting 8,000 req/s, because req/s has not changed. But the concurrency required has gone from 8,000 × 0.05 = 400 to 8,000 × 0.2 = 1,600. You do not have 1,600 threads, so every one of them is now occupied, the queue in front of them grows without bound, and the service fails — while the rate limiter reports that everything is within policy. The limiter was enforcing the wrong invariant.

The concurrency limit of 400 handles the same event without configuration: at S = 200 ms it simply admits 2,000 req/s instead of 8,000 and sheds the rest. You have automatically degraded to what the system can actually do.

Given the stated 4× daily variation in downstream latency, this is not close. A static rate limit is correct at exactly one point in that range and dangerous above it. Go further and use an adaptive concurrency limit (Netflix's concurrency-limits, Envoy's adaptive concurrency, or a TCP-Vegas-style controller) which infers the right N from observed latency and adjusts continuously.

The one thing the rate limit does better, and why you may end up with both: concurrency limits are not a fairness or billing mechanism. They cannot say "customer X gets 100 req/s". If you need per-tenant quotas, you need a rate limiter too — but as a product control at the edge, with the concurrency limit underneath it as the safety control.
