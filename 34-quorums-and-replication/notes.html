Lab 34 · Copies and Agreement

Quorums and replication

Tell a friend: Ana, Ben and Cara each keep a note of when the trip leaves. Dev changes the time, but he can only reach some of them, and Elle, who asks later, can only reach some of them too. If the group Dev tells and the group Elle asks are each big enough, they are forced to share one person, and that person has the new time. That counting trick is the whole rule. The rest of this lab is the five ways the counting still looks right while the answer is wrong.

The simulator for this lab is visualization.html, also published at https://claude.ai/code/artifact/d1e569b2-2338-4c35-9ad0-81417b750086. Run commands, the folder contents and how the numbers were made are in section 19 at the end.

BEGINNER MODEL

1. Why does keeping copies create a problem that nothing reports?

In one breath: One copy of your data can be destroyed. Several copies can disagree with each other. The second problem is worse, because no error ever tells you it happened.

A machine holding your data will fail. Not "might". A disk dies, a rack loses power, a kernel panics, someone reboots the wrong host. If there is one copy, that is the end of the data.

So you keep several copies on several machines. That solves losing the data. It also lets you serve more readers, and it lets you put a copy near your users. But it creates a new problem that is much harder to see. The copies can disagree, and nothing anywhere raises an error when they do.

What does that look like? A user updates their address, reloads the page, and sees the old one. A balance check returns a number that was true four seconds ago. No exception is thrown. No alert fires. The logs look perfect.

One warning before the story starts, about a word you already know.

⚠️ A word you already know, used for something else. In the paxos lab, quorum meant a majority, and it was used to make a group of machines agree on one value. This lab is about a different thing that shares the name: a read/write quorum. It makes two groups overlap, so that a reader cannot miss a writer. It looks like the same idea. It guarantees dramatically less. Keeping them apart is the single most valuable thing you can take from this lesson. So the lab avoids the word entirely until section 7, where both meanings are put side by side.

2. How many friends must Dev tell, and how many must Elle ask?

In one breath: Three friends each hold a note of the departure time. Dev wants to change it and Elle wants to read it. Neither can reach everyone, and a friend with an old note sounds exactly like a friend with the new one.

Ana, Ben and Cara are organising a trip. The departure time is written down in three places: each of them keeps a note of it. Right now all three notes say 2pm.

Dev needs to move it to 3pm. Elle, later, needs to know what time it is.

Here is the awkward question. The whole lab is the answer to it.

How many of the three does Dev have to tell, and how many does Elle have to ask, for Elle to be certain she gets 3pm?

Notice what makes this hard. Dev cannot always reach all three; Cara's phone is off. Elle cannot always reach all three either. And there is a crueller part. If Elle asks Ben and Ben says "2pm", Ben is not lying, and Elle has no way to tell she has an old answer. A wrong answer here is indistinguishable from a right one.

3. Why not just tell everybody?

In one breath: If Dev tells all three, anyone Elle asks has the new time. That is correct. It also means one friend with a dead phone stops every change.

The obvious rule: Dev tells all three. Then Elle can ask any one of them, and whoever she asks will say 3pm.

This is genuinely correct. It is also what most people sketch first. It is what "just replicate it everywhere" means in practice.

🔮 Predict: Cara's phone is off for an hour. Under "tell everybody", what happens when Dev tries to change the time?

Reveal

Nothing can be changed at all. Dev cannot complete the rule, so the update is refused. One person being unreachable has stopped the entire system from accepting any change.

If you said "Dev tells Ana and Ben now and catches Cara later", you have just invented the second attempt. Section 4 is what happens to it.

And this gets worse, not better, as you add copies for safety. With five copies, the chance that at least one is unreachable at any moment is higher than with three. Under "tell everybody", every copy you add to improve durability makes writes less available. That is the trade this whole lab is about, and it appears at the very first step.

4. Why does "tell one, pass it on" fail in the opposite direction?

In one breath: If Dev tells only Ana, the change always goes through. But Elle may ask Ben, who still says 2pm and has no idea he is wrong. And if Ana loses her note, the change never happened.

So "tell everybody" is too fragile. The obvious repair: Dev tells just one person, and they pass it on when they can.

Now writes always work, because someone is always reachable. But watch Elle. Read the diagram top to bottom; it is the order things happen.

Two separate things went wrong. They are worth naming apart.

Elle got an old answer and could not tell. Ben answered instantly. He correctly reported what he knew. There is no error to catch, no timeout, no warning.

And the change is one accident from never having happened. Only Ana has 3pm. If Ana loses her note before passing it on, the update Dev believed had succeeded simply ceases to exist.

So the two extremes both fail, in opposite directions. Read the table across: each rule is good at one thing and bad at the other.

The interesting design is not at either end. It is somewhere in the middle. The question is exactly where, and why that point and not another.

5. What do we actually need?

In one breath: Elle must always see Dev's latest change. Neither of them may need every friend to be reachable. And when the answer cannot be guaranteed, the system should say so instead of guessing.

We want, in this order:

  1. A later reader must see the last change that was accepted. Not "usually". Always, or the guarantee is worthless, because you cannot tell the good answers from the bad ones.
  2. Neither writing nor reading may require every copy to be reachable. Otherwise one sleeping machine stops the system.
  3. When something genuinely cannot be guaranteed, say so, rather than returning a confident wrong answer.

Requirement 1 and requirement 2 sound like they are in direct conflict. The next section is why they are not.

6. What is the key idea?

In one breath: Dev's group and Elle's group only have to be big enough that they cannot avoid sharing a person. With three friends, two and two is enough, because four different people do not exist.

Dev does not have to tell everybody, and Elle does not have to ask everybody. They only have to be sure of one thing: the group Dev tells and the group Elle asks share at least one person.

Count it. There are 3 friends. If Dev tells 2 of them and Elle asks 2 of them, can those groups be completely different? That would need 2 + 2 = 4 different people, and there are only 3. So no. They must share someone, and that someone has the new time.

That is the entire idea. It is nothing more than the pigeonhole principle: four things cannot go into three boxes without two of them sharing a box.

Now the letters, because the rest of the lab uses them constantly. There are three counts in the story, and each gets a letter. Read the middle column first; the letter is only a shorthand for it.

The thing in the story In words Letter
how many friends keep a note the number of copies N

So the rule, in words first: tell W of them, ask R of them, out of N total. If R plus W is more than N, the two groups cannot miss each other. As a symbol, that is the last row of the table: R + W > N.

Everything else in this lab is either a consequence of that inequality or one of the situations where it stops applying.

7. What are these things called, now that the idea is yours?

In one breath: Every piece of the story has an engineer's name. The names arrive here one at a time, each right after the thing it names has happened to Ana, Ben, Cara, Dev or Elle.

What is one of the copies called?

Ana's note is one copy of the departure time. Engineers call one copy a replica: a machine holding a full copy of a given piece of data.

Notice the phrase "a given piece of data". A replica is per-key, not per-cluster. In a 100-machine cluster, each key typically lives on 3 of them. The word exists to separate "a machine" from "a copy of this particular thing". The same machine is a replica for some keys and irrelevant for others.

In this lab's code, the replicas of key k are called n0, n1 and n2. Read them as Ana, Ben and Cara.

Which machines hold which keys is the placement problem you solved in consistent-hashing. "The next N nodes clockwise" is the replica set.

How many copies are there, and who decides?

Three friends keep a note. That count is the replication factor, written N: the number of replicas per key.

It is chosen once per keyspace, not per request. That makes it different from the other two numbers. N is a property of the data. R and W, which come next, are properties of each individual request.

In this lab, N = 3. Cassandra writes it as replication_factor: 3.

How many must Dev tell, and how many must Elle ask?

How many friends Dev insists on telling is W. How many friends Elle insists on hearing from is R.

Precisely: W is the number of replica acknowledgements the system needs before it reports a write successful. R is the number of replica responses it needs before it answers a read. Both are chosen per request. That is the point. The same data can be read cheaply in one code path and carefully in another.

Why two separate words? Because they buy different things. W buys durability and costs write availability. R buys freshness and costs read availability. Only their sum, compared with N, decides the overlap.

The lab's default is W=2, R=2, N=3. Cassandra spells this QUORUM.

Who does Dev actually phone?

In the real system Dev does not dial Ana, Ben and Cara himself. He rings one machine, whichever one happens to answer. That machine forwards the change to the replicas, counts who says "got it", and reports back to Dev. Engineers call it the coordinator.

In a leaderless system the coordinator is any node, and it is a different one on every request. The word exists to distinguish it from a leader. A leader is elected and has authority. A coordinator is just whoever picked up the phone, and it has none.

Compare the coordinator in two-phase-commit. That one had authority, and its death blocked everyone. This one has none, so its death costs one retry.

Why does "quorum" mean two different things?

This is the important entry. In paxos, a quorum was a majority, used so that a group of machines could agree. Here, a quorum is any R or W with R + W > N, used so that a reader and a writer overlap. Read the table down each column, then compare across.

Consensus quorum (what you learned in paxos) Read/write quorum (this lab)
What it is a majority of the nodes any R or W nodes with R + W > N
What it is for making nodes agree on one value and one order making a reader and a writer overlap
Guarantees one decision, globally ordered, never contradicted your read sees the last successful write's value
Does NOT guarantee — any ordering between concurrent writes, or that two clients agree on what happened
Costs a round of messages per decision, a leader in practice one round, no leader, no agreement at all
Where you meet it etcd, ZooKeeper, Raft, Kafka's controller Cassandra, DynamoDB, Riak, Kafka's acks

The sentence to remember: a consensus quorum decides; a read/write quorum merely intersects. Overlap tells you a reader will see a value. It says nothing about two writers agreeing which of them won. Section 10 shows that gap opening up.

Why do people confuse them? Because R=2, W=2, N=3 and "a majority of 3" are both "2 of 3". At R=3, W=1, N=3 they are obviously not the same thing, and the overlap rule still holds.

What happens when Elle notices Ben is wrong?

Elle asks Ana and Ben. Ana says 3pm, Ben says 2pm. Elle now knows Ben is behind, so she tells him on the spot. Engineers call this read repair: when a read finds replicas holding different versions, the coordinator pushes the winning version back to the ones that were behind.

The name says when the fix happens. During a read, paid for by a client request, and therefore only for data somebody reads. Section 10.5 puts a number on it: after a replica misses 200 keys, reading half of them repairs exactly 100.

Who fixes the notes nobody asks about?

Some trips are never asked about. Ben's note about one of them stays wrong for months. The only cure is for the friends to sit down over coffee and compare all their notes. Engineers call that background job anti-entropy.

Replicas periodically exchange compact summaries (Merkle trees in Cassandra and Riak) and stream across only what differs. Why does it exist as well as read repair? Because read repair cannot fix what nobody reads. And the keys nobody reads are exactly the ones whose staleness you find out about at the worst moment.

What if Dev leaves a message with a neighbour?

Dev cannot reach Ben or Cara, so he leaves the new time with Ben's neighbour and asks her to pass it on. Two engineer's words describe this.

A sloppy quorum counts acknowledgements from nodes outside the key's replica set, so the write can succeed during a partition. Hinted handoff is the neighbour's job: those nodes hold the value as a "hint" and deliver it to the real replica when it returns.

"Sloppy" is a warning label. It is not a quorum. The write set is no longer a subset of the replicas a read will ask. So R + W > N becomes arithmetic about a set the write never touched. Section 10.4 shows the result: 300 of 300 writes accepted during a partition, and only 200 of 300 visible to a strict read afterwards.

How do you tell "newer" from "at the same time"?

Suppose Dev and a second organiser both change the time within the same minute, and neither heard the other. Neither change is "newer". They are simultaneous, and a single timestamp cannot say that.

A version vector keeps one counter per writer. If one vector contains the other, there is a real ordering. If neither contains the other, the writes are concurrent, and both values are kept as siblings for the application to merge. Last-write-wins skips all this and keeps whichever value carries the higher timestamp.

Why do these words exist? Because a single counter or a timestamp cannot express "no ordering exists". Last-write-wins does not detect conflicts. It conceals them by always producing an answer.

Concretely, using the names the lab's simulator gives its two writers: Ana writes with the vector {ana:1} and Ben writes with {ben:1}. Neither contains the other, so both survive. Section 10.2 counts it over 1000 concurrent pairs: last-write-wins silently discarded 1000 writes, and version vectors discarded 0.

The expected-version check in event-sourcing solved the same problem by refusing the second write. Here nobody is in a position to refuse, so the conflict is preserved instead.

What if one friend is in charge?

There is another way to run the trip. Ana takes every change, and Ben and Cara copy from her. Engineers call Ana the leader and the others followers. How far behind Ana they run is the replication lag.

In single-leader replication all writes go to the leader, and followers apply its change stream. Lag is the backlog of not-yet-applied changes, measured in entries, bytes or seconds.

These words separate this whole family from the leaderless one above. Both families answer the same question, "how many copies must have it before I say yes?", with completely different machinery. Section 10.7 shows the shape: when the leader accepts 2 writes for every 1 the follower applies, the backlog after 300 rounds is 300 and growing.

Where does the story lie to you?

Five places, and each one matters.

  1. Friends do not half-hear you. A write that the coordinator reports as failed may still have reached some replicas, and there is no rollback. Section 10.1 puts the figure at 100%.
  2. Two people cannot change the time at the exact same instant, but two clients can. When they do, no "later" exists. The overlap rule is silent about who wins; it only guarantees you will see something.
  3. A friend cannot be replaced by a new friend with an empty notebook, and a replica can. A rebuilt or restored replica answers confidently with nothing, and it can make up a perfectly valid R.
  4. Friends who agree to compare notes actually do it. Replicas only converge if something makes them: a read that touches them, or a scheduled background comparison that somebody remembered to run.
  5. Most importantly: the friends are not voting. No decision is being made and no majority is being formed. This is the difference from paxos, and it is why this design cannot give you the ordering guarantees consensus does.

The boundary is exact. Every row where R + W > N has zero stale reads in 4,000 rounds. Every row where it does not has some. There is no gradient at the boundary. The guarantee is on or off.

"Nearly enough" is a trap, not a compromise. Look at R=2, W=1: 119 stale reads out of 4,000, about 3%. That configuration is fast, highly available, and correct 97% of the time. 🔮 Predict: at N=3, W=2, R=2 a write succeeds. Immediately afterwards, a different replica dies: one that had acknowledged the write. Is the next read still guaranteed to see it?

Reveal

Yes, but only just, and only because of what R does when it cannot be met. Two replicas had it. One is now dead. So one live replica has it and one live replica does not. A read of R=2 must ask both survivors, so it reaches the one that has it.

If you said no, because "one of the two copies is gone", you counted copies instead of counting who Elle must ask. She must ask both survivors, and one of them still has the new time.

Now kill a second replica. Only one is alive, so an R=2 read cannot be answered at all. This is the good outcome. The system refuses rather than answering from the single reachable replica, which might be the one that missed the write. Refusing is how it keeps the promise. Availability is what you gave up in exchange. It is the same cap-theorem trade you have seen before, here with a dial rather than a switch.

10.1 Can a write fail and still happen?

The rule is about successful writes. What about the others?

Dev's change needs all three friends to say "got it", so W is 3. Cara is slow. She writes 3pm on her note, but her "got it" arrives after the coordinator has given up waiting. Dev is told the write failed.

🔮 Predict: the client got an error. Is the value in the database?

Reveal

Yes. java src/Experiments.java partial:

failed writes that are readable afterwards: 500 / 500  (100%)

Every single one. If you said no, because the system said "failed", you are trusting that word to mean "undone". It only means "not enough confirmations arrived".

There is no rollback in this design, and there cannot be. The coordinator does not know which replicas eventually applied the write, and there is no transaction to undo. "Write failed" means "not enough replicas confirmed", never "nothing happened".

The consequences are practical and constant:

  • A client that retries after an error may be retrying something that already succeeded, so every write must be idempotent. It is the same discipline as the idempotent consumer in transactional-outbox.
  • A failed write may become visible later, so "I got an error, therefore the old value is still there" is false.
  • A read-modify-write built on a failed write is building on sand.

10.2 What happens when two people write at the same instant?

The overlap rule guarantees a reader sees the latest successful write. Now have two clients write at the same time, both having read the same earlier state.

In the story, Dev and a second organiser both change the shopping list within the same second, and neither heard the other. The simulator and the experiment name the two writers Ana and Ben (the two buttons from section 9), so those are the names below.

🔮 Predict: R + W > N holds. Ana writes "book", Ben writes "milk", both without seeing the other. What should a read return?

Reveal

The overlap rule has nothing to say here, and that is the point. It guarantees the read set touches the write set. But both writes reached overlapping sets, and neither is "the latest" in any meaningful sense.

If you said "whichever came second", ask: second by whose clock? There is no clock either of them shares, and no ordering exists to discover.

There are only two honest options, and java src/Experiments.java concurrent counts both over 1,000 pairs:

last-write-wins   conflicts surfaced to the app:    0   updates silently discarded: 1000
version vectors   conflicts surfaced to the app: 1000   updates silently discarded:    0

Last-write-wins compares timestamps and keeps the higher one. It requires no application code and always converges. It also destroys one of every pair of concurrent writes, with no record that they existed. For a "last seen at" field that is perfectly fine. For a shopping cart it means a customer's item silently vanishes.

Version vectors keep one counter per writer, so "newer" and "concurrent" are different answers. Concurrent values are returned together as siblings, and the application merges them. A cart merges by union, a counter by addition, a document by whatever its own rules are. Nothing is lost. The price is that the application must be written to expect it.

The version-vector rule is the load-bearing code in Node.write. Read the three cases as three answers to "is this newer than what I already hold?":

switch (v.vv().compare(incoming.vv())) {
    case AFTER, EQUAL -> { next.add(v); dominated = true; }  // we already have this or better
    case BEFORE       -> { }                                 // superseded: drop it
    case CONCURRENT   -> next.add(v);                        // a real conflict: keep both
}

Why is your clock your conflict resolver?

If you chose last-write-wins, the timestamp is not an implementation detail. It is the algorithm. java src/Experiments.java clockskew puts one node's clock 60 seconds fast:

genuinely-newer writes thrown away: 1000 / 1000

In the story: Cara's watch runs a minute fast. Every change that passes through Cara gets a later stamp than it deserves, so it beats a genuinely newer change stamped by Ana. Every race that node enters, it wins, for as long as its clock is ahead. The database reports success throughout. A single host with broken NTP becomes a silent data-destruction machine.

This is why Cassandra lets the client supply the write timestamp when it has a better source of ordering. It is the strongest single argument for version vectors.

🐛 A bug this lab actually hit, and what it teaches

The first version of the overlap experiment reported stale reads for every configuration, including the ones that cannot have them. The cause: it passed an empty version context to every write. So every write was {cli:1}: identical, and therefore EQUAL to the previous one. Node.write correctly treated each new write as one it already had and ignored it.

The count was wrong, but the bug is the real lesson. In a version-vector store, a client that writes without carrying forward the version it read is not writing "the newest value". It is writing something the database cannot distinguish from a stale retry. Read-before-write is not a performance quirk of Dynamo-style stores. It is how a write acquires its place in the order.

10.3 What if a friend forgot everything?

R + W > N assumes something the arithmetic never states: that the N replicas are the same N replicas, and that a replica that once had the value still has it.

In the story: Ben loses his notebook and buys a blank one. Cara does the same. Elle asks Ben and Cara, R=2 is satisfied, and both say "no idea". Ana still has 3pm, but nobody asked her.

🔮 Predict: two of the three replicas have been rebuilt from empty disks. Elle's read, with R=2, reaches exactly those two. Does the read fail, or does it succeed?

Reveal

It succeeds, and it returns nothing. If you said it fails, you are assuming an empty replica refuses to answer. It does not. "I have nothing" is an answer, and R counts answers.

PASS  empty replicas: the read succeeds -- R responses arrived
PASS  empty replicas: and returns NOTHING, though R + W > N

Restore a replica from a two-day-old backup, or rebuild it from an empty disk after a hardware swap, and it answers confidently with old data or none at all. Two such replicas can satisfy an R=2 read between them.

The write is not gone. The third replica still has it. But the read never asked it, because R was already satisfied. The overlap argument counts replicas, not copies of the data. A replica that has been reset is a replica that lies by omission.

In production this is why replacing a node is a streaming operation with its own state machine. Cassandra calls it bootstrapping: a joining replica copies its share of the data in before it is allowed to answer reads. It is also why "just restore the box from backup and put it back in the ring" is a genuinely dangerous instruction.

10.4 What is a sloppy quorum, and why is it not a quorum?

Now the failure that matters most, because it is a feature people enable on purpose without reading the small print.

A partition cuts the coordinator off from two of the three replicas that own the key. In the story, Dev can reach Ana but not Ben or Cara. Under the strict rule, W=2 cannot be met and the write is refused. That is correct, and it is also an outage.

So Dynamo-style systems offer an escape: accept acknowledgements from any reachable node, not just the ones that own the key. Dev leaves the new time with Ben's neighbour. The write succeeds. The extra nodes hold the value as a hint and pass it on later.

🔮 Predict: N=3, R=2, W=2, so R + W > N still holds, and the write was acknowledged by two nodes. Is it guaranteed visible to the next strict read?

Reveal

No, and the arithmetic was never the guarantee. If you said yes because the numbers still add up, that is precisely the trap. R + W > N is a statement about two sets being forced to intersect. A sloppy quorum collects its acknowledgements from outside the set a read will ask, so there is nothing for the read set to intersect with. Elle asks Ben and Cara; the neighbour is not on her list. java src/Experiments.java sloppy:

hinted handoff OFF :  writes accepted 300/300   visible to a strict read afterwards 200/300
hinted handoff ON  :  writes accepted 300/300   visible to a strict read afterwards 300/300

A third of the writes are invisible until the hints are delivered. A sloppy quorum is not a quorum. It is an eventually-consistent write wearing a quorum's clothes. During that window you have a different database than the one you configured.

Hinted handoff repairs it, and it is worth being precise about. The hint sits on a node that does not own the key, and that node may itself die while holding the only recent copy. Hinted handoff is a best-effort mechanism, not a durability one. That is why it is paired with anti-entropy rather than trusted alone.

10.5 Why does nothing converge on its own?

A replica that was down during a write comes back holding stale data. Nothing in the protocol has told it so, and nothing will, unless one of two mechanisms runs.

In the story: Cara was away for two hundred changes. She comes back with two hundred old notes and nobody says a word.

🔮 Predict: Cara missed 200 keys. Elle then reads 100 of them, the popular half. How many of the 200 does Cara end up holding?

Reveal

100, and exactly those 100. If you said 200, you assumed a read repairs the replica. It repairs the key that was read, and nothing else.

java src/Experiments.java repair

straight after it returns, n2 holds 0 of 200 keys
after reading the hot half, n2 holds 100 of 200  (read repair fixed 100)
after a background comparison sweep, n2 holds 200 of 200

Read repair fixes exactly what somebody reads: a hundred of two hundred keys, because a hundred were read. It is free, because it rides on requests you were already serving. It is also proportional to traffic. That means the keys most likely to be stale are the ones nobody looks at.

Anti-entropy is the background comparison that fixes the rest. Real systems do it with Merkle trees so replicas can find their differences without shipping everything. It is the process people forget to schedule until an incident teaches them. In Cassandra it has a name and a deadline: repair must complete within gc_grace_seconds, or deleted data can come back to life.

10.6 What does the family with a leader do differently?

Everything so far has been leaderless: any node coordinates, no node has authority. The other family puts one node in charge and has the rest copy it. In the story, Ana takes every change and Ben and Cara copy from her.

The interesting question is identical. How many copies must have it before I say yes? Here it has a name: the replication mode.

🔮 Predict: in fully synchronous mode ("wait for all live followers"), both followers are unreachable. How many of 100 writes are refused?

Reveal

None. If you said all 100, you read "synchronous" as "safe". It is only as safe as the number of followers it happens to have. java src/Experiments.java leader:

  mode         acked   lost on failover   refused 1 down   refused 2 down   refused 2 down + floor
  ASYNC         100                100                0                0                    100
  SEMI_SYNC     100                  0                0              100                    100
  SYNC          100                  0                0                0                    100

"Wait for all live followers" quietly becomes "wait for nobody" at precisely the moment there is nobody left to wait for. Ana, alone, waits for no one and says "done". The setting chosen for safety degrades into the least safe one exactly when safety matters, and reports success throughout.

The last column is the fix: a floor on how many replicas must be in sync before a write is permitted at all. Kafka spells it min.insync.replicas. This is the reason acks=all on its own is not a durability setting. It is only a durability setting with the floor.

Read the three rows as three products:

  • Async never refuses a write and loses whatever had not been copied when the leader died: 100 acknowledged writes, gone. That column is not a failover bug. It is the bill for saying "done" early.
  • Sync loses nothing and stalls behind the slowest follower. It also carries the degradation trap above.
  • Semi-sync with a floor, meaning wait for one follower and refuse if fewer than two replicas are in sync, is what most people should actually run. It is roughly what W=2, N=3 gives you in the leaderless world.

The equivalence worth carrying: W in a leaderless store and the acknowledgement count in a leader-based one are the same knob. acks=all with min.insync.replicas=2 on a 3-replica Kafka topic is W=2, N=3.

10.7 Why is replication lag a queue, not a number?

The last piece of the leader-based family. Ana takes changes at one rate; Ben copies them at another. java src/Experiments.java lag:

  writes in   applied out   final backlog   user cannot see own change
          1             3               0            0 / 300
          1             1               0            0 / 300
          2             1             300          300 / 300
          5             1            1200          300 / 300
Leader writes : follower applies Backlog after 300 rounds What it means
1 : 3 0 Ben copies faster than Ana writes; lag hovers near zero
1 : 1 0 just keeping up, and nobody notices anything
2 : 1 300, and growing every one of 300 users cannot see their own change
5 : 1 1200, and growing the backlog grows without bound

Lag is not a property you tune. It is the backlog of a queue. While the follower's apply rate is at or above the leader's write rate, lag hovers near zero and nobody notices. The moment it drops below, because of a big batch job, a slow disk, or a schema change holding a lock, the backlog grows without bound. Every read from that follower is arbitrarily far in the past.

The user-visible symptom is the classic bug report: "I saved my profile and it still shows the old name." Nothing is broken. Every one of those reads is a correct read of a correct replica. The fixes are all routing rules, not storage changes:

  • Read-your-own-writes: route a user's reads to the leader for a few seconds after they write, or to a replica known to be caught up.
  • Monotonic reads: pin a user to one replica so they never move backwards in time by hopping to a laggier one.
  • Consistent prefix: make sure causally-related writes are not seen out of order, which matters most in partitioned or multi-leader setups.

Those three names describe the three ways a user notices lag. They are worth memorising, because they are what a design review will ask you about.

10.8 What does none of this give you?

Worth stating plainly, because it is the most common overclaim about quorums.

R + W > N does not give you linearizability. It guarantees a read sees the last successful write's value. It does not give a single global order. It does not make concurrent writes resolvable. It does not stop a failed-but-applied write from appearing later. And it does not stop two concurrent reads from seeing different values while a write is in flight: one may catch it, one may not, and read repair makes which one wins depend on timing.

If you need "everyone agrees on one order", you need consensus: the paxos machinery, or Raft, or a single leader with a commit quorum. That is a different, more expensive tool. Dynamo-style quorums are what you use when you have decided you do not need it.

ENGINEERING MODEL

11. What are the ten ways it fails, and what survives each one?

In one breath: Ten failures, each a short story about Ana, Ben and Cara. Each one says how to cause it, what breaks, what still holds, and what a real system does about it. One table at the end lines them all up.

F1: What if Cara's phone is off when Dev calls?

Dev tells Ana and Ben. Cara is unreachable. At W=2, N=3 the write still succeeds; the dead replica simply misses it. When Cara comes back she holds 2pm, and nobody tells her.

To cause it, run java src/Experiments.java overlap, or in the simulator double-click a replica and then write.

The read guarantee survives, because two replicas have the value and any R=2 read must touch one of them. Nothing is lost yet. But the cluster is now one failure away from losing it.

In production this is the normal state of a large cluster, not an incident. Something is always down. It is why anti-entropy is a scheduled job rather than an emergency procedure.

F2: What if so many friends are away that Dev or Elle cannot reach enough?

Kill two replicas at N=3, R=2, W=2. Only Ana is reachable. Dev needs two and Elle needs two, so both reads and writes are refused.

This is the system working correctly. Refusing is how it keeps its promise, because answering from the single reachable replica might return a value that missed the last write. Correctness survives. Availability is lost, completely, for those keys.

In production this is the cap-theorem choice made per request. It is precisely why Cassandra exposes the consistency level per query. The same cluster serves ONE for a metrics dashboard and QUORUM for a balance, and the dashboard stays up during an outage that stops the balance.

F3: What if a write fails and applies anyway?

Cara stores Dev's change but confirms too late. The coordinator gives up counting before the slow replica replies, and the replica had already stored the value. There is no rollback and no transaction.

To cause it, run java src/Experiments.java partial: 500 of 500 such writes are readable afterwards.

Nothing is corrupted. What is lost is the meaning of the error. "Failed" tells you only that confirmation did not arrive.

In production this is why every write must be idempotent, why retry-on-error is safe only for idempotent writes, and why read-modify-write against a Dynamo-style store needs a compare-and-set rather than a bare retry. Cassandra's lightweight transactions and DynamoDB's conditional writes are that compare-and-set.

F4: What if two people change the plan at the same moment?

Dev and a second organiser both write, neither having seen the other. Run java src/Experiments.java concurrent, or in the simulator press Ana writes, then Ben writes, then Read.

With version vectors, no write is lost and the conflict is surfaced. With last-write-wins, one of every pair is lost, silently: 1000 of 1000 in the experiment.

In production the systems disagree on purpose. Riak returns siblings and expects the application to merge. Cassandra is last-write-wins by design and pushes you toward data types that cannot conflict (its counters and collections, or CRDTs generally). DynamoDB gives you conditional writes so you can refuse instead of merging.

F5: What if Cara's watch is wrong?

Under last-write-wins the timestamp is the resolution rule, so a fast clock wins every race. Run java src/Experiments.java clockskew: 1000 of 1000 newer writes are discarded.

Convergence survives. Every replica agrees on the wrong value. What is lost is the newer write, with no error and no record.

In production, monitor clock skew as a first-class metric. Prefer client-supplied timestamps where the client has a better order. And treat "replicas disagree about time" as the reason last-write-wins is a data-model decision rather than a configuration one.

F6: What if Ben is handed a blank notebook?

A replica is rebuilt or restored from backup. R + W > N counts replicas, not copies of the data, so two reset replicas can satisfy an R=2 read between them and return nothing. The test that causes it is rebuiltEmptyReplicas_canSatisfyAQuorumWithNothing.

The data still exists on the third replica; Ana still has 3pm. What is lost is the read guarantee, for as long as the reset replicas are in the ring.

In production a replacement node must bootstrap: stream its data in before it is allowed to serve reads. Cassandra does this explicitly, and auto_bootstrap: false on a replacement node is a well-known way to cause exactly this incident.

F7: What if the network splits while sloppy quorums are on?

Dev leaves the new time with a neighbour because Ben and Cara are cut off. Run java src/Experiments.java sloppy, or in the simulator turn sloppy on, cut the network, write, heal, and read.

Availability survives: 300 of 300 writes accepted. The read guarantee is lost for 100 of 300 keys, until hints are delivered.

In production this is Dynamo's deliberate AP choice. The operational consequences are a hinted handoff queue you must monitor, and a repair job that must complete afterwards. The queue can grow without bound during a long partition, and it is not durable: the holder may die.

F8: What if Ana, the leader, dies before Ben has copied her?

Run java src/Experiments.java leader. In async mode, 100 acknowledged writes are lost on failover.

Availability survives throughout. What is lost is acknowledged writes, the worst category of loss, because the client was told it succeeded.

In production this is what synchronous_commit in Postgres, semi-synchronous replication in MySQL, and acks in Kafka are all about. The question to ask about any system is not "does it replicate?" but "at what point does it say yes?"

F9: What if "wait for everyone who is here" means waiting for nobody?

Take the SYNC row of leader with two followers down: 0 writes refused. "Wait for all live followers" is vacuous when none are live. Ana is alone and waits for no one.

Availability survives. All redundancy is lost, invisibly. The system reports healthy while running on one copy.

In production the fix is a floor: min.insync.replicas in Kafka, synchronous_standby_names with a count in Postgres. Monitor the size of the in-sync set, not just whether writes succeed. A green write-success graph is compatible with having exactly one copy of your data.

F10: What if Ben falls further and further behind Ana?

Run java src/Experiments.java lag: at 2 writes per 1 applied, the backlog is 300 and growing.

Correctness survives, because every stale read is a correct read of a correct replica. What is lost is the user's model of a single database.

In production, alert on lag in seconds and on its derivative. A backlog that is growing is a different incident from one that is merely large. Route read-your-writes traffic to the leader. And remember that a lagging follower is also a failover risk: promoting it makes its lag into permanent data loss.

12. What exactly is promised, and what is not?

In one breath: Five rules the design never breaks, four things it therefore guarantees, seven things it deliberately does not, and the price paid for each guarantee.

Which five rules does the design never break?

Each rule is written twice: once for Dev, Elle and the three friends, and once the way an engineer would state it.

  1. A write is reported successful only when W replicas that own the key have acknowledged it. Dev is told "done" only when enough of Ana, Ben and Cara said "got it". An acknowledgement from anything else, such as a neighbour, is not a W.
  2. A read is answered only when R replicas that own the key have replied, or it is refused. Elle hears an answer only after enough of the three friends spoke.
  3. A value is never discarded because it looks older unless it is provably older. Under version vectors, BEFORE is a proof, and CONCURRENT is not.
  4. Concurrent values are preserved, not resolved, unless you explicitly chose last-write-wins. Two changes at the same instant are both kept.
  5. A write is applied to every replica the coordinator can reach, regardless of whether W was met. Cara writes it down even if her "got it" arrives too late. This is why "failed" never means "did not happen".

What is guaranteed?

  • If R + W > N, and the replica set is intact, and the quorum is strict, a read returns the value of the most recent successful write (or a value that supersedes it). Elle hears Dev's latest change.
  • Convergence: given repair and enough time without new writes, all replicas end up holding the same thing. The three notes eventually agree.
  • No lost updates under version vectors: every write is either superseded by one that saw it, or preserved as a sibling.
  • Tunability per request: the same data can be read cheaply or carefully.

What is explicitly NOT guaranteed?

  • Linearizability. No single global order. Two concurrent reads can disagree.
  • Any resolution of concurrent writes. The overlap rule is silent; you get a conflict, a guess, or a merge, depending on what you configured.
  • That a failed write did not happen.
  • Freshness under a sloppy quorum, for the duration of the partition plus the handoff.
  • Anything at all if replicas lose data. The argument assumes the replica set is intact.
  • Read-your-own-writes in the leader-based family without extra routing.
  • Bounded staleness without anti-entropy actually running.

What was traded away, and for what?

Read each row as a bargain: the left column is the price, the right column is what it bought.

Given up Bought
Linearizability No coordination on the write path: no leader, no consensus round, no commit point
Automatic conflict resolution Either honesty (siblings) or simplicity (LWW); you pick which
Rollback of failed writes A design with no distributed transaction in it at all
Full availability (at strict R/W) The read guarantee, refused rather than faked
The read guarantee (at sloppy) Availability during a partition
A single fixed consistency level Per-request tuning, so one cluster serves both a dashboard and a balance

The through-line: this design buys enormous availability and operational simplicity by refusing to make any decision. Its entire correctness is one counting argument. Every failure in section 11 is a situation where the thing being counted is not what you thought.

15.3 What does an on-call engineer watch?

Not "is the database up". These:

  • Replication lag in seconds, and its derivative. A backlog that is growing is a different incident from one that is merely large (section 10.7).
  • In-sync replica count / size of the healthy replica set, per partition. This is the metric that catches the silent degradation in F9, and a green write-success graph will not.
  • Hinted handoff queue depth and age. Growing means a node has been away too long. Past the hint window, hints are dropped and you now need repair.
  • Repair completion time against gc_grace_seconds. If repair takes longer than the grace period, deleted data can come back. This is a correctness deadline hiding in an operations schedule.
  • Read/write timeouts and unavailable-exception rates split by consistency level. A rise in QUORUM failures while ONE is fine tells you exactly how many replicas you have lost.
  • Clock skew across nodes, if anything in your stack is last-write-wins (F5).
  • Sibling counts, if you use version vectors. A key whose sibling count grows without bound means the application is reading and writing without merging.

The pager you least want: "writes succeeding, min.insync.replicas not set, in-sync set size = 1". Everything is green and you have one copy. Ana is alone, and she is saying "done" to everyone.

15.4 How do you choose the numbers?

A short decision guide, in the order the questions actually arise:

  1. Do you need a single agreed order (leader election, locking, unique constraints, "exactly one of these may succeed")? Then you do not want this chapter's tool at all. You want consensus: etcd, ZooKeeper, a Raft group, or a single-leader database. See paxos.
  2. Otherwise start at N=3, R=2, W=2 (or acks=all + min.insync=2). It crosses the overlap line at the lowest availability cost, and it is the default for a reason.
  3. Write-heavy and can tolerate stale reads? W=1, R=1 is legitimate for metrics, logs, caches and last-seen timestamps. But somebody must have written down that staleness is accepted, because the 3–20% seen in section 10.0 will otherwise arrive as a bug report.
  4. Need more failure tolerance rather than more consistency? Raise N, not R and W. N=5, R=3, W=3 tolerates two dead replicas with the same guarantee.
  5. Multi-datacentre? Use the local-quorum variant unless you can articulate why a cross-region round trip on every write is acceptable.
  6. Concurrent writes to the same key are expected? Decide the conflict rule as a data-modelling decision: conflict-free types where possible, siblings where the application can merge, last-write-wins only where losing one of two simultaneous writes is genuinely harmless.

15.5 What do you say if this comes up in an interview?

The four sentences that show you actually understand it:

  1. "R + W > N guarantees the read set and the write set intersect, so a read sees the last successful write. It is a pigeonhole argument, not a protocol."
  2. "It is not linearizability. Concurrent writes are unordered, and a failed write is never rolled back, so I need idempotent writes and a conflict rule."
  3. "acks=all without min.insync.replicas degrades silently to acks=1."
  4. "Read repair only fixes what is read; anti-entropy is what bounds staleness, and it has a deadline set by the tombstone grace period."

And the question that most often separates candidates: "you have R+W>N. Name three situations where you can still read stale data." The answers are in section 10: a sloppy quorum, a replica that lost its data, and a write that failed but applied (plus concurrent writes, where "stale" is not even the right word).

Solutions — quorums and replication

Attempt EXERCISES.md first. Rungs 7–8 are worked discussions.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 With one copy, whatever it says is by definition the truth — there is nothing to disagree with. With three copies, a change may reach one and not the others, and then three machines hold three different answers, all of them delivered confidently and quickly. You have traded "the data can be destroyed" for "the data can be wrong in a way nothing detects".

1.2 For N = 5: the read set has R members, the write set has W members, both drawn from the same 5. They can be completely disjoint only if R + W ≤ 5, because that is exactly when R + W distinct machines fit. So R = 3, W = 3 gives 6 > 5 and they must share one. R = 2, W = 3 gives 5, and 5 distinct machines fit inside 5 exactly — the reader can ask precisely the two the writer did not reach.

1.3 A consensus quorum makes the nodes agree on one value and one order, so every later reader sees the same history. A read/write quorum only makes the read set and the write set intersect, so a reader sees the last successful write's value — with no ordering between concurrent writes and no agreement between two readers about what happened.

1.4 "What is min.insync.replicas set to?" Because acks=all means "all replicas currently in sync", and when failures shrink that set to just the leader, acks=all becomes acks=1 with no error and no change in behaviour. The measured evidence is in java src/Experiments.java leader: fully synchronous mode with both followers down refuses zero writes.

1.5 The write was sent to three machines. Two of them replied "got it" and one did not reply in time, so we needed three and only counted two — but the third one may well have stored it anyway, and there is no way to reach back and undo that. So an error means "we could not confirm enough copies", not "nothing happened". That is why re-sending has to be harmless.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 The violet replica is in both the write set and the read set. It claims exactly one thing: this replica acknowledged the last successful write and also answered this read, so the value the read returned is at least as new as that write. It claims nothing about ordering between concurrent writes.

2.2 At the step where the acknowledgement count reaches W. The remaining replicas keep being written to afterwards, in the background. It matters because it means the count W is about confirmation, not about how many copies exist — which is the same gap that makes a failed write still land (failure F3).

2.3 The verdict is right and the arithmetic is irrelevant. R + W > N is a statement about two sets being forced to intersect, and it only means anything while both sets are drawn from the same N replicas. A sloppy quorum takes its acknowledgements from outside that set, so the read set has nothing to intersect with. Measured: 300/300 writes accepted, 200/300 visible afterwards.

2.4 A replica in the first loop but not the second received and stored the write, but did not reply in time to be counted. That is failure F3, and it is why "write failed" never means "write did not happen".

2.5 On CONCURRENT the incoming value is added alongside the existing one, so both survive as siblings. If that line set dominated = true instead, the incoming value would be dropped whenever it could not be proven newer — which means every concurrent write would be silently discarded in favour of whichever arrived first. You would have built last-write-wins with an even worse rule (first-write-wins, per replica, so different replicas could keep different values).

2.6 if (responders.size() >= R) break; enforces R. Remove it and every read waits for every replica, which makes reads strictly fresher and dramatically slower and less available — you would have turned every read into R = N. Experiment 10 measures the cost: 27% of requests slow instead of 2.85%.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 Zero stale reads at exactly the six combinations where R + W > N: (1,3), (2,3), (3,3), (2,2), (3,2), (3,1). Write success at W=3 with 20% independent failure is 0.8³ ≈ 51.2% — and the measured figure is 51.2%.

3.2 500 of 500 — 100%.

3.3 Last-write-wins loses 1000; version vectors lose 0 and surface 1000 conflicts instead.

3.4 200 of 300 with hinted handoff off; 300 of 300 once handoff has run.

3.5 Zero. That is the trap: "wait for all live followers" is vacuous when none are live.

3.6 27.31% (waiting for 3) against 2.85% (waiting for 2) — nearly a tenfold increase in slow requests, from replicas that are individually healthy 90% of the time.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Every configuration reports stale reads, including the ones where that is impossible. The cause is not the quorum logic: with an empty context, every write produces the identical version vector {cli:1}, so compare returns EQUAL, and Node.write treats each new write as one it already has and ignores it. The replicas keep the first value for ever.

The lesson is not about measurement. In a version-vector store, a client that writes without carrying forward the version it read is not writing "the newest value" — it is writing something the store cannot tell apart from a stale retry. Read-before-write is how a write acquires its place in the order. This is neither safety nor liveness in the usual sense: the system did exactly what it was told, and what it was told was meaningless.

4.2 No — and this is worth measuring rather than reasoning about. Running overlap with read repair on and off:

readRepair=false   R1W1 stale=784   R2W1 stale=119
readRepair=true    R1W1 stale=784   R2W1 stale=118

Essentially unchanged. Read repair fixes the replica after the stale value has already been returned to the client. It improves the next read of that key by that path; it cannot un-answer this one. What it has changed is the long-run convergence of the replicas; what it has not changed is the guarantee, because there was never a guarantee to change. Anything that makes staleness less likely without making it impossible is a probability, not a property.

4.3 The stale counts for R + W ≤ N fall, sometimes to zero on a healthy cluster, because gathering from more replicas makes it more likely you catch the fresh one. The guarantee does not get stronger — only the luck. The read still returns after R replies in the general case, and under partition or failure the extra replicas are exactly the ones you cannot reach. This is the most dangerous kind of change: it makes the bug disappear in testing and keeps it in production.

4.4 The refused 2 down column goes to 100 for every mode, including ASYNC. You have made it impossible to write unless all three replicas are in sync — you have chosen W = N, with the availability that implies (measured at 51.2% under 20% independent failure). Safety maximal, liveness minimal.

4.5 With "rotate", the stale read appears only when the rotation happens to point at a replica that missed the write, so re-running gives different results. Pinned, it happens every single time.

That is exactly why staleness bugs are so hard to reproduce: in production the client library spreads requests across replicas, so the same user action is correct on most attempts and wrong on some. The bug report says "it sometimes shows old data", it never reproduces on demand, and the natural response — "cannot reproduce" — is correct and useless. The way to reproduce it is to pin the routing, which is the debugging trick worth remembering.

Rung 5 — Implement

5.1 · LOCAL_QUORUM. Label nodes with a datacentre, and count acknowledgements only from nodes in the coordinator's datacentre against a local W. The test that shows what you gave up is the important one: write with LOCAL_QUORUM in dc1 while dc2 is unreachable, heal, then read with LOCAL_QUORUM in dc2 — and observe a stale read. You have not weakened the guarantee, you have narrowed its scope: it now holds within a datacentre and not across them. That is usually the right trade, and it must be a stated one, because a user who fails over between regions will notice.

5.2 · Bounded staleness. It buys a time (or version) bound where R buys a set bound. R says "I asked enough replicas that one must be current"; bounded staleness says "whatever I got is at most K behind". They are useful together: R protects the guarantee, the bound protects you when the guarantee has been suspended (sloppy quorum, reset replica). The new failure it introduces is availability: a replica that is behind now refuses to answer, so during recovery — precisely when replicas are behind — reads start failing. You have converted a silent-wrongness failure into a loud-unavailability one, which is usually right and is never free.

5.3 · Compare-and-set: the instructive failure.

Both succeed. Measured with the lab's own cluster:

CAS: both clients read the same version=true
     bothWritesReportedOk=true
     valuesNowStored=[A-wins, B-wins]

Ana and Ben both read version {init:1}, both find their expected version matches on the replicas they reach, and both write. Each write is applied. The result is two siblings — which is honest, and is not a compare-and-set: the whole point of CAS is that exactly one attempt succeeds.

Why the quorum cannot fix this. The overlap rule guarantees a reader sees a writer. It does not create a moment at which the two clients' attempts are ordered relative to each other, because no node ever decides anything and no node has authority. Both clients observed a true fact ("the version is {init:1}") and both acted on it, and the fact stopped being true between the observation and the action. That gap cannot be closed by asking more replicas.

What you would have to add: an agreement round — a majority of replicas agreeing on which attempt goes first, before either is applied. That is consensus, and it is exactly the paxos machinery. Cassandra offers it as lightweight transactions (IF NOT EXISTS, IF version = ?), which run a Paxos round per operation and cost roughly an order of magnitude more than a normal write. DynamoDB's conditional writes get it from a single-leader partition instead.

The takeaway is the shape of the whole topic: overlap is cheap and gives you visibility; agreement is expensive and gives you exclusivity. Pay for the second only where you genuinely need "exactly one".

Rung 6 — Debug

bug=no-overlap-check — 4 checks fail

Reads answer from the first replica that replies, ignoring R. Violates invariant 2 (a read is answered only when R owning replicas have replied).

Symptom for a user: intermittently old data — worse right after a replica has been down, better later, never reproducible. Also note it breaks two read repair checks, because a read that touches one replica cannot repair the others: disabling the read quorum silently disables the repair mechanism that was compensating for it.

Monitoring: would not catch it. Latency improves, error rates improve, and every read succeeds. This bug looks like a performance win on every dashboard you have.

bug=lww-always — 2 checks fail

Resolves by timestamp even when the version vectors say CONCURRENT. Violates invariants 3 and 4 (nothing discarded unless provably older; concurrent values preserved).

Symptom: customers lose data occasionally and cannot explain it — an item disappears from a cart, an edit reverts. Support cannot reproduce it because it requires two writes in the same instant.

Monitoring: only if you were already tracking sibling counts, and the bug's effect is that sibling counts go to zero — which reads as "no conflicts, everything is fine". A metric that looks healthiest exactly when the bug is worst.

bug=count-any-ack — 2 checks fail

Counts acknowledgements from nodes that do not own the key and store nothing. Violates invariant 1 (W counts owning replicas only).

Symptom: writes that report success and then are not there. This is the worst of the three for a user, because it violates the one thing they were told.

Monitoring: invisible. Write success rate goes up.

6.4 The rest of the suite did not notice because almost every test runs on a healthy cluster where all owning replicas are reachable, and the buggy branch only fires when fewer than W owners can be reached. A quorum system is correct on a healthy network by accident — every configuration, including the broken ones, looks right when nothing is failing.

The consequence for testing: failures must be an input, not an environment. Node death, partitions, slow replicas and clock skew have to be parameters your tests set on purpose, which is why Cluster in this lab exposes kill, makeSlow, partition and skewClock as first-class knobs and why the tests that catch these bugs are the ones that use them.

6.5 Hardest to find in production, in order:

  1. count-any-ack — silent, and every metric moves in the good direction. You would find it only from a user reporting a lost write, and you would not believe them.
  2. lww-always — silent and rare, and its natural metric (siblings) reads as healthy when it is at its worst.
  3. no-overlap-check — also silent, but it produces stale reads at a high enough rate (784 in 4,000 at R=W=1) that users notice a pattern.

The ranking principle worth keeping: a bug is hard to find in proportion to how much it improves your dashboards.

Rung 7 — Handling new failures (discussion)

7.1 · The acknowledgement that outran the disk. All three replicas acknowledged from memory; the rack lost power; the write is gone. The client was told it succeeded, and W=2 was genuinely met — the acknowledgement simply meant something weaker than the client assumed.

The protocol change is to define what an acknowledgement asserts, and there are three defensible answers, each a real setting:

  • "I received it" — Kafka acks=1 / Postgres synchronous_commit = remote_write.
  • "I wrote it to my log and fsynced" — MongoDB j: true, Postgres on.
  • "I applied it and it is visible" — Postgres remote_apply.

The cost is latency, and it is not small: an fsync is milliseconds, and it is on the critical path of every write. The systems' answer is to make it configurable per write, which is the same per-request tuning idea as R and W. The deeper point is that W counts acknowledgements, and the durability of a write is W multiplied by what one acknowledgement means. Quoting W alone tells you nothing.

Note also the correlation trap: three replicas in one rack fsyncing perfectly still lose everything when the rack does. Replica placement across failure domains is part of the durability answer, not separate from it.

7.2 · The hint holder dies. The client was told the write succeeded. The owning replicas never had it. The hint holder is gone. The write no longer exists anywhere, and nothing will ever recover it — anti-entropy compares owning replicas against each other, and they agree, on the old value.

So: should a sloppy quorum satisfy W on its own? The honest answer is that it should be a separate, explicit mode, not a silent fallback. Riak makes this concrete by exposing pw — the number of acknowledgements that must come from primary (owning) replicas — alongside w. Setting pw ≥ 1 means at least one real owner has the value, which turns this scenario from "gone" into "recoverable by repair". The general rule: let a sloppy quorum extend availability, never let it be the entire basis of a successful write.

7.3 · The grey failure. The distinguishing signal is not request volume or latency — both look healthy. It is version age relative to peers on the same keys. A replica with quiet keys and a replica that has stopped applying writes look identical in isolation and completely different in comparison: for the same key, healthy peers hold a newer version than the suspect does, consistently, across many keys. Anti-entropy already computes exactly this comparison, so the detector is a metric derived from a process you should be running anyway — "fraction of compared keys where this replica was behind", per replica.

Two supporting signals: read repair direction (a replica that is always the target and never the source is not keeping up) and the digest-mismatch rate a coordinator sees per replica.

Once sure: take it out of the read path first (stop routing reads to it), which is cheap and reversible, before doing anything destructive. The risk of a false positive is a capacity cliff — removing a replica from a set of three means the other two now carry the load and a further failure makes reads unavailable. So the automation must be rate-limited and must refuse to eject more than one replica per replica set, because the detector's own failure mode is correlated: whatever fooled it about one node probably fools it about its neighbours.

Rung 8 — Design (discussion)

8.1 · Three datacentres, 80 ms apart. Place the replicas one per datacentre (N=3, one per DC) so no single DC failure loses more than one copy.

The naive choice, R=W=2 across all three, is correct and slow: every write waits for an acknowledgement from a second datacentre, so every write costs ≥80 ms, and every read likewise. For a user-profile store that is a bad trade.

The better shape is N=6, two replicas per datacentre, with LOCAL_QUORUM (local W=2, local R=2 out of the 2… which does not overlap locally, so make it 3 replicas per DC, N=9, local R=W=2). Simpler and more common in practice: N=3 per datacentre (9 replicas total, RF 3 in each of three DCs), reads and writes at LOCAL_QUORUM = 2 of the local 3. Writes are local-latency, and cross-DC replication happens asynchronously.

What you gave up, stated plainly: the guarantee now holds within a datacentre, not between them. A user whose request lands in dc2 immediately after writing in dc1 may see old data. When one DC is isolated: users inside it keep reading and writing normally against a diverging copy; users elsewhere are unaffected and do not see that DC's writes; on healing, conflicts are resolved by whatever rule you chose (and if that rule is last-write-wins, the clock skew between datacentres is now a data-loss mechanism — see F5).

To the product manager: "Writes are fast everywhere because each region confirms locally. The cost is that a change made in Europe can take a second or two to be visible in the US, and if a user is bounced between regions in that window they can briefly see the older value. We can make that impossible, and every write would then take at least 160 milliseconds instead of 5."

8.2 · A payment ledger.

  • (a) Appending an immutable ledger entry — a quorum is fine, and this is the best case for one. The entry has a unique id, it is never updated, so concurrent writes cannot conflict: two appends are two different rows. Use W = quorum for durability. This is the same insight as event-sourcing: append-only data has no conflicts to resolve.
  • (b) Reading a balance for display — a quorum read is fine, and R = 1 is arguably fine too if the UI labels it as of a moment. Cheap, available.
  • (c) Reading a balance to authorise a payment — a quorum read is not enough, and the reason is subtle: R + W > N tells you your read reflects the last successful write, but authorising is a read-modify-write, and between your read and your write another authorisation can complete. You would be building exercise 5.3 and getting its answer. You need either a conditional write on a version (single-partition CAS), or to model the balance as a fold over the append-only ledger with a uniqueness constraint on the deduction.
  • (d) "This idempotency key may be used exactly once" — this needs consensus, unambiguously. It is the definition of exclusivity: exactly one of many concurrent attempts may win. A quorum gives you overlap, and overlap lets both attempts see the same "not used yet" and both proceed. Use a single-leader store with a unique constraint, or a compare-and-set backed by Paxos/Raft.
  • (e) The daily reconciliation job — a quorum read is fine, but read at a consistent point and expect to find genuine disagreement; reconciliation is the process that discovers the consequences of every choice above.

What goes wrong if you get (d) wrong: a retried payment request is processed twice. The customer is charged twice, the ledger has two entries, and — because the duplicate arose from a retry, which is most likely during an incident — it happens to many customers at once. It is also nearly invisible in testing, because it needs two concurrent attempts with the same key, which is exactly the case a healthy network never produces. This is why the idempotency key is the one place in a payment system where paying for consensus is not negotiable.

8.3 · Migrating off last-write-wins. The migration is a cqrs-shaped dual-write with a verification period:

  1. Start writing a conflict-free representation alongside the existing column — an append-only set of operations, or a CRDT-shaped structure the application merges on read. Do not switch reads yet.
  2. Backfill history into the new representation. It will be incomplete by definition: the data already lost is not recoverable, because last-write-wins left no record of what it discarded. Say so explicitly rather than implying the migration is a repair.
  3. Run a shadow comparison: read both, serve the old, log the differences. The difference rate is your evidence, and it also measures how bad the original problem was — probably the first real number anyone has had.
  4. Cut reads over when the difference rate is explained rather than merely small.
  5. Remove the old column only after a full gc_grace_seconds period, so no replica that was down can resurrect it.

For the already-corrupted data: identify it (rows whose write timestamps cluster suspiciously, or where an audit trail exists elsewhere), and treat recovery as a business process, not a migration step.

8.4 · A read-heavy service. 10:1 reads, p99 budget 50 ms, 5% stall rate.

Consistency first: any R + W > N works. Latency decides between them, and the arithmetic is the binomial from experiment 10. With a 5% per-replica stall rate:

  • N=3, R=2, W=2: a read is slow if ≥2 of 3 replicas stall = 3(0.05²)(0.95) + 0.05³ ≈ 0.72%. Comfortably inside a p99 budget.
  • N=3, R=3, W=1: slow if any of 3 stalls = 1 − 0.95³ ≈ 14.3%. Blows the p99 budget outright.
  • N=3, R=1, W=3: reads are fastest possible (0.0125% slow) but writes are slow and fragile — one dead replica stops writes entirely.

Given 10:1 reads, the instinct is to push R down and W up. Do not go past the line: R=1, W=3 satisfies the guarantee but makes writes unavailable whenever any replica is down, which at any real cluster size is most of the time. The answer is N=5, R=2, W=4: reads are slow only if ≥2 of 5 stall (≈2.3%), writes tolerate one dead replica, and the guarantee holds (2+4 > 5). If write availability matters more, N=5, R=3, W=3 is the balanced point.

Which constraint bound the answer: latency, not consistency. Every candidate satisfied R + W > N — the consistency requirement did not distinguish between them at all. That is the usual case in practice, and it is why picking R and W by reciting the inequality and stopping there is only half the job.
