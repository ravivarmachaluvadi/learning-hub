Lab 1 · The Ground Floor

Networking for developers — what happens between one line of code and a reply

Tell a friend: Priya posts a parcel to Arjun in another city. Sometimes it comes back stamped "no such flat". Sometimes the courier says "nobody home". Sometimes it just vanishes and she never hears anything at all. Those three feel the same to Priya, but they have three different causes and three different fixes. When your code sends one request, it passes through six hands exactly like Priya's parcel, and every failure it hits is one of those three. This lab teaches you to read which one from the symptom alone, and to know whether trying again can possibly help.

The trainer for this lab is visualization.html, also published as Why The Request Hung. Six runnable experiments live in NetLab.java. Everything about how the lab was built, and where it sits among your other labs, is in section 19 at the end.

BEGINNER MODEL

1. Why does one line of code hide six systems?

In one breath: You write one line. At least six independent systems take a turn before an answer comes back. Each can fail, and almost all of them fail as the same vague exception, or as nothing at all.

You write this:

HttpResponse<String> res = client.send(request, ofString());

What could be simpler? And yet between httpClient.send(request) and a response, at least six separate systems each do a job. Any one of them can break. Almost all of them break in a way that reaches your code as a generic exception. The worst ones reach your code as nothing: a thread that never comes back.

Why does that matter? Because not understanding it produces a very specific kind of stuck. The app team says "the network is flaky". The network team says "nothing is wrong". Both are right, because neither can see the layer that actually broke. Meanwhile the retry you added "to be safe" is making it worse.

2. What happens to Priya's parcel?

In one breath: Priya knows Arjun's name but not his address. Her parcel passes through hands she never sees. When it fails, she gets one of three experiences, and only one of them tells her anything.

Priya wants to send a parcel to Arjun, who lives in another city.

She knows his name. She does not know his address. So first she has to look it up. The directory she uses might be out of date, or slow, or might simply not answer.

Once she has an address, the parcel goes through several pairs of hands: the local post office, a sorting centre, a truck, a delivery office in Arjun's city, and finally a courier at his door. She sees none of this.

Now the important part. When things go wrong, Priya gets one of three experiences:

  • The parcel comes back stamped "no such address". Annoying, but she learned something. The address is wrong.
  • The courier reports "nobody home". Also useful. The building exists; the person was not in.
  • Nothing happens at all. No parcel, no return, no message. Weeks pass.

That third case is the one that ruins her week. It is also the one that maps most directly onto network debugging. Hold on to this: those three outcomes have completely different causes and completely different right responses. But if Priya only checks "did Arjun reply?", all three look identical.

3. Why not just send it, and retry if nothing comes back?

In one breath: Retrying is the obvious fix for an unreliable thing. It is genuinely right for one class of problem, and quietly wrong for two others.

The obvious approach, and the one most code ships with:

for (int attempt = 0; attempt < 3; attempt++) {
    try { return client.send(request, ofString()); }
    catch (Exception e) { /* try again */ }
}

Send it. If it fails, send it again. Networks are unreliable, and retrying is what you do about unreliable things. This is not stupid. For one whole class of problem it is exactly correct.

So what is wrong with it?

4. How does the retry make it worse?

In one breath: The same catch block handles three different failures. One retry is free, one costs nine seconds for nothing, and one never runs at all because nothing ever throws.

Walk it through with real behaviour, all of it from this lab's experiments.

Case A: the machine is up, but nothing is listening on your port. Think of Priya's parcel coming back stamped "no such flat". The failure arrives fast. Three retries cost you almost nothing, and all three fail identically.

Case B: a firewall is dropping your packets. This is the parcel that vanishes. The failure arrives only when your own patience runs out. With a 3-second timeout, three retries hold a thread for nine seconds and every one fails for the same permanent reason.

Case What happened Time to fail Cost of three retries
A nothing listening on the port under 5 ms almost nothing, same answer thrice
B firewall dropping packets 3,001 ms (timeout 3,000 ms) 9 seconds of a held thread

Same code. Same catch block. Same log line. One retry loop is free; the other holds a thread for nine seconds and achieves nothing.

And case C is worse than both. A server accepts your connection and then never answers. Arjun takes the parcel at the door and never opens it. Your retry loop never even runs, because the first attempt never throws. There is no exception to catch. The thread simply stops, on a connection that is genuinely healthy and open.

🔮 Predict: In case C the code did set a timeout: 2 seconds, on opening the connection. The connection opened in about 1 ms. So why did the request still hang forever instead of giving up at 2 seconds?

Reveal

Because that timeout had already succeeded. The setting is called a connect timeout, and the name is exact. It bounds the handshake and nothing else. The moment the connection is established, it has done its job and is spent. It never applies to anything that happens afterwards.

Everything after the handshake is unbounded unless you bound it separately: sending the request, waiting for the server to think, receiving the response. Most code does not bound those, because "I set a timeout" feels like it covers the call.

If you guessed "the server must have sent a byte", that is a real failure too, and you will meet it in section 10 as the slow drip. It is not this one.

| Setting | What experiment 3 saw | |---|---| | connect timeout only | still waiting when the lab's 4-second watchdog gave up; would wait forever | | plus an 800 ms read timeout, same server | failed in 801 ms |

So the naive fix fails in three distinct ways, and they point at one shared cause. The code cannot tell the difference between "refused", "ignored", and "accepted then abandoned". So it applies one response to three different problems.

5. What do we actually need?

In one breath: For any failed or slow request we need three things: which layer broke, whether retrying can help, and a hard ceiling on how long we will wait.

What we actually need, for any failed or slow request:

  1. Which layer broke. Names, routing, the connection, trust, or the application at the far end.
  2. Whether retrying can possibly help. Retrying a permanent failure is not neutral. It is expensive.
  3. A guaranteed upper bound on how long we wait, even when nothing fails and nothing errors.

The rest of this lesson is those three.

6. What is the key idea?

In one breath: The path is a stack of layers, each hiding the one below. That hiding is what makes one line of code possible. And each layer fails with its own signature, so the failure tells you where it happened.

Why is one line of code enough to reach a server across the world? Because the path is layered. Each layer provides one guarantee to the layer above and hides everything below it. Priya does not know about sorting centres; the sorting centre does not know what is in the box.

That hiding is exactly what makes failures hard to read. But the layers leak in a useful way. How a request fails, meaning how long it took and which exception came back, identifies the layer that broke. Each layer fails with a different signature.

Read the signature, and you skip the guessing.

7. Whose hands does the parcel pass through?

In one breath: Follow Priya's parcel from the directory to Arjun's door. Each pair of hands has an engineer's name, one job, and one way of failing.

Let's walk the parcel through every hand, and name each hand as we get there.

The directory: how does a name become an address?

Priya starts with a name. The directory turns it into an address. Your machine does the same: it asks a configured resolver for the records attached to a hostname, and it remembers the answer for as long as the answer says it may.

Engineers call this name resolution, or DNS. An address for the older kind of network address is an A record; an address for the newer, longer kind is an AAAA record. Both come with a time-to-live (TTL), which is how long your machine may reuse the answer before asking again.

Why does the directory exist as a separate step? To separate who from where. Servers move and multiply; the name stays. Everything about deploys, failover and blue-green rests on that separation.

Notice that this happens entirely before any of the protocol knowledge from your communication-protocols lab applies. No socket exists yet.

The address and the flat number: which machine, and which door?

The directory gives Priya a street address. But Arjun's building has many flats, so she also needs his flat number.

The street address is the IP address. It routes a packet to a network interface, meaning one machine. The flat number is the port. It selects which listening process on that machine receives the packet. One machine runs many services, so you need both: the address gets you to the building, the port gets you to the right room.

The knock: how do two machines agree to talk?

Before Priya hands anything over, the courier knocks: "Are you there?" "Yes." "Good, here you go." Three short exchanges, and only then does the parcel move.

Engineers call this the three-way handshake. Your machine sends a small "are you there?" packet called a SYN. The server replies "yes, and are you?" with a SYN-ACK. Your machine answers "yes" with an ACK. That costs one round trip before any of your data can move.

A round trip (RTT) is one message there and back. You met it in the communication-protocols lab. It is repeated here because this lab makes it the unit of account. Every connection cost below is quoted in round trips, not milliseconds, because round trips are what stay true when you move regions.

Why does the handshake matter so much? Because it is the moment reachability is established, and its failure carries the most diagnostic information of any step in the path. That brings us to the two stamps.

The two stamps: why is a fast failure good news?

Priya's first parcel came back the same afternoon, stamped "no such flat". Her second parcel simply never came back. Which of those helped her more?

The first. It was a reply. Something at the far end read the address and answered. Engineers call that reply a reset (RST), and the failure "refused". The machine received your handshake and actively answered: "I'm here, and nothing is listening on that port." Your code sees a ConnectException, and it arrives in about one round trip.

The second parcel was dropped. A firewall or a wrong route discarded your packets. Nobody answered anything. Your code sees a SocketTimeoutException, and it arrives exactly when your timeout expires, because nothing else was ever going to end the wait.

Why is this the single most useful distinction in the lab? Because a refusal proves the network path works. The problem is the process or the port. Silence proves nothing reached anything, and points at routing or a firewall. The tell is the clock: when elapsed time equals your configured timeout almost exactly, nobody answered you.

The ID check: who is Arjun, really?

The courier reaches the door. Before handing over anything private, the courier checks that the person is actually Arjun.

That is the TLS handshake. On top of the established connection, the two sides agree on an encryption method, and your machine checks the server's certificate against the list of authorities it trusts. Roughly one further round trip with TLS 1.3.

Why does it matter for diagnosis? Because it happens after a successful connection. Its failures are therefore never connectivity problems, however much an SSLHandshakeException looks like one.

The two stopwatches: why is one timeout not enough?

Priya has two separate worries. Will the courier reach the door at all? And once there, will Arjun ever open the parcel? Two waits, two stopwatches.

The connect timeout bounds the handshake only. The read timeout (SO_TIMEOUT on a socket) bounds each individual read, not the whole request. They fail independently, and the gap between them is where services hang.

Here is the sharp edge in the second one. A 30-second read timeout does not mean the request takes at most 30 seconds. A server that sends one byte every 29 seconds resets the stopwatch every time, and keeps your thread alive forever.

The courier on retainer: why keep the connection open?

Priya sends parcels to Arjun every week. Looking up the address, knocking, and checking ID every single time is wasteful. So she keeps the same courier on retainer, and he just goes straight to the door.

That is connection reuse, or keep-alive. The client holds established connections in a pool between requests. A request that finds an idle one skips name resolution and both handshakes. Setup is a per-connection cost, not a per-request one, so reuse converts a fixed cost into an amortised one.

The concierge who forgets you: what is a middlebox?

There is a third party in Priya's story that she never thinks about: the building concierge who logs which couriers are expected. If a courier has not been seen for a while, the concierge quietly crosses him off the list and tells nobody.

Engineers call that a middlebox: a NAT gateway, a load balancer, a firewall. It is neither you nor the server, but it holds state about your connection anyway. It tracks active connections in a table, and evicts entries that have been idle too long, silently. There is a third party, it has an opinion, and it will not tell you.

Priya's parcel. A directory lookup, then a chain of handlers, each of which knows only the next hop, and three distinguishable outcomes: returned to sender, delivered, or vanished.

Where the story lies to you

Three places, and each one matters.

The post always eventually tells you something; the network often does not. A postal service has no equivalent of a DROP rule. This is the single biggest gap. It is why "no news" is the hardest network failure to reason about, rather than the mildest.

A parcel is one object; TCP is a stream with no parcels in it. You already know framing from io-multiplexing. This is why. There is no envelope. In experiment 6, three separate writes of ALPHA, BETA, GAMMA arrive regrouped. One run gave two reads (ALPHABETA, then GAMMA). Another gave a single read of ALPHABETAGAMMA. Run it several times: the grouping changes. Your write boundaries are not preserved, and they are not even deterministic.

Delivery is not the same as being acted upon, and TCP only promises delivery. When TCP acknowledges your bytes, it means the operating system at the far end received them. It does not mean the application read them, or processed them, or survived doing so. This is precisely the gap the idempotency key from your communication-protocols lab exists to cover, and section 12 returns to it.

INTERMEDIATE MODEL

10. What does one request actually look like, phase by phase?

In one breath: One request is three systems in a row before your bytes move. Version 1 pays for all three every time. Version 2 keeps the connection. Version 3 puts a stopwatch on every wait, not just the first.

Mechanism v1: the honest version of one HTTPS request

What does client.send(request) actually perform? Here it is in order, timed against cloudflare.com. Read it as Priya's parcel: directory, knock, ID check, and only then the parcel itself.

sequenceDiagram
    participant A as Your app
    participant R as Resolver
    participant S as Server
    A->>R: what is the address of cloudflare.com?
    R-->>A: 104.16.133.229   (2 ms, cached)
    A->>S: SYN
    S-->>A: SYN-ACK
    A->>S: ACK                (18 ms — one round trip)
    A->>S: TLS ClientHello
    S-->>A: certificate + key exchange
    A->>S: Finished           (57 ms — handshake + cert validation)
    A->>S: GET /  (finally, your actual request)
    S-->>A: 200 OK

Read that as three separate systems, not one call. And note the ordering consequence: your request bytes are the fourth thing to move, not the first.

Two hosts, same machine, same run. Read the last column for what each phase is made of.

Phase cloudflare.com www.google.com What it is
Name lookup 2 ms 18 ms configuration, not distance
TCP handshake 18 ms 31 ms ~1 round trip: distance
TLS handshake 57 ms 456 ms ~1 round trip + certificate work

🔮 Predict: Same table, same machine, same run. But api.github.com and github.com both showed a name lookup of just over 5,000 ms, while their TCP and TLS phases were normal. Distance can't explain it. What can?

Reveal

A timeout. 5,000 ms is not a duration anything takes. It is a duration something waits before giving up. A suspiciously round number is nearly always a configured limit rather than a measurement.

If you guessed "GitHub is slow", notice that the TCP and TLS phases to the same host were normal. Whatever it was, it was over before the connection even started.

This turned out to be a real fault on the machine this lab was written on, and section 11 walks the whole diagnosis. The short version: the router at 192.168.1.1 answers the newer-address question (AAAA) only when the answer is yes. When a hostname has no such address, it stays completely silent instead of replying "none". The resolver waits a full 5 seconds before falling back to the older-address answer (A) it already had.

The application is blameless, the server is blameless, and nothing in the code can see it. Every IPv4-only host costs 5 extra seconds on every cold lookup.

Mechanism v2: reuse the connection

Why is v1 slow? Because it pays name lookup, TCP handshake and TLS handshake for every request. At 16 ms RTT that is roughly 2 round trips of pure setup before any useful byte moves. Priya looks up the address, knocks, and checks ID for every single parcel.

The fix is to stop closing the connection. Keep the courier on retainer. Experiment 4 sends 20 identical requests both ways:

Time Connections opened Per request
New connection each time 682 ms 20 34 ms
One connection reused 38 ms 1 1 ms

17.9× faster for identical work. This is why every HTTP client you have used keeps a pool. It is also why constructing a client inside a request handler is a performance bug rather than a style question. It discards the pool. Every request pays the handshakes again.

🔮 Predict: You now hold connections open in a pool. What new failure have you just created that did not exist in v1?

Reveal

Connections can die while you are not using them, and you will not be told.

The concierge from section 7. A NAT gateway or load balancer between you and the server keeps a table of active connections and evicts entries that have been idle too long. It sends nothing to either end. Your socket still reports isConnected() == true, because that method reports whether this socket was ever connected, not whether the peer is still there. TCP has no dial tone.

If you guessed "the pool fills up", that is a real problem too (failure 9), but it is the opposite one: too many connections, not dead ones.

| Experiment 5, step by step | What happened | |---|---| | request 1 | succeeded | | the connection sat idle | 900 ms, past a 600 ms idle timeout | | the socket still said | isConnected=true isClosed=false | | request 2 | failed with Connection reset |

The cruel part is when you find out: on the next request. So the failure lands on a random unlucky user rather than during the idle period that caused it. That is why these errors correlate with traffic gaps rather than with load, and look maddeningly random.

Mechanism v3: bound every wait, not just the first

v2 is fast and reuses connections. Why is it still not safe? Because it still hangs forever against a server that accepts and goes quiet. The connect timeout was spent at the handshake, exactly as section 4 showed.

The fix is a hierarchy of bounds, outermost first. Read the last column: it is what happens to Priya if she forgets each one.

Bound What it covers If you omit it
Connect timeout The handshake only You inherit the OS default: minutes
Read timeout Each individual read The call can block forever
Request timeout The whole call, end to end Slow-drip responses evade the read timeout
Pool idle-eviction How long a connection may sit unused Middleboxes kill it first, and you find out on a user's request

The third row is the subtle one, and the reason v3 exists rather than stopping at v2. A read timeout of 30 seconds resets on every read. A server that sends one byte every 29 seconds keeps the connection alive indefinitely while never completing the response. Only a bound on the whole operation catches that. In Java that is HttpClient.Builder.connectTimeout() plus HttpRequest.Builder.timeout(), two different settings for exactly this reason.

The rule that ties it together: every timeout must be shorter than the timeout of whatever is calling you. If your caller gives up at 5 seconds and you wait 30, you are holding a thread doing work nobody will ever read.

ENGINEERING MODEL

11. What are the nine ways it fails, and how do you tell them apart?

In one breath: Nine failures, nine signatures. Each one is a short story about Priya's parcel, and each has one tell: the observable that identifies it without guessing.

Failure 1: the directory that answers only half the question

A real fault, diagnosed while writing this lab. It is worth the space because it demonstrates the whole method.

Priya asks the directory for Arjun's address and gets it, but only after waiting exactly five seconds every time. The directory is not slow at finding the answer. It is waiting for something else.

The symptom. curl to api.github.com took 5.39 s total, of which time_namelookup was 5.03 s. Three warm runs in a row: 5.004, 5.005, 5.011. Java agreed. InetAddress.getAllByName("api.github.com") took 5,027 ms, while www.google.com took 12 ms on the same machine, same second.

The diagnosis, in four steps:

  1. A round number is a timeout, not a measurement. 5.00 s is the classic resolver timeout.
  2. Is it the name, or the resolver? Querying 8.8.8.8 directly returned the answer in 0.24 s. So the name is fine. The configured resolver (192.168.1.1, the home router) is the problem.
  3. Which query is hanging? macOS asks for the older address (A) and the newer address (AAAA) together. Against the router: A returned in 0.24 s; AAAA timed out entirely, no response at all.
  4. Falsify it. If the theory were "this router hates github", it should hold only for github. The actual theory was "this router stays silent when a hostname has no IPv6 address instead of replying 'none'." So: test six hostnames, three with IPv6 records and three without.
Hostname Has IPv6? Router's answer
api.github.com no timeout (7.02 s)
github.com no timeout (7.02 s)
api.stripe.com no timeout (7.02 s)
registry.npmjs.org yes answered, 0.03 s
www.google.com yes answered, 0.02 s
cloudflare.com yes answered, 0.02 s

Six for six. The split is perfectly along "does this name have an IPv6 record", not along which company owns it.

What does it mean in practice? On this network, every IPv4-only host costs a flat 5 seconds on every cold lookup. That is git push, npm install, a Stripe API call, and any service of yours that happens to be IPv4-only. Nothing in the application is wrong, and no amount of profiling application code would ever find it.

What survived? Correctness: you eventually get the right address. What was lost? Latency, invisibly, and only on cold lookups, which makes it look intermittent. In production the same shape appears as a misconfigured search domain turning one lookup into five, or a resolver whose secondary is dead. The tell: a suspiciously round number in the DNS phase.

Failure 2: refused

Priya's parcel comes back the same afternoon, stamped "no such flat". The building is there. The road is fine. Nobody lives at that number.

Your code sees ConnectException: Connection refused, in under 5 ms (0–4 ms across runs). The machine is reachable and answered with a reset. Nothing is listening on that port: wrong port, or the process is down.

Should you retry? Only if the process is restarting. It fails fast and cheaply. The tell: fast, and an exception that names refusal. Something answered you, so the network path is fine. This is an application problem wearing a network costume.

Failure 3: dropped

Priya's parcel goes out and nothing ever comes back. No stamp, no courier, no word. She stops waiting only because she decides to.

Your code sees SocketTimeoutException, in 3,001 ms against a 3,000 ms timeout. A firewall or security group is set to DROP, or the address routes nowhere. In the lab, case B connects to 10.255.255.1, which routes nowhere. Nobody answered anything.

Should you retry? Pointless while the rule stands, and each attempt costs a full timeout. The tell: elapsed ≈ your configured timeout. That is the fingerprint of silence.

Failure 4: accepted, then silence

Arjun opens the door, takes the parcel, and then never opens it. Priya's courier stands there forever, because nothing ever went wrong.

With a connect timeout only, your code sees nothing. It is still blocked when the lab's 4-second watchdog gives up, and it would block forever. With an 800 ms read timeout, it sees SocketTimeoutException in 801 ms.

Why? The connection is genuinely healthy. The application at the far end is wedged. Everything below the application worked perfectly. What was lost is your thread, permanently.

In production, this is how a service dies without a single error in its logs. Threads accumulate on healthy sockets, the pool drains, and your error dashboard stays green throughout. The response is a read timeout, and above it a whole-request timeout. The tell: no exception, rising thread count, flat error rate.

Failure 5: trust failure

The courier reaches the door and checks ID. The person at the door is not Arjun, or the ID has expired. The courier refuses to hand anything over.

Your code sees SSLHandshakeException, shortly after a successful connect. The certificate is expired, or the hostname is wrong, or a private authority signed it and the JVM does not trust that authority.

Should you retry? Never. It is perfectly deterministic until a certificate or trust store changes. The tell: the TCP connection succeeded first. Any failure after a successful connect is not a connectivity problem, whatever it looks like.

Failure 6: the idle connection that died

Priya's courier has been sitting in the lobby for a while. The concierge crossed him off the list without saying a word. The next time Priya sends a parcel, it bounces at the lobby door.

Your code sees SocketException: Connection reset on the next request, while isConnected() returned true moments earlier. A middlebox evicted its connection-table entry and told nobody.

Should you retry? Yes, on a new connection, and only if the request is idempotent. You cannot know whether the server processed it. The response is pool idle-eviction set below the shortest idle timeout in the path, plus retry-on-fresh-connection for idempotent requests. The tell: errors correlate with quiet periods rather than with load.

Failure 7: the MTU black hole

Priya's letters always arrive. Her big boxes never do. Somewhere on the route is a doorway too small for a box, and the note that says "too big, split it" is being thrown away along with the box.

Your code sees a hang, or a read timeout, but only for large payloads. Something in the path cannot carry full-size packets and should reply "too big, split it". That reply is itself blocked, so your machine never learns and retransmits forever.

The tell: small requests work, large ones hang, on the same connection. That pattern has essentially one cause. Classic on VPNs and tunnels.

Failure 8: the retry storm

Priya's parcel vanished, so she sends three more. So does everyone else on the street. The sorting centre, already struggling, is now buried under four times the parcels.

Combine failure 3 with the naive retry loop from section 3. Every attempt costs a full timeout. Three retries turn a 3-second failure into 9 seconds of held thread. Under load, retries multiply the offered load against a service that is already struggling.

The response is retry budgets, exponential backoff with jitter, and circuit breakers. You met all of them in your software-architecture-patterns lab under resilience patterns. This is the concrete network-level reason they exist. The tell: load increases after a dependency degrades.

Failure 9: not enough ports

Priya hires a fresh courier for every parcel and sends thousands. The agency runs out of couriers, because each one has to rest for a minute after a job before taking another.

A client opening many short-lived connections exhausts its ephemeral ports. Each closed connection sits in TIME_WAIT for around 60 seconds before its port can be reused. New connections then fail on a machine with no other problem.

The response is connection reuse. Notice that reuse is failure 6's cause and this one's cure: the same mechanism, two opposite risks. The tell: failures scale with connection rate, not with concurrency or payload size. This one is described but not reproduced in the lab.

12. What does TCP actually promise?

In one breath: TCP promises that bytes arrive in order, uncorrupted and not duplicated, between two operating systems. It promises nothing about message boundaries, about the application reading them, or about time.

Why be precise here? Because the gap between what people assume and what is guaranteed is where distributed-systems bugs live. Think of Priya's courier: he promises the parcel reaches the building's post room. He does not promise Arjun opens it.

TCP guarantees, between the two operating systems:

  • Bytes arrive in the order sent.
  • Bytes arrive without corruption (checksummed) and without duplication.
  • Lost segments are retransmitted until acknowledged or the connection fails.
  • Flow control stops a fast sender overwhelming a slow receiver.

TCP does not guarantee:

  • Message boundaries. Experiment 6: three writes arrive as one or two reads, and the split differs between runs of identical code. Framing is your job, always.
  • That the application read your bytes. An acknowledgement comes from the kernel. The process may never have called read(), and may have crashed immediately after the ACK.
  • That the application acted on them. Delivered ≠ processed.
  • Timeliness. Nothing bounds how long delivery takes. That is entirely on you, hence section 10's timeout hierarchy.
  • That an idle connection is alive. Failure 6.
  • Privacy or identity. That is TLS, a separate layer with separate failures.

What is the consequence that matters most? When a request fails after the connection was established, you are in a genuine state of ignorance. The server may have processed it completely, partially, or not at all. There is no protocol-level way to find out. Priya's courier can tell her the parcel reached the post room. He cannot tell her whether Arjun read the letter inside.

This is exactly the gap the idempotency key from your communication-protocols lab fills, and this lab is why it is not optional. A retried non-idempotent request over TCP is a coin flip on double execution. It is also why the transactional-outbox lab's at-least-once delivery is the honest default. Exactly-once is not something a network can offer you.

What was traded away to get those guarantees? Three things. Reliability and ordering cost you latency: a handshake before any data. They cost you head-of-line blocking: one lost segment stalls everything behind it, which is the reason QUIC exists, from your communication-protocols lab. And they cost per-connection state on every box in the path, which is the reason middleboxes forget you.

15. What changes in a real system?

In one breath: A real path adds more concierges, each with its own idle timer and its own opinion. Production is mostly the numbers you chose in advance: timeouts, pool sizes, retry budgets.

What is different about the real chain?

This lab runs a client and a deliberately broken server in one JVM on loopback, plus a handful of real internet measurements.

A real path adds a service mesh sidecar, a cloud load balancer, security groups, a NAT gateway, and often a VPN. Each is a middlebox with its own idle timeout, its own health checks, and its own failure modes. Each is capable of turning a REJECT into a DROP: turning Priya's stamped parcel into a vanished one.

Production is mostly about the numbers you chose in advance: timeouts, pool sizes, retry budgets. Every one of them is a bet about the failure modes above.

What should you configure deliberately?

  1. Four bounds, always: connect, read, whole-request, pool idle-eviction.
  2. Pool idle-eviction below the shortest idle timeout in the path. Find the real number, the ALB's or the NAT gateway's. Do not guess.
  3. Timeout budgets that shrink inward. If your caller waits 5 s, you cannot wait 30.
  4. Retry only what is idempotent, with a budget, backoff, and jitter. Failure 8 is what happens otherwise.
  5. Never construct an HTTP client per request.

What should you watch?

  • Latency percentiles per phase, not just totals. A p99 that is exactly 1,000 ms or 5,000 ms is a timeout, not a slow server.
  • Connection-establishment rate. Rising while request rate is flat means the pool is being thrown away.
  • Errors bucketed by exception type, never lumped as "network error". ConnectException and SocketTimeoutException are opposite problems, and merging them destroys the one signal that separates them.
  • Thread pool utilisation. Failure 4 shows up here and nowhere else.
  • DNS lookup latency. Almost nobody instruments it, and failure 1 is invisible without it.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 The two questions are: which layer failed, and is the request safe to send twice?

A retry helps exactly one class of problem — a transient one. It is actively harmful in two others. Against a permanent failure such as a firewall DROP rule, each attempt costs a full timeout and learns nothing: measured, one attempt took 3,001 ms, so three attempts hold a thread for nine seconds and fail identically. And if the request is not idempotent, a retry can double-execute it, because a failure after the connection was established tells you nothing about whether the server processed it.

1.2 A failure at 0 ms means something answered you. A machine received your packets and actively replied "nothing is listening here". That is good news: the network path works end to end, so the problem is small and local — a wrong port, or a process that is down.

A failure at 3,000 ms means nothing answered you at all, and 3,000 ms is simply how long you were willing to wait. That is worse news, because you have learned almost nothing: the packets may have been dropped by a firewall, or gone to an address that routes nowhere, or reached a machine that is wedged. You have no evidence anything you sent ever arrived anywhere.

1.3

Promises Explicitly does not promise
A successful TCP connection A path exists and a process accepted it That the process will ever reply
A TCP acknowledgement The kernel at the far end received the bytes That the application read them, or processed them, or survived
A 200 response The application processed it and said so Anything about requests that failed — those remain genuinely unknown

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 At the default 16 ms RTT: name lookup 22 ms + TCP 16 ms + TLS ~33 ms ≈ 71 ms before your request body moves at all. 2.2 Name lookup, TCP handshake and TLS handshake all go grey. The connection was already established and still open, so there is nothing to resolve and nothing to negotiate. Only "send request", "server works" and "response" remain.

2.3 The server lights up red, not the firewall. This is the point of the distinction: the packets got all the way there. The machine is up, reachable, and answered — with a refusal, because nothing is listening on that port. A firewall would not have answered at all.

2.4 The TCP handshake row changes, from 2,000 ms to 10,000 ms — and so does the total. Nothing about the fault changed. You are controlling the duration of this failure, not the network. It distinguishes "nobody answered" (elapsed ≈ your timeout, so the timeout is what ended it) from "your timeout is simply too short for this server" (elapsed well under the limit, meaning something was happening). Same exception type, opposite problems — only the elapsed time separates them.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 With no read timeout: no exception at all, and the request never returns. In the lab, experiment 3 bounds it with a 4-second watchdog and reports "still waiting after 4,096 ms"; the real call has no upper bound. With a 3 s read timeout: SocketTimeoutException at ~3,000 ms. One dropdown converts an unbounded hang into a bounded, catchable error.

3.2 Send 1 succeeds — the connection is being created right then, so there has been no idle period for a middlebox to notice. Send 2 fails with Connection reset, because between the two requests the connection sat idle long enough to be evicted, and nobody told your end. This is the defining property of the failure: it is created by request 1 and paid for by request 2.

3.3 Request 1: ~5,100 ms (the 5-second resolver timeout plus normal work). Request 2: ~56 ms. The name lookup is skipped entirely on a reused connection, so the fault simply is not on the path. That is why this class of bug looks intermittent — it only bites cold connections.

3.4 TCP and TLS grow (they are round trips); name lookup, server processing and the request send stay flat (they are not). At 250 ms RTT: TCP 250 ms + TLS ~314 ms ≈ 564 ms of setup against ~40 ms of actual server work. Setup becomes roughly 90% of the request. This is why connection reuse stops being an optimisation at distance and becomes the design.

3.5 About 3,000 ms — because that is the configured timeout, and 10.255.255.1 is a non-routable address that will never answer. You are not predicting the network's behaviour; you are reading your own configuration. That is precisely the tell: when you can predict a failure's duration from your own settings, nobody is answering you. Measured: 3,001 ms.

Rung 4 — Modify (all measured)

4.1 — RTT_MS 16 → 150

16 ms RTT 150 ms RTT
New connection each time 682 ms 6,164 ms
One connection reused 38 ms 312 ms
Ratio 17.9× 19.8×

The ratio barely moves; the absolute numbers move by ~9×. The saving is (n−1) × setup, and setup is proportional to RTT — so the further away the dependency, the more absolute time pooling saves, even though the multiple looks similar. Pooling matters most for cross-region and third-party calls, and least for a sidecar on the same host.

4.2 — Is there a break-even point? No, and the exercise is a trap.

Theory first: fresh = n × (setup + work), reused = setup + n × work, so the difference is exactly (n−1) × setup. At n=1 they should tie, and reuse can never lose.

The naive measurement disagrees:

n fresh reused ratio
1 113 ms 35 ms 3.2×
2 158 ms 36 ms 4.4×
20 791 ms 40 ms 19.8×

A 3.2× win at n=1 is impossible, so the measurement is wrong, not the theory. The cause is JVM warmup: the fresh loop runs first and pays for class loading and JIT compilation of the socket path, which the reused arm then enjoys for free. Any benchmark that runs arm A before arm B is biased toward B.

Re-measured after 50 warmup iterations, with the simulated RTT removed:

n fresh reused
1 0 ms 0 ms
5 2 ms 0 ms
20 6 ms 2 ms

At n=1 the difference is zero, exactly as predicted. Two lessons: there is no break-even (reuse never loses, and its benefit grows linearly in both n and RTT), and the first measurement in a JVM is not a measurement.

4.3 — SILENT closes instead of sleeping

Measured: the request finished in 74 ms instead of hanging, and the read-timeout case "succeeded" in 1 ms — readLine() returned null.

The server is equally broken in both versions: it never answers the question. But closing the connection sends a FIN, which reaches the client as end-of-stream — an event. The client learns immediately that no answer is coming and can fail, retry, or fall back.

Leaving it open sends nothing at all, and a healthy open connection with no traffic on it is indistinguishable from one that is about to reply.

This is the single most useful thing a failing server can do for its callers: fail loudly rather than quietly. It is also why Server.SILENT deliberately sleeps rather than closes — the whole point of experiment 3 is the case where the server denies you even that.

4.4 — Adding flush() after BETA. Five identical runs:

Run Reads Grouping
1 1 ALPHABETAGAMMA
2 1 ALPHABETAGAMMA
3 2 ALPHA | BETAGAMMA
4 2 ALPHABETA | GAMMA
5 1 ALPHABETAGAMMA

Three different groupings from identical code, including one (ALPHA | BETAGAMMA) that the un-flushed version never produced. Adding flush() did not make boundaries predictable — it changed the odds.

The conclusion is stronger than "boundaries are not preserved": they are not even deterministic. Any parser that works because messages happened to arrive whole is a parser that will fail in production, on a different network, under load, at 3 a.m. — and it will be blamed on the network.

4.5 — Server idle 2,000 ms, client gap 900 ms

Measured: request 1 → ok:first; request 2 → ok:second. No failure, because the client came back before the server gave up on it.

The production rule: the side that holds the connection must give up on it sooner than the side in the middle does. Concretely, your pool's idle-eviction must be shorter than the shortest idle timeout anywhere in the path — the load balancer's, the NAT gateway's, the server's. If your pool evicts at 50 s and the ALB evicts at 60 s, you always discard connections before they can be silently killed, and failure 6 becomes impossible. Reverse those numbers and it is guaranteed.

Rung 5 — Implement

5.1 The key insight to demonstrate: a read timeout resets on every read, so a drip server sending one byte per interval keeps it alive forever while never completing. Your deadline must be computed once, before the first read, and each subsequent read's timeout set to deadline − now. Prove it with a DRIP mode that sends one byte every 200 ms: a 1 s read timeout never fires; a 1 s total deadline fires at ~1 s.

5.2 The value of Diagnose.java is the ordering: resolve, then connect, then TLS, reporting the first failure with its layer. Stopping at the first failure is the point — once name resolution fails there is nothing meaningful to say about TCP, and a tool that reports "DNS failed, TCP failed, TLS failed" has buried the one fact that mattered.

5.3 The trap: a pooled connection cannot be validated by asking it. There is no way to check liveness without sending something, and isConnected() answers a different question (experiment 5). So the pool must rely on time — evict on age, not on a health check — plus optionally a cheap retry-once-on-fresh for idempotent requests. A test proving it never hands out a dead connection must therefore test the eviction clock, not a liveness probe.

Rung 6 — Debug

6.1 Measured output:

CHECK 1  unreachable_host_fails_within_budget
         outcome  : SocketTimeoutException after 1,514 ms      -> PASS
CHECK 2  silent_server_fails_within_budget
         outcome  : STILL BLOCKED — no exception, no return after 5,004 ms
         -> FAIL  (watchdog gave up at 5,000 ms; the real call has no upper bound)

  • Which check fails: check 2, and it fails by not failing. There is no exception, no return value, and no log line — only a thread that never comes back. Check 1 passes, which is what makes the bug so durable: the obvious test case is green.

  • The line: socket.connect(new InetSocketAddress(host, port), TIMEOUT_MS); is correct but insufficient. Missing: socket.setSoTimeout(TIMEOUT_MS); before the read.

  • What the 1,500 ms bound: the TCP handshake, and only the handshake. They do not bound writing the request, waiting for the server, or reading the response — which together are the entire rest of the call.

  • In a Spring Boot service: each stuck call parks one request thread forever. With a 200-thread pool and a dependency in this state, ~200 requests are enough to consume every thread. New requests queue, then time out at the load balancer. The service is now down, and it has logged zero errors — because no exception was ever thrown anywhere.

    This is worse than the dependency returning 500s in every operational sense. A 500 is fast, it increments an error counter, it trips a circuit breaker, and it frees the thread. A hang does none of those. Your dashboards stay green while the service dies, and the alert that eventually fires is about your latency, pointing investigation at the wrong service.

6.2 The assertion:

assertTimeoutPreemptively(Duration.ofMillis(2000),
    () -> client.call(silentServer.host(), silentServer.port(), "x"));

assertTimeoutPreemptively matters — a plain assertTimeout runs the code to completion and then complains, which against this bug means never.

The production metric is active thread count / thread-pool utilisation. It is the only signal that moves during this failure. Error rate, latency percentiles of completed requests, and status-code distributions all stay flat, because the affected requests never complete and therefore never appear in any of them.

6.3 Two problems.

Total elapsed. Adding retries multiplies the bound rather than enforcing it. Three attempts at a 1,500 ms read timeout is 4,500 ms of reading plus up to 4,500 ms of connecting — the "no call takes more than 1,500 ms" claim is now violated by a factor of six. A retry policy needs a total budget, not a per-attempt one.

Correctness. setSoTimeout fires on requests the server may have received and processed — the timeout tells you only that no answer arrived in time, not that no work happened. Retrying a non-idempotent request in that state can double-execute it. Per stage 12, TCP cannot distinguish "not delivered" from "delivered, processed, response lost", so a blanket retry is only sound for idempotent operations or ones carrying an idempotency key.

Rung 7 — Handle failure (worked discussions)

7.1 — Make the silent hang impossible. The design move is to invert the default: the wrapper requires a total request budget at construction and refuses to build without one, rather than defaulting to "unbounded" as every underlying client does.

Escape hatch: an explicit, awkwardly-named opt-out (unboundedBecause(String reason)) that forces the caller to write down why — streaming downloads and long-poll endpoints are legitimate. Making it verbose is the point; the failure mode is people not thinking about it, not people being unable to.

What breaks: genuine streaming callers must migrate, and anyone who was accidentally relying on unbounded behaviour will start seeing timeouts. Both are good — the second group had this bug and did not know.

7.2 — Retry from the diagnosis, not the exception.

Diagnosis Retry? Why
Refused (fast ConnectException) Yes, briefly Cheap, and a restart may be in progress
Dropped (timeout at your limit) No Each attempt costs a full timeout and the cause is usually a standing rule
TLS failure Never Deterministic until a certificate changes
Unknown host No The name will not resolve differently
Reset after idle Yes, once, on a fresh connection Almost always a dead pooled connection
Read timeout Only if idempotent The server may have processed it

Bound by total attempts and total time, with exponential backoff plus jitter (unjittered retries synchronise clients into a thundering herd). The caller must guarantee the request is idempotent, or supply an idempotency key — your policy cannot establish that, and must not assume it.

7.3 — Survive the middlebox. Numbers to find, in priority order: the load balancer's idle timeout (60 s here), any NAT gateway's idle timeout, the server's own keep-alive timeout, and your client's current pool idle setting.

Set pool idle-eviction to roughly half the shortest of those — 25–30 s against a 60 s LB. Half rather than just-under, so a connection cannot be checked out at the moment it is about to be reaped.

If you cannot find one of the numbers, do not guess upward. Take the smallest value you can confirm and halve it; the cost of evicting too eagerly is a few extra handshakes, and the cost of evicting too late is user-visible errors. Add retry-once-on-fresh-connection for idempotent requests as a belt-and-braces measure — it converts the residual failures into latency rather than errors.

Rung 8 — Design (worked arguments)

8.1 — The timeout budget. The rule: each hop's timeout must be strictly less than its caller's remaining budget, and every hop must pass its remaining budget downstream.

With a 3 s user budget: gateway 3 s → A 2.5 s → B and C 2 s in parallel → D 1.5 s. The shrinking margin absorbs network time and leaves each layer room to respond rather than being cut off mid-answer.

What happens to D when A gives up: D keeps working. Nothing tells it. It will complete, write to its database, and send a response into a socket nobody is reading. This is the ordinary state of affairs and it is why cancellation is a distributed problem, not a timeout setting — and why the work D did must be safe to have happened, which is the idempotency argument again.

A late response must be discarded, not used. A response arriving after the deadline is unsafe: the caller has already returned an answer or retried, and acting on a stale response can produce a second, conflicting action.

8.2 — Diagnose from a description.

Report Most likely layer First check
(a) works from laptop, not cluster Firewall / security group / egress rules nc -vz host port from inside the cluster — does it refuse fast or hang?
(b) fails 09:00–09:04, twice weekly Something scheduled: DNS TTL expiry, cert rotation, a batch job saturating a link Correlate against the exact window; check whether failures are fast or slow
(c) >1 KB times out MTU black hole — the classic signature Send increasing payload sizes and find the exact cliff
(d) only first request after deploy Cold connection pool plus a slow cold lookup — failure 1's shape Instrument DNS lookup time separately
(e) p50 40 ms, p99 exactly 5,000 ms A timeout, not slowness — the roundness gives it away Find what is configured to 5,000 ms; likely a resolver or a retrying client

The unifying method: fast or slow, and is the number suspiciously round?

8.3 — The case against retries. The strongest honest version:

Retries are a correctness liability sold as a reliability feature. They only help transient failures, and most of what we retry is not transient: a firewall rule, a bad certificate, a wrong port. Against those, each retry costs a full timeout and holds a thread — measured, 3 attempts against a DROP rule is 9 seconds of a thread doing nothing.

Worse, they are unsound by default. TCP cannot tell us whether a failed request was processed, so every retry of a non-idempotent operation is a coin flip on double execution. And under partial degradation they amplify: the dependency slows, everyone retries, offered load multiplies, and a brownout becomes an outage.

What would change my mind: evidence the failures actually are transient — a measurable success rate on second attempts. If retry #2 succeeds 40% of the time, retries are earning their keep. If it succeeds 2% of the time, they are pure cost and the honest fix is a circuit breaker plus a fast, clear error.

That is also the cheapest thing to instrument before arguing either way: count retry outcomes, not just retry attempts.
