Lab 7 · One Machine, Done Right

ACID — the four promises, and the one machine that keeps two of them

Tell a friend: Asha the cashier moves ₹500 from Ravi's page to Meera's page in one big paper ledger, and the fire alarm can go off between her two pen strokes. ACID is the name for four promises a database makes. They exist so that Vikram, the cashier who opens up the next morning, can still put the books right from paper alone. Here is the surprise the whole lab is built on: the four letters do not come from one place. A and D are a single machine, a rough day-book written before the ledger. I is a completely different machine. C is not a machine at all; it is your job. ACID is a contract, not a mechanism, and this lab is the audit of who signs which line.

Watch the alarm go off in visualization.html, or at https://claude.ai/code/artifact/584af82b-f8f6-4e22-8580-5d955149d4cf. Where every number came from, and why this lab is shaped the way it is, is in section 17.

1. Why does this problem exist at all?

In one breath: A machine can stop halfway through changing two numbers. When it comes back, one number changed and the other did not. Nobody gets an error, and the money is simply gone.

Somewhere tonight a server loses power in the middle of doing something. Not during an error. Not during a bug. During perfectly ordinary work, halfway through changing two numbers.

When it comes back up, one of three things is true. Either both numbers changed, or neither did, or one did. The third outcome is the one that ends careers.

Here is what that looks like in a real business.

  • A payment was taken from one account and never added to the other.
  • A seat was released and never re-sold.
  • An order was marked shipped and its stock was never decremented.

Now notice what is missing from that list. Nobody gets an error message. No exception is thrown. Nothing is logged as a failure. The system comes back up and carries on being wrong, quietly, forever, until a human notices months later that the totals don't add up.

The other half of the same problem is the exact opposite. A customer was told their transfer succeeded. The machine died a second later. When it came back, the transfer wasn't there. No error there either. Just a customer holding a receipt for something the system has no memory of.

ACID is the name for the promise that neither of those happens. This lab is about how that promise is actually kept. It is just as much about exactly where it stops being kept.

2. What happens to Ravi's ₹500 when the fire alarm goes off?

In one breath: A cashier moves money by making two marks in a book. If the building is evacuated between the two marks, the money vanishes — and nothing in the book says anything is wrong.

Forget computers for a while. We are in a small branch office, before computers. Everything here will map onto a real database, piece by piece.

Asha is the cashier. The branch keeps one enormous bound book, the ledger, with one page per customer. Ravi's balance is written on page 5. Meera's balance is on page 9.

Read the last row of this table first. It is the single fact we will check after every disaster in this lab.

Page Customer Balance
5 Ravi ₹700
9 Meera ₹300
Total ₹1000

Money is only ever moved between customers in this branch. It is never created and never destroyed. So the total must always be ₹1000.

Ravi comes to the counter and asks to send ₹500 to Meera. For Asha that is two pen strokes in the ledger:

  1. On page 5, cross out ₹700, write ₹200.
  2. On page 9, cross out ₹300, write ₹800.

There is one more thing she does, and it is not a pen stroke at all. At some point she looks up, tells Ravi "done", and hands him a receipt. That moment is a promise. From then on Ravi walks out of the building believing this happened.

Now the constraint that makes this whole subject exist. At any instant, the fire alarm can go off. It is a real evacuation. Everyone leaves immediately — mid-stroke, mid-sentence, mid-thought.

The next morning the branch reopens and a different cashier, Vikram, is at the counter. Vikram was not there. He has no idea what Asha was doing. He cannot ask her. Everything that existed only in Asha's head, or as a note on her desk, is gone. Vikram knows only what is written in books that survived the night.

That is the entire problem of this lab, and it comes in two halves.

Half one — the alarm goes off between stroke 1 and stroke 2. The ledger now says Ravi has ₹200 and Meera has ₹300. Total: ₹500. The ₹500 left Ravi and never arrived. It has ceased to exist, and nobody is aware of it.

Half two — the alarm goes off just after Asha said "done" to Ravi, but before she wrote anything down. Ravi has a receipt. The books have nothing.

🔮 Predict: Suppose we tell Asha "always do stroke 1 and stroke 2 as fast as you can, right next to each other, and only then say done." Does that fix either half of the problem?

Reveal **Neither half, and this is the most important idea in the lab.** Speed changes the *probability* of being interrupted between the two strokes. It does not change the *possibility*. There is no pair of actions so fast that nothing can happen between them. The alarm is not polite; it fires in the gap however narrow you make the gap.

If you guessed "it fixes half two, because she promises only at the very end", look again. She still has to say "done" at some particular moment, and the alarm can fire in the instant right after she says it.

"Rare" is not "impossible". At ten thousand transfers a second, "rare" happens several times a day.

Every correct answer in this lab has the same shape. Not "make the gap small" but "make the gap survivable". Arrange things so that whoever arrives after the interruption can work out what to do.

3. Why can't Asha just be careful?

In one breath: Work straight into the ledger, quickly and carefully, and tell Ravi at the end. It is the design everybody writes first, and it is perfect whenever nothing interrupts it.

The obvious plan is the one nearly everyone writes first. Work directly in the ledger, in place, and be careful:

  • Change page 5.
  • Change page 9.
  • Tell Ravi "done".

Let's give it every advantage. Asha is careful. She is fast. She does the two strokes back to back. She says "done" only after both are on paper.

This is the design equivalent of an application that issues two UPDATE statements and then returns success.

It has one genuinely good property. After both strokes the ledger is right, and it is right permanently, because the ledger is the thing that survives the night. If nothing interrupts her, this design cannot be beaten.

4. What are the two ways "be careful" breaks?

In one breath: The alarm can catch Asha between the two strokes, which loses money silently. It can also catch her mid-stroke, which destroys the old number as well as the new one. The second is worse.

Failure A — the gap. The alarm fires between stroke 1 and stroke 2.

Ledger after the fire:   page 5: Ravi ₹200      page 9: Meera ₹300
                         Total: ₹500            ← ₹500 gone

Vikram opens the branch. He sees ₹200 and ₹300. He has no way to know anything is wrong. ₹200 is a perfectly plausible balance for Ravi, and ₹300 is a perfectly plausible balance for Meera.

The ₹500 that left Ravi and never reached Meera has simply ceased to exist. Nothing in the ledger says "this page was changed as part of something bigger that never finished". The evidence of the half-finished job was in Asha's head, and Asha's head is not in the building.

This is the failure of all-or-nothing. The two strokes were meant to be one indivisible act, and the ledger has no idea they were related.

Failure B — the smudge. This one is easy to miss, and it is worse. The alarm fires while Asha's pen is on page 5. She has crossed out the old number and written "2" of "200". The page now reads ₹2.

The version that actually happens to computers is nastier. The page is left half old and half new, and there is no way to tell by looking at it.

Failure A leaves the ledger plausible but wrong. Failure B leaves a page damaged, and the original value is gone too. Even a very clever Vikram cannot reconstruct it from the ledger alone.

Whatever we invent has to handle both. And note the shape of the second one, because an entire later section exists only because of it: the old value was destroyed by the very act of writing the new one.

🔮 Predict: Asha's own fix attempt. "I'll write the new balances on a scrap of paper on my desk first, and copy them into the ledger only once both are ready." Does that fix Failure A?

Reveal **No. It moves the gap; it does not close it.** There are still two strokes into the ledger, page 5 and then page 9, and the alarm can still fire between them.

If you guessed "yes, because now everything is prepared before anything is committed to the book", you are counting the preparation as the dangerous part. It isn't. The dangerous part is the pair of writes into the book that survives the night.

Worse: if the alarm fires while the scrap is still on the desk, the scrap burns and nothing happened. That is at least safe for Failure A. But if Asha had already said "done" to Ravi, we have just built the second half of section 2's problem instead.

The scrap of paper is a real idea and we will keep it. It is exactly what a database's memory is. On its own, it just relocates the danger.

5. What exactly must Vikram be able to do?

In one breath: Strip away every mechanism and one sentence is left. The books must end up holding every transfer Ravi was promised, and no part of any transfer he wasn't.

Here is the goal in plain words. It must be true at every single instant, so that it holds no matter when the alarm fires.

When Vikram opens the branch, he must be able to bring the books to a state that contains every transfer Ravi was told was done, and no part of any transfer that was not.

Read it twice, because two separate promises are hiding in that one sentence. They will get separate names in a moment.

  • No part of an unfinished job may survive. Not one of its two strokes. It is all of it, or none of it.
  • Every finished job must survive. If a receipt was handed over, the books must eventually agree, and no fire may take it back.

There is a third thing we need, and it is easy to overlook. Vikram must be able to do this himself, from paper alone, without asking anybody. A design that requires Asha's memory is not a design. Asha is not in the building.

Everything from here on is the pursuit of that sentence.

6. What is the one idea that makes the gap survivable?

In one breath: Before you touch the ledger, write down what you are about to do, in a book that survives fires. Then the gap stops being dangerous, because the next person can read your intention and finish it.

Here is the whole invention, in one line:

Before touching the ledger, write your intention in a separate book that is safe — and say "done" to the customer only once your intention is safely written.

Asha gets a second, much smaller book: the day-book. It is never neat. It is append-only: she only ever adds a line at the bottom, and never crosses anything out. Each line says what she is about to do, in enough detail either to finish it or to take it back.

For Ravi's transfer, the day-book gets these four lines.

#10   transfer T1 begins
#11   T1: page 5 (Ravi)   was ₹700   →  will be ₹200
#12   T1: page 9 (Meera)  was ₹300   →  will be ₹800
#13   T1 COMMITTED                                     ← the "done" moment

Look at lines #11 and #12 for a second. Each one records both the old value and the new value. That is not redundancy. It is the two directions of travel.

  • The new value lets Vikram finish a job that was promised but not fully written into the ledger.
  • The old value lets Vikram take back a job that was written into the ledger but never promised.

Line #13 is the hinge of the entire subject. Asha may not say "done" to Ravi until line #13 is safely written. Once it is written, the transfer is real forever, even if not one stroke has touched the ledger yet. The day-book alone contains everything Vikram needs to make the ledger right.

Notice what just happened to the ledger's job. It is no longer the thing that records the truth first. It is a slow, tidy copy of a truth that already lives in the day-book. The ledger can lag by minutes and it does not matter.

🔮 Predict: Asha now has two books. Which one has to be in the fireproof safe before she is allowed to say "done" to Ravi — the day-book, the ledger, or both?

Reveal **Only the day-book.** That is the whole trade, and it is what buys the speed.

If you guessed "both", you have described a design that works and that every real database rejects. Putting the ledger in the safe at every "done" means scattering writes across a huge book before every customer can leave. Section 10.2 gives that design its real name and prices it exactly.

If you guessed "the ledger", ask what Vikram would do with a ledger that is right but has no record of why. He could not tell a finished transfer from a half-finished one.

The day-book is small, it is written strictly in order, and it is appended to at one end. Those three properties are why it is cheap to make safe, and section 10.7 is about the price even so.

7. What do engineers call the things in Asha's branch?

In one breath: You have already felt every idea in this section. Now each one gets its professional name, and the four famous letters turn out to come from three different places.

7.1 What do the four letters promise, and where does each one come from?

Start with the three words that describe the job itself.

A transaction is one job that should count as a single act, even though it is made of several steps. Ravi's transfer is a transaction; the lab calls it T1, and it is made of two pen strokes. Precisely, it is a unit of work with a defined start and a defined end, BEGIN … COMMIT/ROLLBACK, to which the database attaches guarantees.

The word is Latin, trans-agere, "to drive through". The point is that the job is driven through to a single conclusion. You used this word constantly in isolation-levels. Here we finally look at what happens to it when the machine dies.

A commit is the moment the job officially counts. In the branch it is day-book line #13, and only then telling Ravi "done". Precisely, it is the point at which the database's promise attaches to the transaction; after it, the effects are guaranteed to survive. The word is the ordinary English one. Before this point you may still change your mind. After it, you may not.

An abort is changing your mind, and a rollback is the work of undoing what the job already did. In the branch: Ravi says "actually, cancel that" after stroke 1, so Asha must put page 5 back to ₹700, using the "was ₹700" she wrote in line #11. Precisely, it is terminating a transaction so that none of its effects remain. Two words, because abort is the decision and rollback is the labour of carrying it out.

Now the four letters. ACID is not a mechanism. It is a contract — four promises a database makes about transactions. Take them one at a time, because each one is a different moment in Asha's day.

A, atomicity, is about the gap. It is Failure A from section 4, forbidden. Both pen strokes land or neither does, and Vikram must never find one without the other. The day-book is what makes that possible, and Vikram's morning is what carries it out.

D, durability, is about Ravi's receipt. It is half two of section 2, forbidden. Once Asha has said "done", the fire may not take it back. What buys it is not the ledger at all. It is line #13 being safe before she speaks.

I, isolation, is about the second counter. Imagine Asha is not alone. A second cashier is serving Meera at the same moment, touching page 9 while Asha's pen is still hovering over it. Nothing in the day-book has an opinion about that. Isolation is a different machine entirely, and you already took it apart in isolation-levels.

C, consistency, is about the number nobody is checking. Look back at the ₹1000 total in section 2. Who enforces it? Not Asha; she writes what she is told. Not the day-book; it records intentions, it does not judge them. Not Vikram; he restores whatever the books say. If Ravi asks to send ₹5000 he does not have, and nobody has written that rule down anywhere, the branch will process it atomically, durably and in perfect isolation. And be wrong.

There is the surprise. Read the fourth column of this table downwards, and notice that it names three different things, not one.

Letter The promise, in plain words The moment in Asha's branch What actually keeps it Where you learn it

A and D come from one machine. I comes from another. C comes from nobody inside the database at all. That is why this lab is shaped the way it is. It is also why isolation could be a dial in the first place: you can turn the concurrency machine down without touching the crash machine.

🔮 Predict: You turn a database's isolation level all the way down to READ UNCOMMITTED. Which of the four letters have you weakened?

Reveal **Only I.** Atomicity and durability are untouched. The log is still written before the pages, the commit record is still made safe before the "done", and recovery still puts the books right after a crash.

If you guessed "A as well, because now other people can see my half-finished work", you have hit the most common confusion in this subject, and it is worth being precise about. Atomicity is about what survives a crash or an abort. Isolation is about what a concurrent reader may observe. A database can be perfectly atomic and still show a neighbour a state that never legally existed. Section 12.1 states this exactly.

If you guessed "C", you are closer than it looks. A weaker isolation level can certainly let your application write nonsense. But the letter C was never promising to stop that, and section 12.2 is the whole argument.

7.2 Where does the data live, and when is something really "written"?

Three places hold Asha's work, and only one of them survives the night.

A page is the fixed-size chunk a database reads and writes at a time. In the branch it is one numbered page of the ledger, holding Ravi's balance. Precisely, it is the unit of storage input and output, typically 8 KB in PostgreSQL and 16 KB in InnoDB. Rows live inside pages, and a database never reads or writes less than one whole page.

The word exists to name the fact that storage has a granularity. You cannot change one byte on disk; you replace a whole page. You already counted these. In btree-and-lsm you counted cold pages and found 119× write amplification for a B-tree. That was this same page, and this lab explains what else has to happen every time one of them is written.

The buffer pool is the database's working memory, holding copies of the pages it is currently using. In the branch it is Asha's desk, where she keeps the couple of ledger pages she is working on right now. Precisely, it is an in-memory cache of disk pages, finite in size, where all reads and writes actually happen. It buffers between a fast processor and a slow disk. And in the branch, it burns.

A dirty page is a page in memory that has been changed, but whose change has not reached the disk yet. Asha has mentally decided Ravi's balance is ₹200, while page 5 in the bound ledger still says ₹700. "Dirty" means needs cleaning, which here means needs writing out. Be careful with this word. It is the same adjective as in dirty read from the isolation lab, and a completely different concern. A dirty read is about who can see it. A dirty page is about where it is stored. Same adjective, two unrelated axes.

The write-ahead log, or WAL, is the day-book: a separate append-only file where every intended change is recorded before the change is made. Precisely, it is a sequential on-disk log of log records, and the rule is that a log record describing a change must reach durable storage before the modified page does. The name says what it is. The log is written ahead of the data. In the branch it is lines #10 to #13.

You have met it twice before. It was the LSM's write path in btree-and-lsm, where the term "WAL-before-ack" is already in your knowledge map. It was Redis's AOF in redis. This lab is where you finally take it apart.

A log record is one line in the day-book. The usual kinds are begin, update (carrying both the before-image and the after-image), commit and abort. There is one more, and it does not exist yet. Section 10.5 has to invent it, because recovery itself can be interrupted.

An LSN, or log sequence number, is the line number in the day-book. In our listing it is the #11 and the #12. Precisely, it is a monotonically increasing identifier for a position in the log, usually a byte offset, so it doubles as "how far into the log is this".

It exists because it gives every change in the entire database one global order, and a name you can point at from elsewhere. That is the trick that makes recovery repeatable, and section 10.4 is where it earns its keep. PostgreSQL exposes it directly: an LSN like 0/16B3A50 in pg_stat_replication is exactly this number, and "replication lag" is a subtraction between two of them.

fsync is the call that means "actually put it on the physical device, and don't come back until you have". Precisely, it is a system call that forces previously written data for a file out of kernel caches to stable storage. In the branch, Asha does not just write the day-book line on a slip in her out-tray. She posts the slip into a fireproof safe bolted to the floor. The out-tray burns. The safe does not.

Durable means it will still be there tomorrow. Precisely, it means present on storage that survives the failure you are designing against — and that last clause is doing enormous work. Section 12.3 is entirely about it.

7.3 What are the two directions of repair?

Vikram can walk in either of two directions, and the day-book was designed so that he can.

Redo means finishing a job that was promised but never fully written into the ledger. Line #13 exists, but page 9 still says ₹300, so Vikram writes ₹800 into page 9. Precisely, it is re-applying a committed change to a page using the log record's after-image. The word says it: the work is being done again.

Undo means taking back a job that reached the ledger but was never promised. Page 9 says ₹700 because of a transfer that never committed, so Vikram writes ₹800 back. Precisely, it is reverting a change using the log record's before-image.

Recovery is what Vikram does before he opens the counter. He reads the day-book cover to cover, before he serves anyone. Precisely, it is the startup procedure that reads the log and brings the data files to the state defined by section 5's sentence.

A checkpoint is a note saying "everything before this line is already safely in the ledger, don't bother reading further back". In the branch it happens at 4 p.m. Asha copies every pending change into the bound ledger. Then she writes one more line in the day-book: "#500 — checkpoint: ledger is current as of here."

Precisely, a checkpoint is a record marking a point from which recovery can start, plus the flushing work that makes it true. The word is already in your knowledge map twice, from btree-and-lsm and from Redis's RDB snapshots. Same idea, third context.

8. What picture should you carry in your head?

In one breath: Two books and a safe. Or, in engineering terms, two places in memory, two files on disk, and one law about the order they get written in.

8.1 What is the picture a friend would understand?

Carry this around:

A cashier writes every intention in a rough day-book the instant she decides it, and posts it into a fireproof safe. Only then does she tell the customer "done". The neat bound ledger gets updated whenever she has a spare moment. If the building is evacuated, the next cashier reads the day-book and makes the ledger agree with it: finishing anything marked committed, and taking back anything that isn't.

Two things to notice in that diagram, because together they are the whole design.

  • The arrow into the data file is lazy and unordered. It can happen at any time, including for changes that have not committed.
  • The arrow into the WAL is eager and strictly ordered.

One law governs the relationship between them.

The WAL rule. A log record describing a change to a page must be durable before that page's new contents are written to the data file. And a transaction's commit record must be durable before the client is told "committed".

Everything else in this lab is a consequence of that two-sentence rule.

8.4 Where does the story lie to you?

An analogy left unqualified becomes a wrong belief. This one lies in four places, and each lie hides a real piece of engineering.

Paper is never half-written in a way you can't see. If Asha's pen stops mid-digit, you can tell. A disk writing an 8 KB page can be interrupted after 2 KB and leave a page that is half old and half new, and looks completely normal. There is no checksum in the page contents, and nothing obviously wrong. This is a torn page. Section 10.6 exists entirely because of it, and the engineering model must therefore assume that a page write is not atomic. The paper model would never suggest that.

Vikram can read the whole day-book in a minute; a real WAL can be gigabytes. Recovery time is a production number that people get paged about. That gap is exactly why checkpoints exist (§10.4), and why the checkpoint interval is a tuning knob with a real trade-off.

Paper never lies about being in the safe. A disk absolutely does. Many consumer SSDs, and some virtualised or misconfigured storage stacks, acknowledge fsync while the data is still in a volatile on-device write cache. PostgreSQL warns about this in its own reliability chapter. The paper model has no equivalent of "the safe lied", so it cannot teach you to be suspicious. You should be anyway.

Asha is one person, so her work is naturally serial. A real database has many transactions in flight at once, interleaved, sharing pages. That is the isolation problem, and this analogy quietly hides it. It hides it so well that the point is worth saying out loud. Nothing in this lab's mechanism prevents two concurrent transactions from corrupting each other's logic. The log gives you A and D. It does not give you I. That separation is the lab's punchline, and section 12.1 states it precisely.

10. What is the machinery underneath? Built five times, because the first four break

In one breath: Start with the simplest day-book that could work. Four times, a specific disaster breaks it, and the repair is a feature you will recognise from a real database's manual. The fifth version is what PostgreSQL and InnoDB actually do.

We are going to build the recovery machine the way it was actually arrived at. Each version is what a competent engineer would design given what they knew. Each one then meets a specific failure, and the fix for that failure is a feature with a name in the documentation.

Throughout, the branch's state stays concrete. Meera now sends money back, so there are two transfers in flight.

Ravi   = page 5   Meera = page 9   Total must always be ₹1000
T1 = "move ₹500 Ravi → Meera"   T2 = "move ₹100 Meera → Ravi"

10.1 Version 1: what if we log the new values and write the pages later?

The simplest design that respects section 6 is four steps.

  1. BEGIN — append a begin record.
  2. Each change: append an update record holding the after-image, the new value, then change the page in memory only.
  3. COMMIT — append a commit record, fsync the log, then tell the client "committed".
  4. Whenever convenient, later, in the background: write the changed pages out to the data file.

Two rules come with it.

  • Never write a page belonging to an uncommitted transaction to disk. We will name this policy in a moment.
  • Don't force the pages out at commit time. The log record is enough.

Why this works for the obvious crash. Crash right after step 3, with page 5 and page 9 both still only in memory:

Log on disk:   #101 T1 BEGIN
               #102 T1 p5 → ₹200
               #103 T1 p9 → ₹800
               #104 T1 COMMIT
Data file:     page 5: ₹700     page 9: ₹300        ← nothing applied yet

Vikram scans the log, sees T1 has a commit record, and redoes #102 and #103 against the data file. Result: ₹200 and ₹800, total ₹1000, and Ravi's receipt is honoured. The commit ack was given at step 3 and it was legitimate. By then the log was in the safe, and the log was the only thing that mattered.

Note what version 1 needs: redo only. It never needs before-images, because an uncommitted change never reaches disk.

🔮 Predict: The buffer pool is finite. Say it holds 4 pages, and it is full. A new transaction needs a page that isn't in memory, so one page must be evicted to make room. Every page currently in the buffer pool has been modified by a transaction that hasn't committed yet. What does version 1 do?

Reveal **Version 1 is stuck.** Its rule forbids writing an uncommitted page to disk, so it cannot evict any of them, so it cannot free a frame, so it cannot proceed until one of those transactions commits.

If you guessed "it just waits a moment and then continues", that is the optimistic reading, and it hides two consequences that are both fatal in practice.

  • A transaction can never modify more data than fits in the buffer pool. UPDATE orders SET status='archived' over ten million rows would need ten million rows' worth of pages pinned in RAM at the same time.
  • Long transactions strangle everyone else, because their pages are unevictable for their entire lifetime.

The obvious fix is to allow the eviction. And the instant you allow it, the disk can contain changes from a transaction that never commits — which version 1 has no way to take back, because it only ever recorded new values.

10.2 Version 2: what happens when memory runs out?

We now permit the buffer manager to steal: to write out a dirty page even though the transaction that dirtied it hasn't committed. This is not an optional nicety. Without it, a database cannot process more data than it has RAM.

The price is exact. Every update record must also carry the before-image.

#106  T2  page 9   before = ₹800   after = ₹700   prevLSN = #105
        ^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^^  the new half — this is what buys undo

Two named policies now exist, and between them they make the 2×2 that every storage engineer knows.

Steal or no-steal answers: may a dirty page from an uncommitted transaction be written to disk? Steal means yes. The word is literal. The buffer manager "steals" the frame back from the transaction that was using it.

Force or no-force answers: must all of a transaction's dirty pages be written to disk before commit returns? Force means yes.

Find the bottom-right cell. That is the corner every serious database lives in.

force no-force
no-steal need neither undo nor redo need redo only
steal need undo only need undo and redo

Why the other three corners lose:

  • no-steal fails for the reason you just predicted. Transaction size is capped by RAM.
  • force fails on cost. Committing would mean writing every touched page before the client hears "committed". That is scattered random input and output across the data file. A hot page touched by a thousand transactions gets written a thousand times.
  • no-force, by contrast, makes commit one sequential append plus one fsync to a single file. The hot page is written once, eventually, carrying all thousand changes at once.

So essentially every serious database picks steal plus no-force, which is exactly the corner requiring both undo and redo. That is not a coincidence or a complication. It is what you buy with the two cheapest properties in the table.

10.3 Why does the same machinery do an ordinary rollback?

Before moving on, notice something that saves an entire subsystem.

Ravi says "cancel that" and T1 aborts while the database is running. No crash is involved. What does the database do? It walks T1's log records backward and applies their before-images. That is exactly the undo pass, on a healthy system.

This is why each record carries a prevLSN: a pointer to the previous log record of the same transaction. One transaction's records form a backward chain through a log full of everybody else's records.

T2's chain:   #106 ──prevLSN──▶ #105 (BEGIN)
T1's chain:   #104 ──▶ #103 ──▶ #102 ──▶ #101 (BEGIN)

Rollback is: follow the chain, revert each step. One implementation serves both "the user changed their mind" and "the building burned down".

10.4 Version 3: how do we stop reading the whole day-book, and stop doing anything twice?

Two problems remain with version 2. Both of them show up on production dashboards.

Recovery scans from the beginning of time. The log is append-only and never rewritten, so Vikram's morning gets longer every day. A database that takes four hours to start is not available, and availability is the thing you were buying.

The fix is the checkpoint. Periodically the database writes a checkpoint record naming two things: which transactions are currently running, and which pages are currently dirty and from which LSN. Recovery starts there instead of at the beginning. In the branch, it is Asha's 4 p.m. note.

The trade-off is a straight dial, and you will meet it as a config parameter. Frequent checkpoints mean fast recovery and more constant background input and output. Rare checkpoints mean less input and output, and a longer outage after a crash.

Redo may be applied to a page that already has it. In the crash below, page 9 was stolen to disk after T1's change was applied to it. If recovery blindly re-applies #103, that is fine for a "set balance to ₹800" record. But for any record expressed as a change rather than a value, such as "add ₹500", it would double the money. And recovery itself can crash and re-run, so "applied exactly once" is not something we can arrange by being careful.

The fix is to put the LSN on the page. Every data page carries a pageLSN: the LSN of the most recent log record whose change is already reflected in this page. Redo then has a rule.

Apply log record L to page P only if L.LSN > P.pageLSN.

That single comparison makes redo idempotent. Running it once, twice or fifty times produces the same page, so recovery can crash and restart as often as it likes.

You have seen this shape twice already. It is the same idea as the idempotency key in the idempotency lab. It is the same idea again as "expected version" in event-sourcing. All three are a stored marker that lets a repeated operation recognise itself. Third context, same trick.

10.5 What do the three passes look like, worked by hand?

Here is the full recovery, with real numbers. This is the trace to remember.

The log on disk when the machine died:

#100  CHECKPOINT        active transactions: (none)   dirty pages: (none)
#101  T1 BEGIN
#102  T1  page 5  before ₹700  after ₹200   prevLSN #101
#103  T1  page 9  before ₹300  after ₹800   prevLSN #102
#104  T1 COMMIT                             prevLSN #103   ← Ravi was told "done"
#105  T2 BEGIN
#106  T2  page 9  before ₹800  after ₹700   prevLSN #105
      *** CRASH ***

Now the data file at the same instant. This is the messy, realistic part. The buffer manager happened to evict page 9 — steal! — after T2 had touched it, and never got around to page 5.

page 5:  ₹700   pageLSN #100      ← T1's change never reached disk
page 9:  ₹700   pageLSN #106      ← BOTH T1's and T2's changes reached disk
Total on disk: ₹1400              ← wrong in a way that flatters nobody

What correctness demands: T1 committed, so its effects must be present. T2 did not, so its effects must be gone. Target: page 5 = ₹200, page 9 = ₹800, total ₹1000.

PASS 1 — ANALYSIS. What was going on when the lights went out? Scan forward from the last checkpoint, #100, building two tables. Read the middle column: it is who was still running.

Read Transaction table Dirty page table
#101 T1 BEGIN T1: running
#102 T1 p5 p5 first dirtied at #102
#103 T1 p9 p9 first dirtied at #103
#104 T1 COMMIT T1: committed → winner, remove
#105 T2 BEGIN T2: running
#106 T2 p9 (p9 already listed)
end of log T2 still running → loser

Analysis writes nothing and changes nothing. It produces two answers: losers = {T2}, and redo must begin at LSN #102, the earliest point at which any page might have fallen behind.

PASS 2 — REDO. Put the world back exactly as it was at the instant of the crash. Scan forward from #102. For every update record, compare against the page's pageLSN.

Record Page's pageLSN Decision Result
#102 p5 → ₹200 #100 < #102 apply p5 = ₹200, pageLSN #102
#103 p9 → ₹800 #106 ≥ #103 skip — already there p9 unchanged
#106 p9 → ₹700 #106 ≥ #106 skip — already there p9 unchanged

State after redo: page 5 = ₹200, page 9 = ₹700, total ₹900.

Stop and look at that total. It is wrong, and it is supposed to be. Redo did not try to produce a correct database. It re-created the crash instant faithfully, including T2's half-finished damage. This is called repeating history, and it is the design decision that makes ARIES simpler than its predecessors.

Why redo a loser's change just to undo it three lines later? Because if redo skipped losers, the undo pass would face two possible starting states per page. Either the loser's change is there, or it isn't, and undo would have to check every time. By repeating history first, undo always starts from a state identical to the pre-crash state. That is exactly the state ordinary runtime rollback (§10.3) starts from too. One undo implementation, one set of assumptions, no special cases. The extra work is paid once; the removed complexity is paid forever.

PASS 3 — UNDO. Take back everything the losers did. Walk T2's prevLSN chain backward from its last record.

Two new pieces of vocabulary arrive here, and both were invented for one reason: undo must survive being interrupted, because recovery can itself be interrupted.

  • A CLR, or compensation log record, is a log record saying "the undo of record X has been performed". It is written to the log before the revert touches the page, exactly like any other change. The WAL rule has no exceptions. Its defining rule: a CLR is never itself undone.
  • undoNext is a pointer inside a CLR, naming the next record of that transaction still needing undo. It is what lets a restarted rollback resume in the middle instead of beginning again.

With those, the pass runs in two steps.

Step Action Log written Result
at #106 revert p9 to its before-image ₹800 #107 CLR: undoes #106, p9 = ₹800, undoNext = #105 p9 = ₹800, pageLSN #107
follow undoNext → #105 that's T2's BEGIN — nothing to revert #108 T2 END (abort complete)

Final state: page 5 = ₹200, page 9 = ₹800, total ₹1000. T1 present, T2 absent. Section 5's sentence is satisfied.

🔮 Predict: The machine crashes again, right in the middle of Pass 3 — after page 9 was reverted to ₹800, but before recovery finished. We restart and run the whole thing again from the checkpoint. Does it come out right?

Reveal **Yes, but only because of that CLR, and the reason is not the one most people guess.**

The common guess is "without a CLR we'd undo twice and double-undo the money". For this record that is not true. The undo writes the literal value ₹800, a physical before-image, so doing it twice is harmless. Being precise about this matters, so here is what CLRs actually buy.

  1. Undo isn't always idempotent. Real databases log much of their undo logically or physiologically. It reads like "remove key K from this index page", or "give back 100". They do that because a physical before-image of an 8 KB page per row change is ruinously large. Re-running "give back 100" twice gives back ₹200. The CLR records that this particular undo has already happened.
  2. Rollback must terminate. Without a record of progress, a crash during undo restarts the rollback from scratch. A transaction that modified ten million rows, crashing 90% of the way through its rollback, would start over — and could, in a crash loop, never finish. With CLRs, each restart resumes from undoNext, and the remaining work only shrinks.
  3. A CLR is never undone. It is redo-only, by rule. That closes the door on the pathological case of undoing an undo.

So, on restart: analysis reads #107 and sees T2's undo has progressed to undoNext = #105. Redo re-applies the CLR if page 9 hasn't got it. Undo then resumes at #105, which is just the BEGIN. Done, correctly, from any number of nested crashes.

10.6 Version 4: what if a page is half old and half new?

Redo assumes something we have not justified: that a page on disk is a coherent page, just possibly an old one. Take that away and redo breaks.

A page write is one write() of 8 KB or 16 KB. The storage device underneath works in sectors — historically 512 bytes, commonly 4 KB. A power loss during the write can leave some sectors updated and others not. The result is a torn page: internally inconsistent, and nothing about it looks wrong from the outside. This is Failure B, the smudge, in its computer form.

Why can't redo fix it? Because most redo records are physiological — "on page 9, set the balance field of the row at slot 3 to ₹800". That instruction is only meaningful if the page's slot directory, row offsets and headers are coherent. If half the page is from before a row was moved and half from after, redo writes ₹800 into a location that is now garbage. Worse, the pageLSN itself may come from either half, so recovery cannot even tell which records to apply.

🔮 Predict: The log already holds a before value and an after value for every change. So why can't recovery just put back the before-image and replay?

Reveal **Because the before-image is not a whole page either.** It is a field inside a page, and writing a field back requires the page's structure to be readable. A torn page has no trustworthy structure.

If you guessed "it can, that's the point of before-images", you have found exactly the assumption every version so far has quietly made: that the page around the field is intact. The two production answers below both work by stopping making that assumption — one of them stores a whole page image in the log, the other stores a whole second copy on disk.

Two production answers, and you should recognise both.

Full-page writes are PostgreSQL's answer, controlled by full_page_writes = on, which is the default. The first time a page is modified after each checkpoint, the entire page image goes into the WAL, not just the change. If that page is later found torn, recovery replaces it wholesale from the log copy, then replays subsequent changes onto a known-good page. The cost is that WAL volume spikes right after every checkpoint, which is why checkpoint tuning shows up in complaints about WAL size.

The doublewrite buffer is MySQL and InnoDB's answer, controlled by innodb_doublewrite = ON, again the default. Every page is written twice: first sequentially into a scratch area, then to its real home. If the home copy is torn, InnoDB restores it from the scratch copy. The cost is roughly double the page-write traffic — sequential, so cheaper than it sounds.

Same problem, two different bills. Neither is free, and turning either off is a "my data is expendable" switch, not a performance tip.

10.7 Version 5: what does the fsync in the commit path really cost?

One thing has been glossed over. Section 10.1 said "append a commit record, fsync the log, then tell the client committed". That fsync is the single most expensive thing in the commit path, and it is unavoidable. It is the exact moment the promise becomes real. Everything else in a commit can be lazy; this cannot.

So the commit path costs at minimum one durable write to one sequential file. That is the best possible shape for a disk: no seeking, one file. The whole no-force design exists to make that sentence true.

But a naive implementation does one fsync per transaction, and now throughput is capped by the device's sync rate no matter how many processors you own. The fix is a batching trick that appears in every mature engine.

Group commit works like this. When several transactions want to commit at nearly the same time, their commit records are already adjacent in the same log file. One fsync makes all of them durable. So the engine waits a moment, collects the commit records that arrive in that window, syncs once, and acknowledges everyone.

The trade is explicit. Each transaction pays a little extra latency; the system gains a large multiple of throughput. PostgreSQL exposes the waiting window as commit_delay and commit_siblings. MySQL has binary-log group commit, tuned with binlog_group_commit_sync_delay.

This is the identical shape as batching you have seen elsewhere: one expensive synchronous step amortised over many callers, paid for in latency. Your work-pressure-and-deadlines lab's queueing arithmetic applies to it directly.

11. What are the eleven ways it goes wrong, and what survives each?

In one breath: Eleven disasters. The machine handles most of them completely and uneventfully. Three of them it does not handle at all, and those three are the ones worth memorising.

Each of these has a scenario on the visualization page. Read one, go and run it, come back. The eleven keep their numbers, F1 to F11, because the exercises refer to them.

F1 — Crash before the commit record is durable. Start the transfer, let both update records be written, then hit CRASH before pressing COMMIT. Leaving fsync-on-commit still pending amounts to the same thing. Analysis finds T1 with no commit record, marks it a loser, and undo reverts whatever reached disk. Balances go back to ₹700 and ₹300. This is correct because the promise was never made: section 5's sentence only obliges us to preserve transfers the client was told about. In production this is the overwhelmingly common crash case, and it is completely uneventful — which is the point. From the client's side it is indistinguishable from a connection error, and that ambiguity is F9.

F2 — Crash after the commit record is durable, before the pages are written. Commit, then CRASH before the background writer flushes. Redo re-applies #102 and #103 from the log, and Ravi has ₹200 while Meera has ₹800. No-force means committed changes routinely exist only in the log at crash time. This is not an edge case; it is the normal state of affairs. Every crash of a busy database is this. "Recovery took 90 seconds" is exactly this quantity, and it is tuned with the checkpoint interval.

F3 — Crash with an uncommitted change already on disk. Start T2, force a buffer eviction using the page's "buffer full → steal a page" control, then CRASH. Redo repeats history, taking page 9 to ₹700 and the total to ₹900, wrong on purpose. Then undo reverts T2 using its before-image and writes a CLR, and ₹1000 is restored. Steal is mandatory for a database that handles more data than RAM (§10.2), so uncommitted data on disk is a designed-for state. Recovery now has a third pass, and it is the slow one — undo does random reads and writes over whatever the losers touched. This is why killing a database in the middle of a giant DELETE produces a long, un-skippable rollback on restart, and why the advice is always "let it finish". InnoDB reports it as InnoDB: Rolling back trx ... N% complete.

F4 — Crash during recovery itself. Run recovery, hit CRASH mid-undo, then recover again. You land in the same final state. Redo is idempotent because of the pageLSN comparison, and undo resumes from the last CLR's undoNext instead of restarting. Recovery is not a privileged mode. It can be interrupted like anything else, so it had to be designed to be restartable (§10.4, §10.5). Crash loops are real: bad memory, a failing disk, an OOM killer with a taste for the database. A recovery that were not restartable would turn a transient fault into permanent corruption.

F5 — Torn page. Switch off full-page writes in the visualization, then CRASH during a page write. With the protection on, recovery restores the whole page from the log's full image and replays forward, and the result is correct. With it off, the page is half old and half new, and redo's physiological instructions land on incoherent structure. The visualization marks the page corrupt, and the total is unrecoverable from the log alone. A multi-sector write is not atomic (§10.6). In a real system this surfaces later as invalid page header in block ..., a checksum error at read time, long after the crash. This is exactly why full_page_writes=off and innodb_doublewrite=OFF are documented as unsafe, except on storage that guarantees atomic page writes.

F6 — The device that lies about fsync. Set the visualization's safe to "lies about the seal". Commit records are acknowledged as durable while still sitting in a volatile on-device cache. Power loss then destroys transactions the client was told had committed. Recovery cannot detect it; the log simply ends earlier than the client's receipts imply. Durability is a claim by every layer beneath you, and a claim is only as good as the weakest layer (§12.3). This is the worst failure in the lab, because nothing anywhere reports an error. PostgreSQL's own reliability documentation discusses drives that acknowledge writes before they are stable, and the same class of problem appears with misconfigured virtualised storage. The self-inflicted version is fsync = off, which is a benchmark-only setting.

F7 — Process crash versus power loss. The CRASH control has two modes. A kill -9 of the database process loses the buffer pool but not the OS page cache, so log bytes that reached write() are still there and recovery finds them. A power cut loses the page cache too, so anything not fsynced is gone. The page cache belongs to the kernel, not to the process. You have your own figures for this from redis: kill -9 with appendfsync no lost nothing, power loss cost 530 ms of traffic, and always gave zero loss at 3.4× lower throughput. Same three moments, same arithmetic, different product. This is why a setting like innodb_flush_log_at_trx_commit = 2 is not simply "unsafe". It is safe against process crash and unsafe against power loss, which is a real and sometimes acceptable distinction.

F8 — The log disk fills up. This one is not simulated in the visualization; it is here from vendor documentation, not from a run. The WAL cannot be appended, so no transaction can commit, because committing is appending. The database stops accepting writes, and PostgreSQL will PANIC and shut down when it cannot write WAL. The log is on the critical path of every commit by construction. Nothing is corrupted, but availability is gone completely. The classic cause is a replication slot or an archiver that stopped consuming WAL, so the log cannot be recycled and grows without bound. "Disk full on the WAL volume" is an outage, not a warning.

F9 — The client never learns the outcome. CRASH in the window between the commit fsync and the acknowledgement reaching the client. The transaction is committed and durable, but the client sees a timeout or a dropped connection and does not know it. The acknowledgement travels over a network, and the network is not part of the transaction. Atomicity and durability are both perfect. What is lost is the client's certainty — and if it retries, you get the transfer twice. This is precisely the problem your idempotency lab solves. The boundary is worth stating bluntly. ACID gives you a correct database. It tells you nothing about whether the caller knows it. The fix lives above the database: an idempotency key stored inside the same transaction, so the retry recognises itself.

F10 — Failover to a replica that was behind. This one is not in this lab's visualization either, since the lab is single-node; it is described from the labs that ran it. The primary acknowledges a commit after its own local fsync, then dies. An asynchronous replica is promoted. Transactions committed inside the replication lag window are gone — and they were acknowledged. The fsync made the write durable on that machine, the machine was the failure domain, and the machine is what failed. Your own figures from redis: 500 acked writes, 500 lost across a failover, with WAIT able to measure the window but not to close it. Single-node atomicity is intact and now irrelevant; durability, as the user understands the word, is gone. This is what synchronous_commit = remote_apply, min-replicas-to-write, and Kafka's acks=all with min.insync.replicas are for. 12. What do the four promises actually promise?

In one breath: Atomicity is only about crashes and aborts. Durability is only about the failures you configured for. Isolation is a different machine. And consistency was never the database's job in the first place.

12.1 What do A and D really guarantee, and what do people think they do?

Atomicity, precisely stated: for every transaction, either all of its changes are present in the database or none are, with respect to crashes and aborts. That's it. The two words at the end are the whole scope.

Three things atomicity is regularly assumed to mean, and does not.

It does not mean "invisible while running". Whether another transaction can see your half-finished work is isolation, not atomicity. At read uncommitted it can; at read committed it cannot; and you measured all of it in isolation-levels. A database can be perfectly atomic and still show a concurrent reader a state that never legally existed.

It does not extend past this database. Two databases, or a database and a message broker, are two commit points. Making them one act is two-phase-commit. Doing it without fracturing readers is distributed-transactions, whose result was 16 fractured audits in 40 runs, with perfect 2PC and zero crashes, because atomic durability is not atomic visibility.

"Atomic" is now three different words in your vocabulary. In the java-memory-model it meant "no other thread observes a half-written value". In redis it meant "the command runs to completion on the one thread, and there is no rollback". Here it means "all or nothing across a crash". They are genuinely different guarantees, and conflating them is a reliable source of bugs.

Durability, precisely stated: once the client has been told "committed", the effects survive the failures the storage layer was configured to survive. Not "all failures". Section 12.3.

12.2 Why is C not like the others?

A, I and D name mechanisms the database implements: a log and recovery passes for A and D, a concurrency-control scheme for I. Disable any of them and something inside the database changes.

C names an outcome you are responsible for. Written out fully, the C in ACID says: a transaction takes the database from one valid state to another valid state. Now ask the obvious question. Valid by whose definition? The answer is: yours. The database has never heard of your business rules.

🔮 Predict: Your service layer checks every balance before it writes, and refuses anything negative. Does the database now guarantee that no balance is ever negative?

Reveal **No, and the gap is not about bugs in your check.** Your check protects one code path. The database has many writers.

If you guessed "yes, because every write goes through our service", list the writers again. The other team's service. Last month's migration script. The support engineer typing UPDATE into a psql session at 2 a.m. A bulk import. A restored backup. None of those pass through your if statement.

The one bridge between your definition of valid and the database's ability to enforce it is a declared constraint. That is the whole of what C buys you, and it is why C is a to-do list rather than a feature.

Go back to the branch for a second. There is exactly one thing Asha's manager can do that survives Asha leaving. She can paint the rule on the wall behind the counter. Then every cashier has to obey it, including Vikram, and including the temp on Saturday. In a database that painted rule is a declared constraint. Three of them, in plain words first, then as statements:

  • No account balance may ever go below zero.
  • No two bookings may hold the same room on the same day.
  • No order may name a customer who does not exist.

And the same three, as SQL:

Everything you declare there, the database will defend against every writer, forever: your application, the other team's application, the migration script, and the support engineer at 2 a.m.

Everything you don't declare lives in an if statement in one service. "The total across all accounts is ₹1000." "A shipped order must have a payment." The database will happily, atomically and durably commit their violation.

You reached this same conclusion from the other direction in isolation-levels. It is worth quoting your own knowledge map back at you.

"a constraint beats an isolation level whenever the rule can be stated about the data rather than the decision — an isolation level protects the code paths that remember to ask; a constraint protects the table."

That is the practical form of C. The letter C is a to-do list. Move as much of your definition of "valid" as you can into declarations the engine enforces. That portion is the only part of C that ACID is actually buying you.

Two more traps worth naming.

The C in ACID is not the C in CAP. ACID's C is "the data satisfies its rules". CAP's C is linearizability, a recency and ordering guarantee about reads on a distributed system, which you have a whole lab on (linearizability, plus the distinction from serializability in linearizability-vs-serializability). Same letter, unrelated concepts, and the collision causes real confusion in design discussions. When someone says "we need consistency", make them say which one.

Constraint checking has a timing. Most constraints are checked per statement. Some can be DEFERRABLE INITIALLY DEFERRED and are checked at commit instead, which is what lets a transaction pass through an intermediate state that violates a foreign key. Worth knowing the switch exists; the details are engine-specific.

12.3 Durable against what, exactly?

There is no such thing as durable. There is a ladder, and every rung survives strictly more than the one below it. Read the third column: it is what still kills you at that height.

Rung Survives Doesn't survive Costs
in the process's log buffer nothing a kill -9 free
after write() — OS page cache process crash power loss free
after fsync() power loss on this same machine this disk dying one sync per commit, or per group
on redundant local storage (RAID, cloud block store) one disk dying this machine, this availability zone hardware or service cost

Two consequences decide real architectures.

"We use an ACID database, so we can't lose data" is a category error. ACID's D is a rung on that ladder, chosen by configuration. F10 is what it looks like when the rung you're standing on is lower than the failure you met.

Every rung above fsync is bought with a network round trip. That is the exact latency-versus-consistency currency you priced in pacelc. Even with no partition anywhere, stronger durability costs latency on every single write. Durability is a PACELC "else" branch.

12.4 Which rules does the machine actually keep?

Six rules, and each one was earned by a specific failure above. The bracket at the end of each says what happens without it.

  • WAL rule: for any change, its log record is durable before the modified page is written to the data file. (Without it: F3 has no before-image to undo with.)
  • Commit rule: a transaction's commit record is durable before the client is told "committed". (Without it: F6 — silent durability loss.)
  • Redo idempotence: apply record L to page P only if L.LSN > P.pageLSN. (Without it: F4 corrupts on a second recovery.)
  • Repeating history: redo re-applies losers' changes too, so undo always starts from the pre-crash state. (Without it: two undo code paths.)
  • CLRs are never undone, and each carries undoNext. (Without it: F4's rollback may never terminate.)
  • Page-write atomicity is not assumed. It is manufactured, by full-page writes or a doublewrite buffer. (Without it: F5.)

12.5 What did all of this cost?

Nothing here is free, and the bills are specific.

Every change is written twice, once to the log and once to the data file. That is write amplification on top of the amplification you already found for the storage structure itself in btree-and-lsm: 119× for a B-tree, 2.5× for an LSM.

Every commit costs a durable sequential write. Group commit amortises it. Nothing removes it.

Crash recovery time is not zero, and it scales with how far the data file lagged. That is an availability cost, paid at the worst possible moment.

Rollback is expensive and unskippable. A huge aborted transaction must be undone before the database is fully usable.

The log must be retained and recycled. That introduces a whole class of operational failure, F8, that a database without a log would not have.

InnoDB keeps undo separate from redo, and that is not a detail. Its undo logs are also what serve MVCC reads: an old row version is reconstructed by walking undo records backwards. So in InnoDB, the same structure that provides atomicity also provides isolation. That is why a long-running read transaction bloats undo, with History list length climbing. SQLite's original design sits in the other corner of section 10.2's table. A rollback journal is undo-only with force. After a crash, it copies the originals back. It is a complete, correct, simple design, and it pays exactly the price the table predicts: every commit forces the changed pages out, and readers block writers. SQLite's WAL mode later moved it to the redo corner. The release notes list better concurrency and fewer fsyncs as the benefits, which is precisely what the table says you get for it.

13.2 What does the durability dial look like in the three forms you'll meet?

All three are the same ladder from section 12.3. All three have a middle setting that people misread as "unsafe", when it really means "safe against process crash, unsafe against power loss" (F7).

PostgreSQL uses synchronous_commit. Its settings: on means the WAL is fsynced locally before the acknowledgement. local means the same, ignoring replicas. remote_write, on with synchronous_standby_names, and remote_apply all require a standby to have it, which moves you up the ladder and costs a round trip. off acknowledges before the local fsync, so you can lose a bounded window of committed transactions while the database stays consistent. Postgres's own documentation is careful about this distinction: synchronous_commit=off risks data loss, while fsync=off risks corruption. Those are very different bets.

MySQL and InnoDB use innodb_flush_log_at_trx_commit. Setting 1 writes and fsyncs at every commit; it is the default and it is fully durable. Setting 2 writes at every commit and fsyncs roughly once a second, so it survives mysqld crashing but not the operating system dying. Setting 0 writes and fsyncs about once a second, losing up to a second in any crash. And sync_binlog=1 is the second half of the story: the binary log has its own durability setting, and replication and point-in-time recovery depend on it.

SQLite uses PRAGMA synchronous. FULL and EXTRA are the safe settings. NORMAL, in WAL mode, does not sync on every commit, so a power loss can lose recent commits without corrupting the file. OFF is what it sounds like.

13.3 Why is there a two-phase commit inside a single MySQL server?

This one is worth knowing because it closes a loop with an earlier lab.

MySQL has two logs that must agree. InnoDB's redo log decides what the storage engine recovered. The server's binary log decides what replicas and point-in-time recovery see. If a crash left them disagreeing, a replica would diverge from its primary.

So MySQL runs an internal two-phase commit between them on every commit: prepare in InnoDB, write the binlog, commit in InnoDB. On recovery, a transaction found prepared in InnoDB is committed if it is present in the binlog, and rolled back otherwise.

Coordinator, participants, prepare and vote, the commit point, in-doubt on recovery — every noun from your two-phase-commit lab, running inside one process, on a single machine, thousands of times a second. It is the cleanest example there is of why that lab's material is not only about distributed systems.

13.5 What did this lab deliberately leave out?

The machine in section 10 is complete enough to be correct, and small enough to hold in your head. Real engines add more, and this lab does not cover it.

  • Log-file management — segmentation, recycling, archiving, and point-in-time recovery by replaying archived WAL onto a base backup. This is the feature that makes the log valuable for something other than crashes.
  • Physical versus logical logging — how much of a change to record, and the physiological middle ground real engines use. We waved at this in §10.6.
  • Index and structural changes — a B-tree page split is many page changes that must be atomic among themselves. Real systems use nested top actions, which are CLR-like markers making a structural change un-undoable. Your btree-and-lsm lab's split cascade is exactly this case.
  • Concurrency in recovery — parallel redo workers, and the fact that redo must respect per-page order while being parallel across pages.
  • Isolation and MVCC, entirely — isolation-levels.
  • Replication, entirely — the log is also the replication stream. The WAL is what a Postgres standby consumes, and what logical decoding turns into a CDC feed. That is a lab of its own.
  • Backups and verification — a durable database with an unrestorable backup is a slower way to lose data.

16. Glossary — what does each word mean, in one line?

Grouped by theme, and in order of first appearance within each group.

The contract itself

  • Transaction — one job that should count as a single act.
  • Commit — the moment a transaction officially counts and its effects are guaranteed.
  • Abort / rollback — ending a transaction so that none of its effects remain; abort is the decision, rollback the work.
  • Atomicity (A) — all of a transaction's changes are present, or none, with respect to crashes and aborts.
  • Consistency (C) — the data still satisfies its rules afterwards; only the rules you declared are enforced by the database.
  • Isolation (I) — concurrent transactions do not corrupt each other's logic; a separate dial with its own levels.
  • Durability (D) — once committed, the effects survive the failures the storage layer was configured to survive.

Where the bytes actually sit

  • Page — the fixed-size chunk (8 KB / 16 KB) a database reads and writes at a time.
  • Buffer pool — the database's in-memory cache of disk pages, where all changes actually happen.
  • Dirty page — an in-memory page whose change hasn't reached disk yet.
  • OS page cache — kernel memory holding written-but-not-synced bytes; survives a process crash, not a power loss.
  • fsync — the call that forces written data onto the physical device.
  • Failure domain — the scope of what a given durability guarantee actually protects against: a process, a machine, a disk, an availability zone, a region.

The log's own vocabulary

  • Write-ahead log (WAL) — an append-only file recording every intended change before it is made.
  • Log record — one entry in the log: begin, update, commit, abort, or CLR.
  • Before-image / after-image — the old and new values in an update record; they buy undo and redo respectively.
  • LSN (log sequence number) — the position of a record in the log; a global order for every change in the database.
  • prevLSN — pointer from a log record to the previous record of the same transaction, forming a backward chain used by rollback.
  • pageLSN — the LSN of the most recent change already reflected in a page; the comparison that makes redo idempotent.
  • Checkpoint — a log record marking a point recovery can start from, plus the flushing that makes it true.

Putting the books right afterwards

  • Redo — re-applying a committed change using its after-image.
  • Undo — reverting a change using its before-image.
  • Recovery — the startup procedure that reads the log and repairs the data files.
  • Analysis / redo / undo passes — the three phases of ARIES recovery.
  • Repeating history — redoing all changes, losers included, so undo starts from the pre-crash state.
  • Loser / winner — a transaction that had not committed / had committed at the moment of the crash.
  • CLR (compensation log record) — a record of undo work already performed; never itself undone, and carrying undoNext.
  • undoNext — the pointer in a CLR to the next record still needing undo, making rollback restartable.
  • ARIES — the recovery algorithm (analysis, redo, undo, pageLSN, CLRs) that section 10 derives and real engines implement.

Policies, and the hazards they answer

  • Steal / no-steal — whether an uncommitted dirty page may be written to disk. Steal requires undo.
  • Force / no-force — whether all of a transaction's pages must reach disk before commit returns. No-force requires redo.
  • Torn page — a page left partly old and partly new by an interrupted multi-sector write.
  • Full-page writes — logging a whole page image after each checkpoint to survive torn pages (PostgreSQL).
  • Doublewrite buffer — writing every page twice, scratch then home, to survive torn pages (InnoDB).
  • Group commit — batching several transactions' commit records into one fsync; latency for throughput.

ACID — solutions

Attempt EXERCISES.md first. Rungs 7–8 are worked discussions, not single answers — judge yours by whether it names the same tradeoffs, not by wording.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 A program writing to a file can be interrupted halfway and leave the file in a state that looks perfectly normal but is wrong, with nothing left anywhere to say what was in progress. A database has to survive that, because the thing it's changing is often two separate numbers that must always agree. The idea that solves it: write down what you're about to do, somewhere that survives, before you do it — then whoever comes along after the interruption can either finish the job or take it back.

1.2 Being fast changes how often you land in the gap between the two updates; it never removes the gap. At any real write rate, "rare" happens several times a day — and each occurrence is silent.

1.3 Because "committed" doesn't mean "the data is in its final place". It means the record of what to do is somewhere that survives. The tidy version of the data gets written later; if the machine dies first, the record is enough to reproduce it exactly. It's the difference between "the cheque has cleared" and "the money has been physically moved to the right shelf".

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 (a) kill -9: everything except the buffer pool and the log buffer — so the OS page cache, the device cache and the disk all survive. (b) power cut: only the disk band survives.

2.2 The stamp is the page's pageLSN — the LSN of the most recent log record already reflected in that page. Recovery uses it for exactly one decision: apply this log record, or skip it (record.LSN > page.pageLSN). That single comparison is what makes redo safe to run any number of times.

2.3 The after-image finishes the job (redo). The before-image takes it back (undo). Both are on the same record, which is why steal + no-force is affordable.

2.4 Recovery wrote the CLR line(s) and the END line. They must be in the log because recovery can itself be interrupted: the CLR is the only evidence that a particular piece of undo already happened, and END is the only evidence that a rollback finished. Without them, the next recovery would start the rollback again from the beginning.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 Analysis only changes anything internally; redo and undo each do nothing visible. Redo checks both pages, sees each page's stamp is already at or beyond the record's LSN, and skips. Undo has no losers. This is the fastest possible recovery, and it is what a clean checkpoint just before the crash buys you.

3.2 It is completely legal, and it is the whole point of steal: page 5 carries an uncommitted change. Recovery's analysis marks T1 a loser (no commit record), redo re-applies #102 (skipped here — the stamp already matches), and undo reverts page 5 to ₹700 using the before-image, writing a CLR. Total afterwards: ₹1000.

3.3 Yes, it re-applies them — and then undoes them moments later. The reason is not efficiency, it's simplicity: by repeating history, the undo pass always begins from a state identical to the pre-crash state, which is exactly the state ordinary runtime rollback begins from. One undo implementation serves both cases with no special-casing. Skipping losers in redo would force undo to ask "is this change even here?" on every record.

3.4 Process kill: the transfer survives. The commit record was handed to the out-tray (write()), and the out-tray is the kernel's, not the process's — so recovery finds it. The lying safe is irrelevant here because nothing had to be sealed. Power cut: the transfer is lost, and the verdict says Durability broken, because the client was told "done". Two switches, both "unsafe", and only one of them bites in each failure — which is the entire argument of README §12.3.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Day-book off. A crash between the two page writes leaves ₹500 and no trace that the two changes belonged together. Atomicity, lost silently — no error at crash time, none at startup, none at read time. The books are simply wrong forever. This is §3's naive design and it is why the log exists.

4.2 Stealing off. Nothing breaks in this simulation, and that is the lesson: correctness is fine, capacity is not. A transaction can now never modify more data than fits in the buffer pool, and a long transaction makes its pages unevictable for its whole lifetime, starving everyone else. No production engine chooses it because "your UPDATE must fit in RAM" is not a database.

4.3 Force on. It buys the ability to delete the redo pass entirely — every committed change is already on disk, so there is nothing to finish. It costs random I/O across every touched page inside the commit path, before the client hears anything, and a hot page gets rewritten once per transaction instead of once per checkpoint. That is why real engines pay for redo instead: one sequential append plus one fsync beats scattered page writes, every time.

4.4 Full page copies off. Redo can repair an out-of-date page because such a page is still internally coherent — the row offsets, headers and slot directory all agree, they're just older. A torn page is incoherent: half its structure is from before and half from after. A redo record saying "set the balance field of the row in slot 3" has no valid target. Neither is the page's own stamp trustworthy, since it may have come from either half. Durability and Atomicity, lost silently at crash time and discovered much later as a checksum error at read time.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 "Re-read the row to roll back." The data file does not hold the old value — it holds whatever is there now, which may already be your own new value (the page was stolen to disk), or someone else's. Concrete run: T1 sets p5 ₹700 → ₹200; the page is written to disk; T1 aborts. Rolling back by re-reading page 5 reads ₹200 and "restores" ₹200. Atomicity violated, and the money is gone with no error. The before-image exists precisely because the old value is destroyed by the act of writing the new one (README §4, Failure B).

6.2 "The pageLSN check is redundant." Two problems. First, real redo records are often changes, not values ("add ₹500"); applying one twice doubles it. Second, and fatal even for absolute values: recovery can crash and re-run, and it can also legitimately re-apply records to a page that already has them because the page was stolen to disk after the change. Concrete run: T1 commits "p9 += ₹500"; page 9 reaches disk with the change; crash; redo re-applies → ₹1300; total ₹1500. Violated: Atomicity/Durability's arithmetic, silently.

6.3 "Skip undo for changes that never reached disk." This is exactly what repeating history was invented to avoid, and it breaks on partial page history. Concrete run: T2 changes p9 twice — ₹800→₹700 (#106) and ₹700→₹650 (#107). Page 9 is stolen to disk after #106 but before #107. The optimisation inspects page 9, sees ₹700, concludes "#107 never landed, skip it" — correct — then inspects #106, sees ₹700 present, undoes it to ₹800. That happens to work here; now reverse the steal order or add a third update and the check has to reason about which of several changes a page reflects, using only one stamp. The real cost is that undo now needs a different code path from runtime rollback, and every future refinement has to be correct in both. Redo-then-undo is more work and less machinery.

6.4 "Ack from the log buffer, fsync in the background." The log buffer is process memory. A kill -9 — not even a power cut — destroys it. Concrete run: 10 ms of transactions are acked; the process is OOM-killed; those transactions never existed. Durability broken, and worse than the synchronous_commit=off case, which at least survives a process kill because the bytes reached the kernel. Group commit is not this: group commit still does a real fsync before any ack, it just shares one fsync between many transactions. The distinction is the entire content of README §7.2's three moments.

6.5 "The service layer validates, so we don't need constraints." The service layer protects the code paths that remember to ask. The constraint protects the table. Concrete run: a data migration written by another team, a backfill script, an admin's psql session, a second service added next quarter, or your own code path added by someone who didn't know the rule — any of these writes a negative balance, atomically and durably, and no error is raised. Consistency broken, and the database performed perfectly. See README §12.2: this is what the C in ACID actually asks of you.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 The lost ack. Store an idempotency key — the client's own request identifier — in a table with a UNIQUE constraint, written inside the same transaction as the transfer. On a retry, the insert violates the constraint, the transaction aborts, and the service returns the stored original outcome instead of transferring again.

Why nowhere else fails:

  • In a cache (Redis) instead: the key and the money are now two commit points, so a crash between them re-opens the exact hole you were closing. Your redis lab measured the general version of this.
  • In a separate transaction, before: crash after writing the key and before the transfer, and the retry is rejected for work that never happened.
  • In a separate transaction, after: crash after the transfer and before the key, and the retry duplicates it. The key must be in the same atomic unit as the effect, which is the whole point of having atomicity in the first place. See the idempotency lab for the response-replay contract that goes with it.

7.2 The crash-looping rollback. Each undo step writes a CLR before it touches the page, and each CLR names undoNext. So after every crash, the next recovery's analysis finds the transaction's most recent CLR, reads undoNext, and resumes there. The remaining work only ever shrinks, so with each crash the rollback gets closer to done and eventually finishes even if it is interrupted hundreds of times. Without CLRs, every restart would begin the rollback from the transaction's last record — a 900,000-row rollback restarting every two minutes never completes, and the database is permanently unavailable while being, technically, perfectly correct.

7.3 The lying storage. Two mitigations at different rungs:

  • Same machine, lower rung: battery-backed or capacitor-backed write cache (a proper RAID controller with BBU, or an enterprise SSD with power-loss protection). This makes the device's acknowledgement honest, so fsync regains its meaning. Covers power loss on that machine. Does not cover the machine dying, the disk dying, or firmware that lies about having power-loss protection.
  • Above the machine, higher rung: synchronous replication — require a second machine to have the commit record before acking (synchronous_commit with a sync standby, min-replicas-to-write, acks=all + min.insync.replicas). Now durability no longer depends on any single device telling the truth. Covers the machine and the disk. Does not cover a correlated failure (identical firmware bug on both), a region loss, or the extra ~1 network round trip on every write — which is the pacelc "else" branch, priced.

Both are mitigations, not fixes. The fix is storage that doesn't lie.

7.4 Crash during analysis. Nothing needs to be added: analysis writes nothing and changes nothing. It only builds two in-memory tables from the log. A crash during it loses only that in-memory work, and the next recovery rebuilds the identical tables from the identical log — the log did not change. It is easier than surviving a crash during undo for exactly one reason: undo mutates, so it needs a durable record of its own progress (the CLR), whereas analysis is a pure function of the log.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 Payments across three regions.

The rule "must never be lost even if a region is destroyed" puts you on the quorum-of-replicas rung, minimum. Local fsync and even a local sync standby both fail the stated requirement.

  • Commit path: local WAL fsync, then wait for at least one remote-region replica to have the record durably, then ack. Cost: ~80 ms one way, so ≥ 80 ms added to every write, before any local work. Against a 250 ms p99 budget that is affordable but it is now the dominant term — you have spent a third of the budget on durability, deliberately.
  • Shape: 3,000 writes/s against one sync round trip means the log's group commit is doing heavy lifting; check that the engine batches replication acknowledgements rather than paying 80 ms serially per transaction.
  • Monitor: replication lag in LSN bytes and in seconds per replica; the number of currently-synchronous replicas (a silent demotion to async is the failure that looks like a latency improvement); commit latency p50/p99 split by "local fsync" and "waiting for remote"; WAL volume and free space on the log volume; and time-to-recover after a crash.
  • During failover: the sync replica becomes primary with no acked data lost — that is what you paid for. But the quorum shrinks, so either you drop to a lower durability rung (faster, weaker) or you block writes until a replacement joins (safe, an outage). Decide which before the incident; the default is usually the one you didn't choose deliberately.
  • What I would least be surprised to be paged about: the sync replica silently falling out of the synchronous set — writes get faster, nothing alerts, and durability quietly drops a rung. Alert on the count of synchronous replicas, not just on lag.

8.2 Order plus Kafka event. Walk §13.6: the work spans two datastores; they cannot live in one; and the event is derivable from the order. That lands on transactional outbox: write the order and an outbox row in one local ACID transaction, and a relay publishes to Kafka afterwards with an idempotent consumer downstream.

  • Kept: full A, C, I, D on the order itself — the part that is the source of truth.
  • Given up: atomicity across the two systems, and immediacy. The event is eventually published, not simultaneously; consumers may see it late, and may see it twice.
  • Business consequence: a customer can be shown "order placed" before any downstream service reacts, so anything that must be instantly consistent with the order (a stock count on the same page, say) has to read the database, not the event stream. The duplicate delivery is harmless only if every consumer is idempotent — which is a requirement you now own forever, not a detail.
  • Why not 2PC: it makes Kafka and your database into each other's availability dependency and reintroduces the blocking problem, for a guarantee the business did not ask for. Why not a saga: there is no multi-step business workflow to compensate here, just one derived effect.

8.3 The tuning complaint. Their proposal helps one problem and worsens the other, and neither is the actual cause of the WAL growth.

  • 6-minute startup is the redo pass: the data file was far behind the log when the crash happened. Less frequent checkpoints make this strictly worse — more log to replay. To fix it you want checkpoints more frequent (or a smaller max_wal_size), paying steadier background I/O for a shorter outage.
  • WAL volume filling twice a month is almost never checkpoint frequency. The usual cause is that WAL cannot be recycled: an abandoned replication slot, a failing archive command, or a very long-running transaction. Checkpoint frequency affects WAL generation a little (via full-page writes, which spike right after each checkpoint — so more frequent checkpoints generate more WAL), but it cannot cause unbounded growth. Something is holding the log.
  • First change: find and fix what is pinning WAL (inspect replication slots and the archiver), and add an alert on the oldest retained WAL position — not just on disk free space, which alerts only when it is already an outage.
  • Then: shorten the checkpoint interval to bring recovery time down, and watch background write I/O and WAL generation rate to confirm the cost is acceptable. Note the tension you have just made explicit: more frequent checkpoints shorten recovery and increase WAL volume. That is the real tradeoff, and it is not the one they proposed.

8.4 The invariant that isn't a constraint. Three places, ranked by how many writers each protects against:

  1. In the schema, by restructuring so the rule becomes declarable — the strongest answer and the one most often missed. Store deposits and withdrawals as immutable rows and derive balances, or hold the invariant as a per-account rule the engine can check. If the rule can be restated as a property of rows rather than of a decision, a constraint can hold it, and then it protects every writer that will ever exist, including migrations and a human at a psql prompt.
  2. In the database, in code the database runs — a trigger or an assertion at commit. Protects every writer that goes through the database, which is all of them, but costs correctness-critical logic living in a place that is hard to test and easy to forget during a schema change.
  3. In one service's application code — protects only the code paths that call it. The moment a second service, a backfill, or an admin writes directly, the rule is gone. This is the option most systems actually have, which is why the reconciliation job in option 4 exists.
  4. (The one everyone ends up with anyway) A periodic reconciliation check that recomputes the sum and alerts on drift. It protects nothing — it only tells you the rule has already been broken. Worth having regardless, because it is the only one of the four that detects a bug in the other three.
