Anti-Entropy

Tell a friend: Asha at head office puts the croissant price up and rings her three shops. Meera and Arjun answer, so the change is live. Omar's line is busy, and no part of the process is supposed to go back for him, so his shop sells at last week's price for a year. Anti-entropy is the background job that does go back: it compares a summary of each shop's whole price list, splits wherever two summaries disagree, and repairs what it finds. The rest of this lab is the eleven ways that repair still leaves a customer paying the wrong price.

The simulator is visualization.html, also published at https://claude.ai/code/artifact/f8ee9833-c3e8-4c29-9796-c2c68b8954b2. The lab contents, the engine runs, and how every number in this document was made are in section 17 at the end.

BEGINNER MODEL

1. What part of the system goes back and fixes the third copy?

In one breath: You keep three copies of every row so that one dead machine cannot lose your data. The moment you do, the three copies can stop being the same. Nothing in the ordinary business of reading and writing is responsible for noticing.

Asha runs pricing at head office. She keeps three copies of every price, because a machine holding the only copy will one day die.

The instant she does that, she creates a new thing that can go wrong. The three copies can stop being the same.

Here is the part that surprises people. This is not caused by a bug. It is caused by the system working exactly as designed.

A write that reached two of the three copies is a successful write. Asha configured it that way on purpose, so that one sick machine cannot stop her shops selling. The write returns success. And the third copy is now holding last week's value.

So ask the obvious question. What part of the system goes back and fixes that third copy?

In the write path, nothing. The write is finished, and it was a success.

In the read path, almost nothing. A read that happens to touch the stale copy might notice. But only if it touches it, and only for the one row it asked about.

There is no other code running. The third copy holds a wrong value for as long as nobody happens to look. For most rows in most databases, that means forever.

So divergence is not a rare accident to be handled. It is the continuous, normal residue of a system that keeps serving while machines come and go. And it accumulates.

Anti-entropy is the name for the background work that goes looking for those differences and removes them. Without it, "eventually consistent" is a promise with no machinery behind it.

Why did your last three labs walk past this?

Three labs you have already done all pointed here and then changed the subject.

quorums-and-replication ended by listing "read repair, anti-entropy, Merkle tree" among its key terms. Three names, no mechanism. It is also the lab where you learned the counting rule that this one leans on: tell enough replicas and ask enough replicas, and the two groups are forced to share one. In symbols, R + W > N.

eventual-consistency told you the replicas converge. It did not tell you what does the converging.

gossip-protocols taught you, the hard way, that a merge rule must be a join. You built one that was not, and forty orderings of the same five facts produced two permanently different states.

Put those together. You have a system that creates divergence on purpose, then promises it will go away, and holds one rule for combining two versions. Nothing in any of them ever goes and looks. That missing piece is this lab.

2. Why does Omar's shop sell croissants at last week's price for a year?

In one breath: Head office changes a price and rings three shops. Two answer and one does not, and the rule says two is enough. Nobody's job includes going back for the third.

A chain of three bakeries: shop A, shop B, shop C. Meera runs A. Arjun runs B. Omar runs C.

Asha at head office sets the prices. Each shop also keeps its own printed price list, the whole list, all sixteen items. A shop has to be able to sell when the office phone is engaged.

Monday morning, Asha puts the price of a croissant up to £4.00 and rings round. Meera answers and writes it down. Arjun answers and writes it down. Omar's line is busy, because he is out at the back signing for a flour delivery.

Asha's rule is simple. Two shops confirmed means the change is live. Two shops confirmed. She ticks the job off her list and moves on to the scones.

Nobody is coming back for Omar.

Omar sells croissants at £2.40 on Tuesday. And next month. And next year. Not because anything broke. The phone worked, the rule was followed, the change was recorded as a success. The only thing that happened is that no part of the process was responsible for going back to check.

3. Why not just fix it when a customer asks?

In one breath: When Priya asks the price, ring two shops instead of one, believe the newer answer, and correct the shop that was behind. It is cheap, it is real, and on its own it is close to useless.

The trouble is that one shop is out of date. So check when it matters.

When Priya actually asks the price of a croissant, ring two shops instead of one. Take the newer of the two answers. And while you have them on the phone, correct the one that was behind.

This is genuinely a good idea, and every real system does it.

It costs almost nothing, because you were making the phone call anyway. It fixes the row Priya cares about at the exact moment she cares about it. It needs no new background process, no schedule, and no extra machine.

It is also, on its own, close to useless. There are three separate reasons. Each one teaches a different thing about what a repair mechanism has to do, so it is worth seeing all three.

4. Where does fixing-on-read break?

In one breath: It only fixes rows somebody reads, it only reaches the shops you happened to ring, and every repair it does is work done while a customer waits.

Limit one — what happens to the four rows nobody asked about?

Covered just above. Five rows wrong, one read, four still wrong.

In the week-long run this is the difference between the 3 divergent hot rows and the 13 divergent cold rows. Fixing-on-read attacks exactly the 3. It cannot touch the 13.

Limit two — which shop does the call actually reach?

This is the one people do not expect.

You keep three copies but you only ring two, because ringing two is what makes the system fast and available. Which two? Whichever two answer first. In practice that means the two that are healthy and nearby.

Now think about which shop is stale. It is usually the one that has just come back from being sick. Which is to say, it is the one least likely to be in the group you rang.

🔮 Predict: So lower the number of shops you ring to one. Faster, cheaper, fewer calls. What does fixing-on-read do now?

Reveal

Nothing at all, ever. The read went to Omar's shop, was answered 2.40 when the true price was 9.00, and repaired nobody. That is Scenario 2 with the number of shops asked, R, set to 1.

If you said "it still fixes the row it read", you are assuming the answer can be checked. It cannot. There is nothing to check it against.

The reason is worth saying precisely, because it is the seed of the whole lab. You cannot detect a disagreement with one copy. Repair is not something a shop can do by looking at its own list. It requires comparison, and comparison requires at least two. A single copy has no way to know it is wrong. From the inside, being stale looks exactly like being right.

Limit three — who pays for the repair?

Every repair on the read path is work done while somebody is waiting.

Do it before answering and Priya's read gets slower. Do it after answering and the read that triggered the repair may still hand her the stale price.

Neither is a disaster. But it means you cannot solve the problem by turning this dial up. Repairing hard on the read path degrades the very thing you built the copies for.

What do the three limits add up to?

Put them together and you get the deeper problem, the one this lab is actually about.

The rows most likely to be silently wrong are exactly the rows nobody is looking at. And any repair mechanism that lives on the read path is, by construction, blind to them.

That is a nasty shape. Your monitoring will look fine.

The problem stays invisible until one of three days arrives. The day somebody runs a report. The day a row that was cold for a year suddenly becomes hot. Or the day you fail over to the copy that was wrong.

5. What would a real repair process have to do?

In one breath: Five requirements. It runs on a clock, it compares whole lists, and it visits the sick shop on purpose. It costs what the differences cost rather than what the data costs. And it ends in an answer that does not depend on the order things were repaired in.

Say it in plain words first. Then we will go and build it.

Asha needs a process that does five things.

  1. Runs whether or not anyone reads the data. Its trigger is a schedule, not a customer.
  2. Compares whole price lists, not single lines. It must be able to find a difference in a row nobody has named.
  3. Reaches every shop, not a fast subset. It must go and look at the sick one specifically.
  4. Costs in proportion to the differences, not to the data. If two copies holding 500 GB differ in three rows, finding those three rows must not cost 500 GB of network. This requirement is the one that generates all the interesting engineering. Without it the whole lab would be one paragraph long.
  5. Ends in a defined answer that does not depend on the order. When it finds Meera saying 4.00 and Omar saying 2.40, it needs a rule that both sides can apply and get the same result. And the result must not depend on which pair repaired first.

Requirement 5 should look familiar. It is the join property from the gossip-protocols lab. A combining rule that gives the same answer no matter what order you apply it in. Here it arrives as a hard prerequisite rather than a nicety. Section 10 measures what happens when you break it.

6. What is the one idea that makes this affordable?

In one breath: Do not compare the lists. Compare one number that stands for each whole list. If the two numbers match you are finished. If they do not, cut each list in half and ask the same question about each half.

Two sentences hold the whole invention. Here is the first, which is about how.

Compare summaries instead of data, one summary for the whole range. If the summaries match, you are done, and you have sent one number. If they do not, split the range in half and ask the same question about each half. Keep splitting until you have cornered the differences.

And here is the second, which is about when rather than how.

Run this on a schedule, in the background, between every pair of shops, independently of what anyone is reading.

That is it. That is the whole thing.

The first sentence buys requirement 4, cost proportional to the differences. Matching halves are eliminated wholesale, without anybody looking inside them. The second sentence buys requirements 1, 2 and 3.

7. What are all of these things called, now that you have met them?

In one breath: Every word below names something you have already watched happen. The name comes after the experience, not before it, so each one is tied to the section where you met it.

Why "entropy", and not "repair" or "sync"?

Entropy is the tendency of things left alone to become disordered. Anti-entropy is the work you do to push them back.

In a replicated store, entropy is the accumulating difference between copies that should be identical. Anti-entropy is the family of background mechanisms that detect and remove that difference.

The word was chosen deliberately. It comes from Demers and colleagues at Xerox PARC in 1987, the same paper that gave us gossip. The point they were making is that divergence is not an event that happens and is then over. It is a continuous pressure, like heat leaking out of a house.

There is no state called "repaired" that you arrive at and stay in. There is only a pump you keep running. That is why the mechanism is a scheduled sweep and not an error handler. In the week-long run, entropy reached 16 divergent rows out of 64. One anti-entropy pass took it to 0. Next week it will be non-zero again.

"Eventual" in eventual-consistency is exactly "as soon as the anti-entropy pass gets to it".

The disagreement itself has a name too, and it splits in two.

What are the three cures called?

Read repair is what Asha invented in section 3. While answering a question, fix what you notice. Precisely: during a read, the shop handling the call compares the answers it got back, and if they differ it writes the winning version back to the ones that were behind. Before answering, which is blocking, or after, which is asynchronous.

The word "read" in the name is load-bearing. It distinguishes repair that is triggered by and scoped to a read from repair that runs on its own schedule. That distinction is exactly the three limits of section 4.

Hinted handoff is what Asha does when the phone is engaged. She writes the change on a sticky note addressed to Omar, and reads it out when he rings back.

"Hint" rather than "queued write" is deliberate and load-bearing too. It is advisory. It may be dropped. It is not part of the write's durability guarantee. Systems that treat it as a guarantee have a bug, and section 10 measures two distinct ways it silently fails.

The note has a shelf life, called the hint window. Past it, the note is binned unread, on the grounds that a shop gone that long has too much to catch up on to do it one change at a time. Cassandra's default is 3 hours, set by max_hint_window_in_ms, and the simulator uses the same figure. Getting the window wrong does not lose data. It just shifts work from the cheap mechanism to the expensive one.

What is the machinery called?

Full sync is the honest first answer: ship every row and let both sides merge. Correct, and proportional to the data rather than to the differences.

A Merkle tree is the pyramid of checksums from section 6. Each leaf is a hash of a contiguous slice of the price list. Each node above is a hash of its two children. Two shops compare root hashes. Equal roots mean the whole dataset matches. Unequal roots mean descend into the children and repeat, following only the branches that differ.

It is named for Ralph Merkle, in 1979. The reason it needs its own name rather than being called "checksums" is the tree part. A flat checksum tells you whether you differ. Only the tree tells you where, and it does so at logarithmic cost. It is a binary search for disagreement.

Dismissing a whole subtree because its two hashes matched is called pruning, and it is the entire saving.

Now the part that costs you. A leaf does not cover one row. It covers a bundle of rows, a leaf range. So a single differing row makes its whole leaf mismatch, and repair transfers every row in that leaf. The wasted transfer is over-streaming.

That word earns its place because in production it is the dominant cost of repair, and it has nothing to do with how much data is actually wrong. It is the reason repair can move a hundred gigabytes to fix a hundred rows.

Finally, the job itself. A repair session is one pairwise comparison-and-reconcile over one key range: build the trees on each side, exchange and compare them, stream the mismatched ranges, apply the merge rule on both sides. In Cassandra you start it with nodetool repair. The tree-building half has its own name, validation compaction, because it reads every row in the range off disk.

What are the words for a deletion?

A tombstone is a headstone. Deleting an item leaves a marker saying "this was deleted, at this time", rather than leaving nothing at all. The marker carries the deletion's timestamp, which makes the deletion a fact that can be replicated instead of an absence.

That distinction closes a hole you cannot otherwise close. If deletion means absence, then repair seeing "Meera has no row, Omar has a row" cannot tell apart two completely different situations. Meera deleted it. Or Omar received a write Meera never got. The two demand opposite responses. The tombstone is what makes them distinguishable.

Headstones are eventually cleared away. gc_grace_seconds is how long a tombstone is kept before compaction discards it. Resurrection, also called a zombie, is a deleted row that comes back because a copy that never saw the tombstone was repaired after the tombstone was purged.

That setting converts a storage-reclamation knob into a hard operational deadline. Every copy must be repaired at least once within gc_grace_seconds, or deleted data returns. That single sentence is the most consequential thing anti-entropy imposes on the people who operate the cluster. Cassandra's default is 10 days, and it is 10 days because it is a repair deadline, not because tombstones are expensive to keep for 10 days.

What is the word for deciding who wins?

When two copies disagree, something has to pick the answer. That something is the merge rule: a function combining two records into one.

For the result to be independent of the order repairs happened in, the merge rule must be a join. Commutative, associative and idempotent. Last-write-wins, often shortened to LWW, means highest timestamp wins. That is a join. "Take whatever arrived most recently" is not.

The reason this gets its own name here is to separate two things people confuse. Anti-entropy is transport. It finds differences and moves rows. The merge rule is semantics. It decides what the answer is. A perfect transport with a broken merge rule converges to nonsense, reliably and quickly.

This is precisely the bug you shipped and caught in gossip-protocols, and precisely what crdts exists to guarantee by construction.

8. What does the stocktake picture get right, and where does it lie?

In one breath: Anti-entropy is a stocktake. Dev and Ben walk the shelves with the stock book, comparing section totals rather than lines, and splitting only the sections that do not add up. Three things about that picture are lies, and each one is load-bearing.

What does the stocktake get right?

Once a month Dev and Ben close the shop early and walk the shelves with the stock book.

They do not read every line of the book against every shelf. That would take all night. They work in sections. Count the total for the bread section. Compare it against the book's total for the bread section.

If the totals match, the whole section is fine, and they move on without looking at a single individual line. If the totals disagree, they split the section and count the halves. Then they keep splitting until they find the line that is wrong.

Everything important about anti-entropy is in that paragraph. It is scheduled. It is comparison-based. It summarises before it inspects. And its cost is driven by how many disagreements there are, rather than by how big the shop is.

What does an engineer see instead?

Two copies, X and Y, each holding a set of records over the same key range. A record is a key pointing at three things: a timestamp, a value, and whether it is deleted. Written compactly, that is (key → {timestamp, value, deleted}).

The prune box is why this is cheap. A matching subtree is dismissed wholesale, at the cost of one comparison, no matter how many rows are underneath it.

Where does the story lie to you?

Three places, and each one is load-bearing.

The first lie: a stocktake has an authority, and replication does not. The stock book is right. The shelf is checked against it.

Between Meera's list and Omar's list there is no book. Neither copy is the master. Neither is more trustworthy. "Repair" does not mean "restore the correct value". It means "make both sides equal by applying an agreed rule".

If that rule picks wrong, repair spreads the wrong value to the healthy shop with exactly the same enthusiasm it would have spread the right one. Section 11 measures this happening.

The second lie: a stocktake happens when the shop is shut, and repair happens while you are trading. Rows are being written throughout. So the two trees are built from two slightly different moments in time, and differences appear that were never faults.

That is engine run 12, and section 17 says what an engine run is.

The third lie: in a stocktake a missing item means it is gone, and in a store a missing row is ambiguous. The shelf being empty and the book having no line for the item look identical. They mean opposite things: "we deleted it" against "you never heard about it".

This is the ambiguity the tombstone exists to remove. It is also the subject of the worst failure in this lab.

10. How would you build it, one failure at a time?

v1 — Why not just leave Omar a note?

v4 — Why doesn't one checksum for the whole list help?

The obvious improvement: hash the entire range on both sides and compare the two numbers. That is 64 bytes on the wire.

This fails in an interesting way. It answers the wrong question.

It tells you whether you differ, with no information about where. So the only thing you can do with a mismatch is fall back to v3 and send everything anyway. And in a busy system the answer is essentially always "yes, you differ". So v4 costs you one extra round trip and then does v3.

That failure generates the real design. You do not need one summary. You need a hierarchy of summaries, so that a mismatch at the top can be localised by descending.

v6 — Why does fixing one row move eight?

In the table above, the 16-leaf tree had one row per leaf. Real trees never do.

A Cassandra Merkle tree has a bounded depth, 15 by default, so 32,768 leaves, covering a token range that may hold millions of rows. A leaf is not a row. It is a bucket of thousands of rows, and the unit of repair is the bucket.

At production scale the arithmetic is ugly. With 32,768 leaves over a range holding 30 million rows, that is roughly 900 rows moved to fix one. And a leaf is contiguous in the key space, so they are 900 large rows if you happen to keep large rows together.

This is over-streaming, and it is the single biggest operational cost of anti-entropy in a real cluster.

The obvious response is to make the tree deeper. That runs into memory: the tree must be held in RAM while it is compared, and it doubles in size per level.

The real fix arrived later and is a different mechanism entirely. ScyllaDB's row-level repair exchanges per-row checksums within a mismatched range, instead of streaming the range. It trades more small messages for far less data. Section 13 comes back to this.

Everything so far is transport: finding differences and moving rows. It says nothing about what the answer should be.

11.4 What happens to the row nobody reads?

Take any of the three failures above, and apply it to a row that gets no read traffic at all.

With hinted handoff and read repair both switched on, the hot rows reach 0 divergence and 8 cold rows stay wrong indefinitely.

Why: both cheap layers are triggered by activity. A write for one, a read for the other. A cold row has neither, so only a scheduled repair will ever touch it.

Every guarantee that is stated per-operation still holds. What you have lost is any belief that "the copies agree" without a repair having run.

This is the reason nodetool repair is on a cron. It is also why the damage surfaces at the worst possible moment: an analytics scan, a restore, a failover, or the day a dormant customer logs back in.

11.5 What can a read from one shop possibly detect?

Set the number of shops asked to one. Read the stale row from the stale shop.

The read answers 2.40 against a true price of 9.00, and repairs nobody.

Why: repair requires comparison, and one response is not a comparison. There is nothing the system can do here.

What you gained is real. This is the fastest read the system offers. What you lost is detection, entirely.

Reading from one copy, which Cassandra calls ONE, is extremely common on latency-sensitive paths, and it is fine. Provided you understand that on those paths all of your convergence comes from layer 3, and none from layer 2.

11.6 How does a deleted item come back on sale?

The most consequential failure in this lab, so it gets the most room.

First version: no headstones. Close Omar's shop. Discontinue eclair, implemented as simply removing the line. Reopen Omar's shop. Repair Meera against Omar.

Repair sees Meera with no row and Omar with a row. It cannot tell "Meera deleted it" from "Omar got a write Meera missed". The only sensible default for "one side has data the other lacks" is to copy the data over.

So eclair is back on sale at 4.60, on every copy, permanently. The deletion is gone, and no error occurred at any point.

Second version: headstones, repaired promptly. Same thing, but a delete now writes a marker carrying its timestamp. Repair compares a tombstone at t=101 against a live row at t=0, applies last-write-wins, and the tombstone wins. eclair stays gone.

This is why tombstones exist. Not for bookkeeping, but because a deletion has to be a fact that can be replicated, and an absence cannot be replicated.

🔮 Predict: Headstones are on. eclair was discontinued while Omar's shop was closed. It stays closed for longer than gc_grace_seconds, and during that time compaction runs on Meera's and Arjun's shops. Then Omar reopens and you repair. What does the customer see?

That is Scenario 8. Here is the mechanism step by step, with real values.

  1. t=1 — Meera and Arjun write a tombstone for eclair with timestamp 1. Omar is closed and knows nothing.
  2. t=1..360 — the tombstone sits on both shops. Any repair in this window would carry it to Omar, and his live row at timestamp 0 would lose to it.
  3. t=480 — compaction runs. The tombstone is older than the 6-hour grace, so it is discarded. Meera and Arjun now have no row at all for eclair, byte for byte the same state as the first version above.
  4. t=481 — Omar reopens, still holding eclair = 4.60 @ t=0.
  5. Repair compares "Meera: nothing" against "Omar: a live row". The tombstone that would have settled it no longer exists. The row is copied back.

Nothing malfunctioned. Compaction did its job. Repair did its job. The merge rule did its job. The failure is entirely in the scheduling. The grace period ran out before the repair ran.

Why this one is special: deletion is the single operation where "I have less data than you" is the correct end state, and the marker proving it is deliberately temporary.

Nothing automatic can save you. The system cannot detect a resurrection, because once the tombstone is purged there is no evidence the row was ever deleted.

All three copies agree perfectly. What has been lost is correctness. This is the sharpest illustration in the lab that convergence and correctness are different properties, and that anti-entropy only delivers the first.

In production this converts a storage setting into an operational deadline.

Every copy must be repaired at least once every gc_grace_seconds, or deleted data comes back.

Cassandra's default is 10 days for exactly this reason. The standard practice is to complete a full repair cycle well inside it, and a week is typical.

Two mistakes are common. The first is lowering gc_grace_seconds to reduce tombstone-scan latency, without shortening the repair cycle to match. The second is letting repair fail silently for a fortnight on a cluster nobody is watching. The second one is more common.

11.7 Why does repair move so much more than it fixes?

One differing row, coarse leaves. Then run the same repair again with the checksum-range setting changed.

Eight rows moved to fix one, an eightfold waste. At production tree sizes, that is hundreds of rows moved per real difference.

Why: the leaf is the unit of transfer, and a leaf covers a range rather than a row. Your options are deeper trees, which are bounded by memory, sub-range repair, or a different mechanism entirely such as row-level repair.

Correctness is untouched. Over-streaming sends redundant correct data. What you lose is network, disk and time.

This is what makes repair the most disruptive routine job in a Cassandra cluster, and why teams throttle streaming and repair one sub-range at a time.

11.8 Why does repair stream data when nothing is wrong?

Run a repair while writes are flowing.

1 row streamed to fix 0 real differences with fine leaves, and 8 rows to fix 0 with coarse ones.

Why: the two trees are snapshots of two different instants. An in-flight write is a difference by construction. You can fix it with snapshot-based repair, validating against a frozen snapshot on both sides, or you can accept the tax.

Nothing is harmed. The extra streaming is harmless data movement. What you lose is the assumption that "repair streamed data" means "something was broken".

On a busy cluster, repair metrics therefore always show non-zero streaming, which trains people to ignore them.

11.9 What happens when the merge rule is not a join?

Switch the merge rule to "whoever received the row wins", and repair in two different orders.

Two of the four orders leave every copy on 4.40. The other two leave every copy on 8.50. All four converge.

Why: the rule is not commutative, so the result depends on repair order, and repair order is scheduling noise. The system cannot tell it has done this, so no response is possible.

Convergence survives. Availability survives. Everything a dashboard measures survives. What is lost is the meaning of the data.

This is the class of bug that crdts exists to make impossible. It is also the exact bug you hit and caught in gossip-protocols. Recognise the shape rather than the instance: if two different orderings of the same facts can give two different answers, you do not have a merge rule, you have a race.

12. What has a finished repair actually promised you?

In one breath: One narrow sentence, resting on four things that must be true. Everything you might hope it promised is on the list of things it does not.

What is the guarantee, exactly?

Suppose a repair session between copies X and Y completes over key range R. Suppose also that the merge rule is a join. Then at the moment that session completes, X and Y hold identical records for every key in R.

Extend that pairwise across all the copies and you have convergence for the whole set. That is the entire safety claim. It is worth noticing how narrow it is.

What must be true underneath it?

Four invariants. The first is the one everything else in the lab descends from.

  1. Comparison requires two. No copy can detect its own staleness. Omar cannot look at his own price list and tell that it is wrong.
  2. A deletion must be representable. Absence is ambiguous. A tombstone makes deletion a positive fact with a timestamp. Any system where "gone" means "no bytes" will resurrect data under repair.
  3. The merge rule must be a join. Commutative, associative, idempotent. Otherwise the final state depends on repair order, and repair order is scheduling noise.
  4. The tombstone must outlive the slowest copy's repair interval. This is the gc_grace deadline. It is the only invariant in the list enforced by an operator rather than by code.

What is explicitly not guaranteed?

🔮 Predict: Dev has just finished a clean repair pass over every range. Two customers read the same row, one after the other. Can the second read see an older value than the first?

Reveal

Yes. Repair makes the copies equal at the moment it completes. It says nothing about what happens after that moment, and it never claimed the copies would stay equal.

If you said no, you have quietly upgraded "the copies match right now" into "every reader sees one consistent story". That upgrade is the subject of linearizability, and anti-entropy does not help with it at all.

Six things repair does not do.

  • No ordering. Anti-entropy makes copies equal. It says nothing about the order writes were applied in, and it never will. Ordering is a different problem with different machinery. See raft and paxos.
  • No linearizability, and no help toward it. A read can still see a value that a later read does not. See linearizability.
  • No recovery of lost updates. Under last-write-wins, the losing concurrent write is gone, and repair propagates the loss rather than repairing it. Recovering both needs version vectors and siblings, or a CRDT. See quorums-and-replication and crdts.
  • No time bound. Repair is a liveness property. It happens eventually, where "eventually" is whatever your cron says. There is no bound on how stale a copy can be between passes, and the system will not tell you it is stale.
  • No protection against a write that reached zero copies. Anti-entropy reconciles what exists. It cannot invent a write nobody stored.
  • No correctness of the converged value. Sections 11.6, 11.9 and 11.10 each produce a cluster in perfect agreement about the wrong thing.

What did it cost?

Read this as a receipt. Every line on the left was paid for by the line on the right.

You bought You paid
Convergence for cold data A full disk scan of every range on every copy, every pass, called validation compaction
Network cost proportional to differences Over-streaming: whole leaf ranges moved for single-row differences
Deletions that survive replication Tombstones on disk, tombstone scans on reads, and a hard repair deadline
Order-independent results The obligation to design the merge rule as a join, which for real business data usually means version vectors or CRDTs rather than last-write-wins
A repair you can schedule A repair that competes for the same disk and network as your traffic

The honest summary of the trade: anti-entropy converts an unbounded, invisible correctness problem into a bounded, visible operational cost.

That is a very good trade. It is not a free one. And the bill arrives as disk input and output and on-call pages, rather than as wrong answers.

13. What does this look like on a real cluster at three in the morning?

In one breath: Dev's bakery becomes a Cassandra cluster, the sixteen items become a token range with millions of rows, and the stocktake becomes a cron job that competes with your traffic and can page you for a data-correctness problem you cannot see.

How does a bakery become a cluster?

Take this lab's model one step at a time.

Replace "16 rows" with a token range owned by a node. Replace "shop A" with a node holding several such ranges. Cassandra defaults to 16 virtual nodes per machine, so a single machine takes part in dozens of independent repair sessions.

Replace "repair Meera against Omar" with nodetool repair, which runs a session per range per replica pair.

The Merkle tree becomes a bounded-depth tree, 15 levels and 32,768 leaves, over a range that may hold tens of millions of rows. That is where over-streaming stops being a curiosity and becomes the dominant cost.

Who does this in the real world, and how?

The three-layer structure is Dynamo's, from the 2007 paper, and it is the shape almost everyone adopted.

Layer Cassandra Riak DynamoDB
1 — during the write Hinted handoff, 3h window (max_hint_window_in_ms) Hinted handoff Internal, not exposed
2 — during the read Blocking read repair on the replicas the read touched (the probabilistic read_repair_chance that also digested the others was removed in 4.0) Read repair Internal
3 — on a schedule nodetool repair — full, incremental, or sub-range; commonly driven by Cassandra Reaper Active anti-entropy (AAE) with persistent hash trees updated as writes land Continuous background anti-entropy

Riak's choice is worth pausing on, because it attacks the cost this lab measured back in section 6: the 32 rows read off the shelf even when nothing was wrong.

Riak keeps its hash trees incrementally on write, and stores them on disk. So a repair does not need a full scan to build them. The trade is complexity, and the risk of the persisted tree itself drifting from the data. Riak ships an expiry that periodically rebuilds the trees from scratch for that reason.

ScyllaDB went at the other cost, over-streaming, with row-level repair. Instead of streaming a whole mismatched range, the two replicas exchange per-row checksums within it, and transfer only genuinely differing rows. That is v6's problem solved by giving up the "one hash per bucket" simplification.

Which systems need none of this?

Kafka partitions and PostgreSQL streaming replicas do not run anti-entropy, and the reason is instructive.

They have a single leader and an ordered log. So a follower's state is defined as "the log up to offset N". A lagging follower is not divergent, it is behind, and catching up means replaying the log from its offset.

Anti-entropy is the price of leaderless writes. Once any copy may accept a write, there is no log that defines the truth, and comparison is the only tool left.

What does the on-call engineer actually see?

Four pages, in rough order of how badly you want to be woken for them.

  • "Repair has not completed on this keyspace for 9 days." Against a 10-day gc_grace_seconds, this is a data-correctness page, not a housekeeping ticket. The clock in section 11.6 is running.
  • "Read 2,000 live rows and 91,000 tombstone cells." That is Cassandra's tombstone warning, and then its abort threshold. It usually means a workload that deletes heavily. The temptation is to cut gc_grace_seconds, and doing that without shortening the repair cycle to match is exactly how you buy section 11.6.
  • Latency spikes on a schedule. Validation compaction reads everything, and streaming writes everything. Repair competing with peak traffic looks like a mysterious recurring p99 cliff.
  • Repair that never finishes. A session stalls on one unreachable replica, and because repair is pairwise, the range stays unrepaired. Reaper exists largely to make this visible and resumable.

What do you tune, and what does each knob do?

The third column is the one to read twice. Every one of these knobs has a trap.

What has this lab deliberately left out?

Six things, each of which is a lab of its own.

  • Token ranges and virtual nodes. Here every shop holds every row. Really, each node holds many ranges with different replica sets. See consistent-hashing and partitioning-and-rebalancing.
  • Multi-datacentre repair, where the expensive question is which pairs you repair across a wide-area network, and how often.
  • Incremental repair state. Real clusters track which data has already been repaired, so passes get cheaper. This lab repairs everything every time.
  • Digest reads and speculative retry, the machinery that decides which copies a read actually contacts. Per section 11.5, that is what determines whether read repair sees anything at all.
  • Real hashing and real streaming costs. The simulator counts hashes and rows, not CPU cycles and bytes on a socket.
  • Sibling values and version vectors. The simulator's merge rule is last-write-wins, so that section 11.10 can be shown cleanly. A production store that cares about concurrent writes keeps both versions instead. That is quorums-and-replication and crdts.

What is this commonly confused with?

Five pairs, each separated by one sentence.

  • Anti-entropy against gossip. Gossip spreads new information to everyone quickly. Anti-entropy finds old information that failed to arrive. Same paper, same authors, opposite direction of travel: gossip is push, anti-entropy is compare.
  • Anti-entropy against quorum reads. A quorum read makes this answer fresh by overlapping sets. Anti-entropy makes the copies equal. R + W > N does not remove a stale copy; it just avoids being fooled by one.
  • Anti-entropy against consensus. Consensus, in raft and paxos, makes replicas agree on an order before anything is applied. Anti-entropy makes them agree on a set after the fact. Consensus prevents divergence; anti-entropy cleans it up.
  • Anti-entropy against replication lag catch-up. A Kafka or PostgreSQL follower catches up by replaying a log from an offset. That is not anti-entropy, because there is a leader and an authoritative order. Anti-entropy is what you need precisely when there is no such log.
  • Repair against bootstrap. Repair compares and fixes differences. Bootstrap streams a whole range from scratch. Past a certain amount of drift, bootstrap is cheaper, as section 11.11 shows.

16. Glossary: what does each word mean, in one line?

In order of first appearance, grouped by what the word is for.

The problem.

Term In one line
Replica One of the N machines holding a copy of a row.
Divergence (drift) Two replicas holding different records for the same key.
Stored divergence The replicas hold different bytes.
Visible divergence The replicas would give a customer different answers.
Entropy The accumulating difference between replicas that should be identical.
Anti-entropy The background work that finds and removes that difference.

The three cures.

Term In one line
Read repair Fixing the replicas a read touched, using the versions that read returned.
Hinted handoff Parking an undeliverable write on the coordinator and replaying it when the target returns.
Hint The parked write. Advisory: it may be dropped without notice.
Hint window The maximum age of a hint before it is discarded unread (Cassandra: 3 hours).
Coordinator The node handling a client request; here, also the node that holds hints.

Comparing without sending.

Term In one line
Full sync Repairing by shipping every row. Correct, and proportional to the data.
Merkle tree A tree of hashes over ranges, letting two replicas binary-search for their differences.
Root hash The top of that tree; equal roots mean the whole range matches.
Leaf range The contiguous slice of key space one leaf hash covers.
Pruning Dismissing a whole subtree because its two hashes matched.
Over-streaming Transferring a whole leaf range because one row in it differed.

Doing the work.

Term In one line
Validation compaction Cassandra's name for the full disk scan that builds a Merkle tree.
Streaming Moving the rows of mismatched ranges between replicas.
Repair session One pairwise comparison-and-reconcile over one key range.
Bootstrap Streaming an entire range to a node from scratch, instead of repairing it.

Deciding the answer.

Term In one line
Merge rule The function deciding which of two conflicting records wins.
Join A merge rule that is commutative, associative and idempotent — so order does not matter.
Last-write-wins (LWW) The merge rule "highest timestamp wins". A join, and frequently the wrong one.

Deletion.

Term In one line
Tombstone A deletion marker with a timestamp, making a delete a replicable fact rather than an absence.
gc_grace_seconds How long a tombstone survives before compaction discards it — in practice, a repair deadline.
Resurrection (zombie) A deleted row that returns because a replica was repaired after its tombstone was purged.

The variants you will meet.

Term In one line
Sub-range repair Repairing one slice of a node's range at a time, to keep sessions small.
Incremental repair Repairing only data not marked as already repaired.
Row-level repair ScyllaDB's alternative: exchange per-row checksums inside a mismatched range instead of streaming it.
Active anti-entropy (AAE) Riak's version, with hash trees kept up to date on write rather than rebuilt per repair.

Anti-Entropy — Solutions

Rungs 7 and 8 have no single right answer; what follows there is a worked discussion and the set of concerns a good answer covers.

Rung 1 — Explain

1.1 Keeping three copies means one machine can die without losing anything. But a change only has to reach two of the three copies before the system calls it done — that is deliberate, because waiting for all three would mean one sick machine could stop the whole service. So the third copy is left holding the old value, and nothing in the ordinary business of writing and reading is responsible for going back to fix it. The background job exists because otherwise nobody ever does.

1.2 R+W>N guarantees that a read touches at least one replica holding the latest write; it says nothing about the replicas themselves. Better: "Quorum reads mean a read is not fooled by a stale copy. The stale copy is still there, and only repair removes it."

1.3 Because "repair" and "sync" sound like things you finish. Anti-entropy names a continuous pressure — replicas drift apart as an ordinary consequence of the system working — so the mechanism is a pump you keep running, not an error handler you invoke when something breaks. There is no state called "repaired" that you arrive at and stay in.

1.4 Instead of comparing every line of the price list, you compare one total for the whole list. If the totals agree, the lists are identical and you are finished after one comparison. If they disagree, you split the list in half, compare the two halves the same way, and keep splitting — so you only ever look inside the parts that actually disagree.

Rung 2 — Identify

2.1 The verdict number counts items where a customer would be told different things depending on which shop serves them. The other figure counts items where the shops hold different records. They differ when the difference is invisible to a customer — most importantly when one shop holds a deletion marker and another holds no row at all. Both mean "not sold", so visible divergence is 0 — and that is exactly the state from which repair resurrects deleted data.

2.2 croissant, highlighted in the drift colour on all three shop cards (the highlight marks the item, not the wrong shop — the shops disagree, and no copy is authoritative). Clicking it shows the timestamp on each shop's record, which is the thing the merge rule actually compares and which the card does not display.

2.3 With a 16-range tree and one differing item: 4 green (pruned) boxes, 4 red (descended or streamed) boxes on the path down, and 22 faded. A faded box is a range whose parent's hashes matched, so neither shop read a single row inside it or sent anything about it. That pruning is the entire saving.

2.4 Rows read off disk — always 32, whether one row differs or none do. It matters because it is the cost that does not respond to your data being healthy: a perfectly in-sync cluster still pays a full scan of every range on every replica for every repair pass. In Cassandra this is validation compaction, and it is why repair is scheduled for quiet hours.

Rung 3 — Predict

3.1 +1 hour → 0 divergent (hints replayed). +4 hours → 3 divergent. The hints were discarded at the 3-hour mark. The difference is not that more went wrong in the longer outage; it is that the cheap layer stopped covering it, and nothing reported that.

3.2 3 divergent. Hinted handoff failed — not through timeout, but because hints are held on the single shop that took the write, and that shop restarted. Note the elapsed time was never the problem: no outage in this run exceeded ten minutes.

3.3 2 ranges: 8 rows sent, 6 hashes. 16 ranges: 1 row sent, 18 hashes. Deeper trees cost more comparisons and dramatically less transfer — and since a hash is 32 bytes and a row is 200, that is a good trade until the tree stops fitting in memory.

3.4 Divergence stays at 1 forever. At R=1 the read has one response, and one response is not a comparison. Read repair does not need "a read" — it needs two differing answers to the same question in the same request. This is why a latency-tuned path reading at ONE gets no convergence from layer 2 at all.

3.5 Hashes 2, rows sent 0, rows actually different 0, bytes 64 — and rows read off disk: 32. That is the full-scan cost, called validation compaction, and it is the part of repair that a healthy cluster cannot avoid paying.

Rung 4 — Modify

4.1 Hint window 0. Every hint is discarded immediately, so layer 1 is off. Nothing becomes wrong — no bad value appears — but drift that used to be cleaned up in seconds now waits for a scheduled repair. Liveness lost, safety intact.

4.2 Headstones off. Repair sees "one side has a row, the other has nothing" and copies the row. The deleted item is back on sale on every replica, permanently, with no error. Safety lost — the system produces a wrong answer, not a late one.

4.3 Merge rule = whoever received it wins. Both orders converge, quickly and stably, to different values. Note what did not happen: no oscillation, no error, no failure to converge. Safety lost, and lost in the hardest way to detect, because every health check reports a perfectly consistent cluster.

4.4 C's clock 120 minutes fast. The shelves show C's 3.00, and the genuinely later 3.50 is gone. The unusual part is when: the wrong outcome was decided at write time, by each replica independently, on arrival — before any repair, any read, or any conflict. Repair then propagates it authoritatively. Safety lost, and the loss is in the write path, not the repair path.

4.5 W=1, R=1. Hinted handoff still works (the coordinator still notices unreachable replicas). Read repair is dead — R=1 gives it nothing to compare. Scheduled Merkle repair still works and is now doing essentially all of the convergence work; it is also now the only thing standing between you and permanent divergence, so its schedule has become a correctness parameter.

Rung 5 — Trace

5.1 tart is item 13 of 16, so it falls in leaf index 12 of a 16-leaf tree.

level 0  root                      A: differs from C          → descend   (2 hashes)
level 1  items 1–8                 equal                      → PRUNE (8 items never read)
         items 9–16                differ                     → descend   (+4 hashes)
level 2  items 9–12                equal                      → PRUNE (4 items never read)
         items 13–16               differ                     → descend   (+4 hashes)
level 3  items 13–14 (tart,eclair) differ                     → descend   (+4 hashes)
         items 15–16               equal                      → PRUNE (2 items never read)
level 4  tart                      differ                     → STREAM    (+4 hashes)
         eclair                    equal                      → PRUNE (1 item never read)

Total 18 hashes, 1 row streamed in each direction, 15 of 16 items never looked at. Merge: A's 8.50 @ ts 40 beats C's 4.40 @ ts 0, so both become 8.50.

Divergence before: 1. After: 0 — because B already held 8.50. It is not zero in general because a repair is pairwise: had B been the odd one out, repairing A ↔ C would have changed nothing at all. Convergence for the whole replica set needs every pair covered, which is why real repair runs a session per pair per range.

5.2 Root differs (2 hashes); descend (4 hashes) → total 6 hashes. The second leaf covers items 9–16, so 8 rows are transferred each way, of which 7 were already identical on both sides. That is the over-streaming: a 7-row tax on a 1-row problem, paid because the leaf, not the row, is the unit of transfer.

5.3 The zombie trace:

Time A B C
t=11 headstone @ ts 11 headstone @ ts 11 eclair = 4.60 @ ts 0 (closed, knows nothing)
t=500 no row at all no row at all eclair = 4.60 @ ts 0
t=501 no row at all no row at all eclair = 4.60 @ ts 0 (reopens, serving)
t=502 eclair = 4.60 no row eclair = 4.60

The outcome became inevitable at t=500, when the headstones were cleared — not at t=502 when repair ran. Up to t=499 a repair would have carried a live tombstone to C and settled it correctly. After the clear, the evidence that the item was ever deleted does not exist anywhere in the system, and no mechanism can recover it. What had to happen before t=500 is simply: a repair involving C. That is the whole of the gc_grace_seconds rule.

Notice also that B ends up disagreeing with A and C — one pairwise repair does not converge the set, per 5.1.

5.4 Deletes performed on days 1–2, whose tombstones were purged around day 11, may now exist as live rows on any replica that missed the original delete. The damage is bounded by: deletes issued in the window × replicas that missed them.

The first thing to check is whether that keyspace does deletes at all — if it is append-only, there is no exposure and you can simply repair. If it does, the important thing is what not to do: do not just run repair. Running repair is the step that performs the resurrection. The safer sequence is to raise gc_grace_seconds first (so that any tombstones still alive are not purged mid-repair), establish which replicas were behind, and prefer rebuilding a badly-behind replica over repairing it. Then repair, then lower the grace back once the cycle is healthy again.

Rung 6 — Spot the flaw

6.1 A single checksum answers whether you differ, never where, so a mismatch leaves you no option but to stream everything. On a cluster taking continuous writes, two replicas essentially always differ by at least one in-flight row, so the answer is always "differ" and the design degenerates into a full sync plus an extra round trip and a full scan. It is strictly worse than just doing the full sync. The lesson: the tree is not there to detect differences — one hash detects them — it is there to localise them.

6.2 One-directional repair. A holds tart = 8.50 @ ts 40; B and C hold 4.40 @ ts 0. Rule: higher ID sends to lower.

  • C → B: identical, nothing changes.
  • C → A: A merges 4.40 into 8.50 and keeps 8.50.
  • B → A: same.

Final: A = 8.50, B = 4.40, C = 4.40, and no further repair will ever change that. Data flows only downhill, so a value that exists only on the highest-ID node can never leave it. A read at R=2 landing on B and C returns 4.40 — the newer write is invisible — and if A is later replaced, it is gone. Convergence is broken, permanently, by a design that looked like it merely halved the traffic.

6.3 "Deleted if missing on a majority" replaces what happened most recently with how many copies exist, and those are different questions.

  • It deletes live data. Write a new row at W=1; it lands on A only. It is missing on B and C — a majority — so repair deletes it. A successfully acknowledged write is destroyed.
  • It resurrects deleted data. A row exists on all three. A delete is issued while B and C are unreachable and is accepted at W=1, so only A removes it. The row is now present on B and C — a majority — so repair copies it back to A.

Both failures come from the same root: absence carries no timestamp, so counting absences cannot order events. A tombstone is exactly the fix, because it gives the deletion a timestamp and makes it comparable.

6.4 Keeping hints forever. Storage: hints accumulate unboundedly on the coordinators during a long outage — and the moment the replica returns you replay a gigantic backlog write-by-write into a machine that has just started, which is a reliable way to knock it over again. This is precisely why the window exists. Correctness: it still does not achieve "no write is ever lost", because hints die with the coordinator holding them, and because hints only cover writes the coordinator accepted and could not deliver — they do nothing about a replica that lost data locally (disk corruption, a restore from an old snapshot, an operator error). Hinted handoff is an optimisation. Making it more thorough does not make it a guarantee.

6.5 The 4% is measuring how often no write landed between the two tree builds — that is, it is a measure of your write traffic and the gap between the two scans, not of your data quality. On a busy cluster it will approach 0% while the data is perfectly healthy. Tracking it as data quality means the dashboard gets worse exactly when the business gets busier, which is how a metric trains a team to ignore it.

Rung 7 — Handle failure

7.1 Eleven days down, ten-day grace. The concerns a good runbook covers:

  1. Do not repair it, and do not let it serve reads. Repairing is the operation that resurrects deleted data, because tombstones for anything deleted in the last eleven days have been purged on the healthy replicas. Letting it join and serve is how stale values reach customers.
  2. Replace rather than repair. Wipe it and bootstrap it — stream the ranges from scratch. This is deliberately choosing full sync over Merkle, and it is also the cheaper choice: at that much divergence the tree comparison prunes nothing and costs more than sending everything (measured in the lab: 8,384 bytes against 6,400).
  3. If you genuinely cannot rebuild it, raise gc_grace_seconds across the cluster first so no further tombstones are purged during the operation, then repair, then accept that anything deleted in the missed window may need reconciling at the application level. Restore the grace afterwards.
  4. Afterwards, fix the thing that let a replica be down for eleven days without a page. That is the actual defect.

7.2 400 GB nightly to fix a few thousand rows. The levers, and their costs:

Lever What it costs
Deeper Merkle trees (smaller leaves) RAM per repair session, and sessions run concurrently
Sub-range repair More sessions, more coordination, longer wall-clock
Repair more often Less drift per pass, so less to stream — but more scans
Repair less often Fewer scans, more drift per pass, and you approach the gc_grace deadline
Throttle streaming Protects live traffic; risks repair not finishing inside gc_grace
Move to a database with row-level repair (ScyllaDB) A migration
Reduce data per range (more nodes / more vnodes) Hardware, and rebalancing

Also worth checking before tuning anything: whether the 400 GB is over-streaming at all, or whether the replicas genuinely are that far apart because something upstream is dropping writes.

7.3 24-hour compliance deletes vs a 30-hour repair. In plain language for a product manager: "Deleting a row does not erase it — it writes a note saying 'this was deleted'. Every copy of the data has to see that note before we are allowed to throw the note away. Right now it takes us 30 hours to make sure every copy has seen it. If we throw the note away after 24, some copies never learn about the deletion, and the next time the copies are compared the deleted record comes back — and it comes back looking completely legitimate."

Two designs:

  • Make repair fit. Sub-range or incremental repair, more parallelism, more nodes — get the full cycle to, say, 8 hours, then set the grace to 24 with real margin. This keeps the model simple and costs operational work and hardware.
  • Stop relying on tombstone GC for the guarantee. Encrypt each user's data with a per-user key and delete the key on request. The rows may survive for weeks; they are unreadable within milliseconds. This satisfies most regulatory definitions of erasure, decouples compliance from repair scheduling entirely, and costs you a key management system and the ability to do anything with the data after deletion — including debugging.

A third, narrower option: put the compliance-relevant data in its own table with its own much smaller repair scope, so only that slice has to meet the tight deadline.

7.4 Monitoring. The alert that matters most is the one with no symptoms:

  • "Time since last successful repair completion, per keyspace, per replica." Page at 60% of gc_grace_seconds. This is a data-correctness alert, and it fires before anything is visibly wrong — which is the entire point, because after gc_grace the damage is unrecoverable and silent.
  • Repair job failures and stalls (a session blocked on one unreachable replica leaves its range unrepaired while every other range looks healthy).
  • Bytes streamed per repair pass, trended. A sudden jump means something upstream started dropping writes; a slow climb means your cadence is losing to your drift.
  • Hint delivery vs expiry ratio. Rising expiries mean layer 1 has stopped covering your outage profile and layer 3 is quietly taking the load.
  • Tombstone-per-read warnings, which are the early symptom of the pressure that tempts people to shorten gc_grace_seconds — the change that buys you 11.6.

7.5 A four-second clock drift under LWW. Every write from that server for a week carried a stamp four seconds in the future. Consequences: its writes beat genuinely later writes from every other server, and those later writes were discarded on arrival, with no error. Repair does not help — it hurts, because it takes the winning (wrong) value and propagates it authoritatively to every replica, converting a one-replica problem into a cluster-wide fact.

Finding the damage is genuinely hard, because nothing is inconsistent afterwards — it is uniformly wrong. The realistic approach: identify the affected window and server, then look for rows whose current timestamp falls in that window while a later audit-log or event-stream entry exists for the same key. If you have no independent record of writes, you likely cannot recover the lost updates at all — which is the real argument for hybrid-logical-clocks, or for a merge rule that keeps concurrent versions instead of picking one.

Rung 8 — Design

8.1 Product catalogue, 40M items, 3 DCs, RF=5. What survives from the lab: the three-layer structure, unchanged — hinted handoff, read repair, scheduled Merkle repair. What must change:

  • The merge rule. "Prices must never be silently wrong" and last-write-wins do not belong in the same system, for the reason in 7.5. Either use server-side timestamps from a single trusted source per key, or keep concurrent versions and resolve them in the application (the price a human last approved wins over an automated feed). Say which, and why, before anything else — it is the decision the rest hangs on.
  • Repair topology. Repairing all pairs across 80 ms WAN links is the expensive part. Repair frequently within each datacentre, less often across them, and be explicit that cross-DC drift therefore lives longer.
  • Quorum settings. Heavy reads and modest writes suit a high W and low R: LOCAL_QUORUM reads with W across DCs, so writes carry the coordination cost and reads stay fast.
  • Monitoring. Per 7.4, with the time-since-repair alert as the primary. Add a business-level check that does not depend on the storage layer at all: sample prices from each replica directly and compare them against the source of truth. That check catches every failure in this lab, including the ones where the cluster agrees with itself about the wrong number.

8.2 A message store with 30% deletes. The four things interact badly here. Tombstones accumulate; reads scan them and slow down; the temptation is to cut gc_grace_seconds; cutting it tightens the repair deadline; and repair is already expensive because there is a lot of data.

A workable scheme: partition by conversation and time, so deletes cluster into whole partitions that can be dropped rather than tombstoned; use TTLs where the retention policy allows, so rows expire without a delete being issued at all; keep gc_grace_seconds at the default and instead invest in making repair reliably fast (sub-range, frequent, monitored); and accept slower reads on the small number of partitions that genuinely have heavy interleaved deletes. What you give up: some storage efficiency, and the ability to delete an arbitrary message cheaply — deletes become a design-time concern rather than a runtime one.

8.3 Two billion rows, a 9-hour scan. The scan is the thing to remove, so the mechanism has to maintain its tree incrementally as writes land, rather than building it per repair. That is Riak's active anti-entropy: a persistent hash tree, updated on every write, stored alongside the data.

The state you now maintain is a second copy of the truth in summary form — and the hard question is how that state stays honest, because a bug or a crash that leaves the tree disagreeing with the data produces repairs that are confidently wrong in both directions. Riak's answer is an expiry: rebuild every tree from scratch periodically, so a corrupted tree has a bounded lifetime. So you have not removed the scan; you have moved it from every repair to once per expiry period, which is the actual win.

An alternative worth naming: skip trees and repair by recent-writes — every replica keeps a log of what it wrote in the last N hours and exchanges that, falling back to a full comparison only occasionally. That covers the common case (drift caused by recent misses) very cheaply and does nothing about silent disk corruption, which is a different failure that trees do catch.

8.4 Where you would run none. A Kafka partition, or a PostgreSQL streaming replica. One leader, one ordered log; a follower's state is defined entirely as "the log up to offset N". A lagging follower is not divergent, it is behind, and catching up means replaying from its offset — there is nothing to compare because the log already says what the truth is and in what order.

What flips the answer: allowing any replica to accept a write. The moment writes are leaderless (or multi-leader, or accepted during a partition), there is no single log defining the truth, two replicas can hold unrelated histories, and comparison becomes the only tool left. Anti-entropy is the price of leaderless writes — which is also why it shows up in exactly the systems that chose availability over coordination, and in none of the ones that chose the other way.
